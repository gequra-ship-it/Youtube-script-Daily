Can a person with no heartbeat, no breathing, and an ice-cold body still wake up alive? In emergency medicine, doctors live by a famous rule: a hypothermia patient is not dead until they are warm and dead. When the human core temperature falls below twenty-eight degrees Celsius, the heart can quiver and stop. Breathing ceases, the pupils widen, and the body feels ice-cold to the touch. In ordinary circumstances, the brain starts to suffer permanent damage within minutes after the heart stops. But profound cold changes the biology of dying. Extremely low temperatures slow cellular metabolism dramatically, so cold brain cells need far less oxygen to stay alive. One famous case happened in Norway in 1999. A young doctor and skier named Anna Bågenholm fell through the ice of a frozen stream and was trapped beneath it for about eighty minutes. When rescuers reached her, her heart had stopped, and her core temperature was about thirteen point seven degrees Celsius. Doctors warmed her blood outside her body, her heart started again, and she eventually recovered. Cases like hers are rare, and each depends on many fortunate details, such as air to breathe under the ice and a team ready to treat her quickly. But the principle behind it is real. Surgeons even use it on purpose. In some heart and brain operations, they cool a patient's body so that blood flow can be paused safely for a limited time. Cold can kill, but it can also protect the brain in ways that seem miraculous. The key is that the cold must come first. A body that cools slowly while still alive is very different from a body that stops and then cools. Doctors also know that a very cold heart can be hard to restart, so they warm the patient carefully before deciding anything.

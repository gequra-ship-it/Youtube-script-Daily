Right now, your head is older than your feet by a tiny fraction of a second. Time is not running the same everywhere.

Time travel to the future is not science fiction. It is measured, and you are doing it at this moment.

Albert Einstein showed that time does not flow at the same rate for everyone. There are two causes. The first is speed. The faster you move, the slower your clock ticks compared with someone standing still. The second is gravity. Clocks run faster where gravity is weaker.

You can test the second effect on a mountain. A clock at the top of a tall mountain ticks slightly faster than one at sea level, because gravity is weaker higher up. The difference is tiny, but modern atomic clocks can measure it.

Astronauts show the first effect. After months on the International Space Station, they return having aged a fraction of a second less than the rest of us. The cosmonaut Sergei Krikalev spent eight hundred and three days in orbit across his career. He came home about two hundredths of a second into the future.

Engineers use this every day. GPS satellites orbit about twenty thousand kilometers up. Weaker gravity makes their clocks run fast, and their speed makes them run slow. The net result is about thirty-eight microseconds gained per day. Without a correction, GPS positions would drift by kilometers in a single day.

Now imagine a spacecraft traveling at ninety-nine percent of the speed of light. If its crew flew away and came home, they would find that decades had passed on Earth while they aged only a few years. The technology to do this does not exist, but the physics is not in doubt.

Here is the strange part. We can sprint into tomorrow, but yesterday seems locked away. General relativity does allow some paths that loop back in time, called closed timelike curves. But no one has ever found one in nature.

Stephen Hawking suspected nature forbids them. He proposed this in 1992 as the Chronology Protection Conjecture, which remains unproven.

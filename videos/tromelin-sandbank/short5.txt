After fifteen years of watching an empty horizon, a sail finally appeared.

In November of 1776, fifteen years and four months after the Utile was lost, the corvette La Dauphine reached the island. Its commander was a young naval officer, Jacques Marie Boudin de Tromelin.

On November twenty-ninth, a boat finally made it through the surf. On the white sand, the sailors found the survivors. After more than fifteen years, how many people do you think were left? Out of about sixty Malagasy men and women who had stayed behind in 1761, only seven women remained. With them was an eight-month-old baby boy, born on the sandbank. He knew no other world than crashing waves, seabird cries, and the women who kept him alive.

The sailors carried them across the reef to the corvette. In Isle de France, the governor, Maillart, declared them free. The baby boy was given the name Jacques Moyse.

The island was later named Tromelin after the officer who rescued them, and it still bears that name on maps today. But for many modern historians, it is not a monument to the officer. It is a monument to the eight people who turned a barren sandbank into a place where life continued. Their survival was not an accident. It was a daily act of courage, ingenuity, and community.

Still, life was an endless struggle. Sickness and the strain of waiting slowly reduced their numbers. In the years before the rescue, a small group of the castaways built a craft and sailed away, hoping to reach land. They were never heard from again.

By the end, the community that remained was made up mostly of women. They kept the well, gathered food, tended the fire, and raised a child. We can only imagine how they supported one another through grief, sickness, and the weight of endless waiting.

Their story is a monument to what human beings can do.

Today, if you want to look into the eyes of a living relative, you must climb into the misty mountain forests of the Andes, from Venezuela to Bolivia. There lives the spectacled bear, Tremarctos ornatus, named for the pale rings of fur around its eyes. It is the only surviving member of the short-faced bear subfamily. It is far smaller than Arctodus, and eats mostly plants, including fruit, bromeliads, and palm. It survives as a quiet cousin of the Ice Age giants.

With Arctodus gone, the largest living land carnivore is the polar bear, Ursus maritimus. Genetic studies suggest it split from brown bears within the last few hundred thousand years, though estimates vary. An adult male often weighs between three hundred fifty and seven hundred kilograms. Since splitting off, it has evolved a thick layer of fat, dense insulating fur, and a diet built mostly around seals, perfectly suited to the frozen pack ice of the far north.

But the polar bear depends on one narrow condition: sea ice. It hunts mostly seals, waiting at breathing holes and along the ice edge, so the ice is its hunting platform. As the Arctic warms and sea ice shrinks, scientists warn that polar bears are threatened, and some populations may decline sharply. It echoes the old lesson. Specialists are fragile when their world changes.

Yet bears can also surprise us. In parts of the Canadian Arctic, polar bears and brown bears have been documented mating and producing hybrids, sometimes called pizzly or grolar bears. Some scientists think shifting ranges in a warming Arctic may bring the two species into contact more often. It shows that the bear family still has some flexibility.

Size alone never saved a species. Flexibility did.

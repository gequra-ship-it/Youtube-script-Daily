Imagine a mountain of rock roughly ten to fifteen kilometers across, traveling at tens of thousands of kilometers per hour. When it slammed into a shallow sea, the energy released was comparable to billions of atomic bombs detonating all at once. Rock vaporized instantly, and scientists estimate that the blow carved a cavity deeper than the crust is thick. Can you fathom the sheer scale of such a blow?

Earthquakes shook the planet, and towering tsunamis swept across the ocean, leaving traces in rocks far from the crater. But the true horror did not stop with the blast. Vast amounts of vaporized and melted rock were thrown high above the atmosphere.

As these tiny droplets of molten glass rained back down across the globe, friction with the air made them glow. Many scientists think the heat from that rain turned the sky into something like an oven. Scientists have found those glass beads in rocks from that very layer. For creatures on the surface, the heat from above was a second disaster arriving right behind the first.

How could any creature survive when the sky itself felt like a furnace? Anything standing unprotected on the surface faced terrible heat, and many scientists think fires broke out across large parts of the world. Soot found in the clay layer is one of the clues that fires burned.

Try to hold that moment in your mind, and be honest about how little our ordinary experience prepares us for it. Nothing in everyday life moves like a mountain of rock at tens of thousands of kilometers per hour. When something that large and that fast meets the ground, the rock behaves almost like a liquid for a moment. That is why the crater ended up so wide, and why the effects reached so far.

Think about what happened, in order. In the first minutes there was the blast, the shaking, and the waves. In the next hours there was the glass raining down and the heat. And in the years that followed, as we will see, there was the dark. This was not one disaster. It was a chain of them, each one setting up the next.

Imagine a quiet morning 66 million years ago. Giant reptiles have ruled the land for over one hundred and sixty million years, and nothing on Earth seems able to challenge them. Then, in a geological blink, about three quarters of all species vanish. What could possibly end an empire that powerful? For decades, scientists had only a mystery, and the clue was hiding in a thin ribbon of clay a few centimeters thick.

Geologists had long puzzled over a boundary line hidden in ancient rock across the globe. Above a thin layer of clay, the fossils of dinosaurs and many other animals no longer appear. Yet on one side of it, a whole age of life was there, and on the other side it was gone.

In 1980, researchers measuring this clay found something baffling. It contained an astonishingly high concentration of iridium. On our planet's crust, iridium is exceedingly rare, yet it is far more abundant in many space rocks. The clay was first measured in rocks near Gubbio, Italy, and similar layers soon turned up in Denmark and New Zealand. Why would a cosmic element be spread across the entire globe in a layer barely a few centimeters thick?

A team led by physicist Luis Alvarez and his son, the geologist Walter Alvarez, proposed an answer. An enormous asteroid had struck the Earth and scattered its dust around the world. But an extraordinary claim always demands extraordinary proof. If a giant space rock truly struck our planet with enough violence to end an entire geological era, where was the scar?

That was the hard part. Earth heals its wounds. Mountains rise and wear down, oceans open and close, and rain and wind grind rock into sand. A crater from that long ago could have been buried, or stretched, or worn away entirely.

Then geologists working for an oil company noticed something. Beneath the Yucatan Peninsula in Mexico and the Gulf of Mexico next to it, gravity and magnetic measurements showed a huge circular pattern. Later studies confirmed a buried impact crater called Chicxulub, stretching more than one hundred and fifty kilometers wide. Its age matched the iridium layer closely.

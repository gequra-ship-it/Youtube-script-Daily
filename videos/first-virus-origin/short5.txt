When you think of a virus, you probably picture sickness, coughs, and global pandemics. But what if the ocean could not work properly without them?

Step into the sea for a moment. In just one single milliliter of natural seawater, there are tens of millions of viral particles. That is more viruses in a spoonful of water than there are people in many countries.

Every single second of every day, an astronomical number of viral infections take place beneath the ocean waves. But they are not targeting whales, dolphins, or fish. Their primary targets are marine bacteria and microscopic plankton.

When viruses infect these marine microbes, they cause them to burst open. This microscopic explosion releases carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus directly back into the water. This process, known as the viral shunt, acts as a global recycling engine. Nutrients locked inside one microbe become food for the next, feeding the base of the marine food chain.

Marine biologists measure these viruses by counting the particles in water samples. The numbers are so large that they are hard to picture.

Without viruses constantly culling microbes and recycling nutrients, the flow of chemistry through the sea would look very different. Scientists now think of the ocean's viruses as a key part of how the planet recycles carbon and other elements, not as a side effect of disease.

Think of the sea as a giant kitchen. Microbes grow, viruses pop them, and the spilled contents feed the next round of growth. This cycle repeats every day, across every ocean on Earth, mostly unseen by anyone.

It is a strange partnership, because the same viruses that kill microbes also keep the water full of the food that new microbes need.

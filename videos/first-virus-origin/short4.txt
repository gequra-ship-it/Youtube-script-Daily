What if I told you that a piece of you isn't completely human? In fact, roughly eight percent of your DNA comes from ancient viruses.

It sounds like pure science fiction, but it is written directly into your biological code. Eons ago, ancient retroviruses infected our distant ancestors. These viruses have a unique ability: they do not just hijack a cell, they physically stitch their own genetic sequence into the host's chromosomes.

When this infection happened inside reproductive cells, like a sperm or an egg, that viral sequence became locked into the genome and was passed down from generation to generation. Over millions of years, our lineage accumulated a huge number of these viral scars, known as endogenous retroviruses.

Think about how this works. Most viruses infect a body, make it sick, and are gone. But a retrovirus that lands in a sperm or an egg is different. Its code is copied into every cell of the child, and then into the next generation, and the next, for millions of years.

Over time, many of these inserted sequences were copied, shuffled, and silenced. Yet they remain readable to geneticists today, like fossils written in DNA instead of stone.

You carry an entire viral museum inside every single cell of your body. But evolution did not just leave this viral DNA to gather dust. In a stunning twist of natural history, scientists suspect that our mammalian ancestors repurposed an ancient viral gene to build a protein called syncytin. This protein helps form the placenta, allowing nutrients to flow to an unborn baby.

That is a remarkable thought: a gene that once helped a virus invade cells now helps a mother feed her baby. Evolution is a great recycler, and it does not care where a useful tool came from.

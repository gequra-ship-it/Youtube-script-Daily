What makes a blood type golden? Since the discovery of Rh-null blood in 1961, only a small number of people have been identified with it. Commonly cited estimates say fewer than fifty people in the world, and only a handful of them are active donors. The exact count differs between sources and changes over time, so treat these numbers as approximate. Whatever the true figure, it is tiny, and the blood became known as golden blood. But why would a field known for sober language choose such a glittering name? Rh-null blood also comes in every ABO type, so even among the few donors, the right ABO match may be rarer still.

The name reflects both its value and its irony. Because Rh-null red cells carry no Rh antigens, they cannot set off an Rh reaction in anyone. Some patients have extremely rare Rh types and have made antibodies against nearly every Rh antigen. For them, ordinary blood may be unsafe, and Rh-null blood may be the only compatible choice, as long as the ABO type also matches. So Rh-null is best described as a lifeline for people with rare Rh types, not a magic cure for everyone.

Because donors are so few, rare blood is tracked through national and international networks. Red cells can be frozen and stored for years, and when a patient needs a rare unit, frozen blood has been shipped from one country to another. A single donation can reach a patient on another continent.

Yet consider the person who carries it. If an Rh-null person ever receives ordinary Rh-negative blood, their immune system may see those Rh antigens as foreign and build strong antibodies, which makes future transfusions even harder. So the only truly safe blood for them is Rh-null blood, from someone else or from themselves. Counting such people is hard, because a registry only includes those whose blood has been tested by a specialist laboratory.

The most valuable blood in the world can also be the hardest to replace.

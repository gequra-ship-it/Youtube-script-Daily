Why does your body treat the wrong blood like an invading army? The answer starts on the surface of a red blood cell. Imagine each microscopic cell not as a smooth drop of paint, but as a small ship sailing through a crowded waterway. The outside of that ship is decorated with chemical markers called antigens, and scientists have catalogued hundreds of them. They are proteins and sugars that act like identification flags fluttering in the wind. Your immune system is a vigilant coastal patrol, scanning every passing cell to check whether its flags belong to your home fleet. An adult body holds about five liters of blood, and trillions of these tiny ships are in motion inside it right now.

If you have type A blood, your red cells carry A flags, and your plasma naturally contains antibodies against B. If you have type B, the reverse is true. Type AB cells carry both flags, so the immune system attacks neither. And if you are type O, your cells carry neither flag, so your plasma holds antibodies against both A and B. That is why type O red cells are the classic emergency choice when there is no time to test: they carry no A or B flags for a recipient's antibodies to attack. Strangely, you never have to receive foreign blood to own these antibodies. Scientists suspect that similar sugars on bacteria and in our surroundings teach the immune system to make them in early childhood.

This system was discovered in 1901 by the Austrian scientist Karl Landsteiner, and it turned transfusion from a deadly gamble into a reliable treatment. Before that, giving someone another person's blood could kill them. What goes wrong when the wrong blood arrives? Antibodies lock onto the foreign flags like molecular handcuffs and bind the donor cells together. The blood clumps in the smallest vessels, and the immune system summons proteins that tear the donor cells open, spilling hemoglobin into the plasma. Doctors call this an acute hemolytic transfusion reaction, and it can be fatal.

Blood type is not just a label. It is a defense system.

A fragile ocean creature lay undisturbed six kilometers beneath the frozen rocks of the Arctic Circle. When geologists examined the rock cores pulled from that depth, they were looking at crystalline rock, and they expected nothing but mineral grains. Instead, a microscope revealed the fossilized remains of twenty-four distinct species of single-celled marine plankton.

These microscopic fossils had been entombed inside the Baltic Shield for nearly two billion years. The cores came from the Kola Superdeep Borehole, a Soviet project that began on the Kola Peninsula in 1970. In 1979 it passed the previous world depth record of nine thousand five hundred eighty-three meters, set by an oil well in Oklahoma. The fossils turned up on the way down.

Marine plankton are tiny drifting organisms. Today they float in every ocean on Earth, and their ancient relatives drifted through seas that existed long before the first animals. Finding them preserved in rock so far underground was a shock.

Over nearly two billion years, the rock around those fossils had been folded, heated, and squeezed by the moving crust. It should have destroyed anything delicate. Yet there they were, still recognizable under the microscope.

That is the puzzle. How could the remains of tiny single-celled organisms survive that much heat and pressure? Finding ocean plankton six thousand meters below the surface showed that traces of ancient life can survive far more punishment than most scientists had expected.

It also showed something practical. The deep crust holds records of early life that no one could have reached any other way. You cannot see them from the surface, and no mountain exposes them. Only a drill can bring them up.

The Kola team went on to reach twelve thousand two hundred sixty-two meters, but those microscopic fossils came from only about halfway down. If tiny fossils can endure this much, what traces of life might be waiting inside the crust of other worlds?

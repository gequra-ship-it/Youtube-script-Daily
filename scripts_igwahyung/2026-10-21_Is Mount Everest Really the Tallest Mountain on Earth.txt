- 에콰도르의 침보라소 산(해발 6,263m)은 지구 중심에서 정상까지의 거리가 약 6,384km로, 에베레스트보다 지구 중심 기준 2,000m 이상 더 멀다.

- 소행성 베스타의 레아실비아 충돌구 중앙봉은 분지 바닥 대비 약 20~25km 솟아 있다.
- 지구 지름은 12,700km 이상이며, 지표면 최고점과 최저점의 차이는 20km 미만으로 지구 전체 크기의 1% 미만에 불과하다.

=== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 ===

Take a quiet look at the objects resting on your desk right now. A warm ceramic mug, a notebook with ruled lines, perhaps a ruler tucked neatly into a pen holder. Every tool we use to make sense of our daily routine relies on a standard unit of measurement. We measure time in minutes, coffee in ounces, and distance in meters. From our very first geography lessons, we were taught one fact with absolute certainty: Mount Everest is the highest mountain on Earth. It stands as an undisputed symbol of human ambition and natural majesty. But what if that familiar answer depends entirely on where you place your ruler?

Imagine for a moment that the ocean waters were completely drained away. What if we measured a mountain not from the surface of the sea, but from the true physical base where its stone roots touch the crust? Or what if we measured height from the center of our planet toward the open cosmos? Suddenly, Mount Everest is no longer alone at the top. Other silent giants step forward from the deep ocean floor and the equatorial bulge of our spinning globe. When you change the reference point, the geography of our planet transforms completely. Welcome to Miya's Lab, where today we explore what truly makes a mountain the tallest in the world.

Let us begin our journey high up in the frozen realm of the Himalayas. Picture a landscape of sharp granite, blinding white glaciers, and winds that roar like jet engines. Mount Everest straddles the border between Nepal and China, piercing into the sky at an officially recognized height of 8,848 meters above sea level. At this extreme altitude, the atmosphere is dangerously thin. The air holds barely one-third of the oxygen found at the coast. Climbers enter what high-altitude doctors call the death zone, where human cells slowly starve of life-giving oxygen. It is a harsh, beautiful, and unforgiving world.

Yet the stone beneath a climber's boots holds an incredible secret. If you inspect the yellow band of limestone near the very summit of Everest, you will find marine fossils. You can touch the ancient remains of sea creatures that lived in a shallow, sunlit ocean hundreds of millions of years ago. How did ocean floor sediment end up on the rooftop of our world? The answer lies in the slow, relentless dance of plate tectonics. Tens of millions of years ago, the landmass of India drifted northward across an ancient sea and collided violently with the Eurasian continent. 

That immense collision is still taking place right beneath our feet. As the Indian plate pushes under the Eurasian plate, the crust crumples and folds, shoving ancient seabeds into the sky. In fact, geologists estimate that the Himalayan range continues to rise by a few millimeters each year, counterbalanced only by the destructive forces of wind, ice, and gravity. In terms of altitude above sea level, Everest remains the undisputed king of dry land. No other summit on Earth allows a human being to stand higher relative to the ocean surface. But here is the critical question: why did we decide that sea level should be the ultimate judge of height?

Sea level feels natural to us because human civilization developed along the coastlines of the world. We build harbors, sail across oceans, and watch the tide come and go. When nineteenth-century surveyors mapped the great mountain ranges, using the ocean surface as a universal baseline seemed perfectly practical. It provided a shared starting line for every country on the planet. Whether you were measuring the white cliffs of Dover, the peaks of the Andes, or the crest of the Himalayas, sea level was the zero mark. It was simple, intuitive, and remarkably convenient for human cartographers.

However, the sea is not a flat sheet of glass. The surface of our oceans is constantly warped by winds, ocean currents, and variations in water temperature. More importantly, gravity itself is not uniform across the planet. Dense rock formations deep within the Earth pull water toward them, while less dense areas let the water dip downward. Geophysicists have to rely on an imaginary mathematical construct called the geoid, which calculates an idealized global sea level based on gravitational potential. When we say Everest is 8,848 meters high, we are measuring against an abstract gravitational curve, not a physical slab of rock.

What happens if we strip away that liquid surface and look at a mountain the way an engineer would look at a skyscraper? When an architect measures the height of a building, they do not start from the street curb across town; they measure from the foundation up to the spire. If we apply that same physical logic to the mountains of our world, an entirely different contender takes the crown. We must leave behind the freezing snows of the Himalayas and travel across thousands of kilometers of open water to the tranquil islands of Hawaii.

Rising silently from the floor of the North Pacific Ocean is the massive volcanic giant known as Mauna Kea. To a traveler standing on a sandy Hawaiian beach, Mauna Kea looks like a gentle, broad-shouldered mountain rising peacefully into the tropical clouds. Its summit reaches 4,207 meters above sea level, which is certainly impressive, but seemingly modest compared to Everest. It even hosts astronomical observatories near its peak because the atmosphere above it is remarkably dry and clear. But that visible summit is only the tip of an unimaginably massive volcanic structure.

The true base of Mauna Kea does not sit on dry land. It rests on the deep oceanic crust, anchored nearly six thousand meters below the surface of the Pacific Ocean. If you could drain the ocean basin, you would see a monumental volcanic tower rising from the abyss. When measured from its actual underwater base to its snow-dusted peak, Mauna Kea spans roughly 10,210 meters in total vertical height. That is well over a thousand meters taller than Mount Everest. By physical stature, it is the largest continuous mountain base-to-peak elevation on Earth.

How did such a colossal giant form without the violent collision of continental plates? While Everest was born from a head-on crash between two continents, Mauna Kea was born from a deep mantle plume, often called a volcanic hotspot. Deep within the Earth, an intense column of thermal heat melts rock and punches through the oceanic crust like a welder's torch. Over millions of years, fluid basaltic lava erupted onto the ocean floor, cooling in contact with the frigid water. Layer after layer of lava stacked up over the millennia, slowly building an enormous shield volcano.

Shield volcanoes do not have the steep, jagged cliffs of the Himalayas. Because basaltic lava is relatively low in silica, it flows easily and spreads across vast distances before it hardens. This creates a mountain that resembles a warrior's broad shield laid flat on the ground. Mauna Kea is so massive that its colossal weight actually bends and depresses the oceanic crust beneath it by several kilometers. It acts like an enormous bowling ball placed on a firm mattress. If you count this depressed crustal root, the mountain is even taller than standard measurements suggest.

Think of Mauna Kea as an iceberg of stone. We sail past it and admire the gentle crest catching the morning sun, completely unaware of the mountain's true majesty hidden beneath the waves. If you were a deep-sea creature crawling along the Pacific floor, looking up at Mauna Kea, you would be gazing at a stone wall more than ten kilometers high. From this physical perspective, Everest is simply a tall spire resting on an already elevated continental plateau, while Mauna Kea is the true physical titan built from the ocean floor.

Now, let us turn our attention to yet another fascinating way to measure height. What if the most meaningful measurement is not how far a peak rises above the water, or how tall it stands from its base, but how far its summit reaches into the cosmos? Which point on our planet brings you physically closest to the Moon, the planets, and the stars? If you assume the answer is Everest, prepare for another stunning surprise. For this perspective, we must travel to the rugged highlands of South America, along the volcanic spine of Ecuador.

Here stands Mount Chimborazo, an ancient, ice-capped stratovolcano located in the Western Cordillera of the Andes. When you check its official altitude above sea level, Chimborazo seems to be entirely out of the running. Its summit rises to 6,263 meters above the ocean, which places it far down the list of global summits. It is not even the highest peak in the Andes; that title belongs to Mount Aconcagua in Argentina. Yet, despite its seemingly modest altitude, the summit of Chimborazo holds a unique planetary distinction. It is the farthest point on the Earth's surface from the center of our planet.

How can a mountain that is over two thousand meters shorter than Everest reach farther into space? The secret lies in the peculiar, imperfect shape of the Earth itself. In school textbooks, our planet is drawn as a smooth, perfect blue marble. But in reality, the Earth is an oblate spheroid, slightly flattened at the poles and bulging outwards at the middle. For billions of years, our planet has been spinning around its rotational axis at roughly 1,670 kilometers per hour at the equator. That continuous rotational motion generates centrifugal force, pushing the planet's equatorial regions outward.

As a result of this planetary spin, the Earth's radius at the equator is approximately twenty-one kilometers thicker than its radius at the geographic poles. The waistline of our planet bulges substantially into the surrounding void. This means that any location sitting near the equator is automatically granted a twenty-one-kilometer head start toward the stars compared to a location near the North or South Pole. Geography is not just about vertical elevation; it is also about planetary latitude.

Mount Chimborazo sits just one degree south of the equator, nestled right on top of this massive planetary bulge. Mount Everest, on the other hand, sits much farther north, at roughly twenty-eight degrees latitude, well away from the maximum bulge. When geophysicists measure the distance from the center of the Earth to the summit of Chimborazo, the result is approximately 6,384 kilometers. When they perform the exact same measurement for Mount Everest, Everest comes up short by more than two thousand meters. 

Think about that astonishing reality for a moment. If you stand on the icy, wind-whipped peak of Chimborazo, your feet are resting on the spot that is physically closest to the celestial sphere. You are farther from the molten heart of our planet than any other walking human being. When you look up into the clear equatorial night sky, you are standing on the true physical rooftop of Earth's geometry. In a cosmic race toward the stars, this quiet South American volcano defeats Mount Everest by a decisive margin.

Isn't it remarkable how a single question changes depending on your frame of reference? If you define height by sea level, Everest is the champion. If you measure true base-to-peak topographic rise, Mauna Kea stands supreme. And if you measure radial distance from the center of the Earth, Chimborazo reigns victorious. Nature does not provide a single, universal scoreboard; human beings design the scoreboard, and the results depend entirely on the rules we choose to invent.

This realization forces us to think about the opposite side of the spectrum. What about the world hidden beneath the waves, where the planet drops into darkness? To truly understand the vertical scale of our world, we must examine Earth's deepest depressions. If mountains are the monuments of tectonic collision, ocean trenches are the chasms of planetary recycling. In the western Pacific Ocean, near the Mariana Islands, the seabed plunges into the Mariana Trench. At its lowest known point, the Challenger Deep, the ocean floor reaches nearly eleven thousand meters below the surface.

To grasp that incredible depth, imagine an inverted mountain of stone. If you were somehow able to carve Mount Everest from its continental roots and drop it into the Challenger Deep, an extraordinary thing would happen. The entire mass of Everest would slip beneath the dark waters. Its jagged summit, which pierces the sky on our continents, would still be submerged under more than two kilometers of crushing ocean water. The deepest abyss on our planet is significantly deeper than the highest mountain is tall. 

Why is our planet asymmetrical in this way? Why can ocean trenches plunge eleven kilometers down, while our tallest dry mountains struggle to exceed nine kilometers? The answer comes down to the fundamental physics of rock strength, planetary gravity, and heat. A mountain is not just an inert pile of gravel; it is an immense physical load pressing down upon the Earth's ductile mantle. As a mountain grows taller, the pressure at its roots increases exponentially. If a mountain becomes too massive, the immense weight causes the rock deep beneath it to deform and flow like soft plastic.

Furthermore, Earth's gravity constantly pulls that mass downward, while our dynamic climate unleashes torrential rains, grinding glaciers, and relentless freeze-thaw cycles that erode the peaks. On a planet like Earth, with high gravity and active atmospheric weathering, geologists suspect there is a natural ceiling to how tall a mountain can physically become. Scientists estimate that under Earth's present gravity and rock composition, a mountain cannot easily exceed about ten to twelve kilometers above sea level before its base begins to collapse under its own weight.

This raises a thrilling question: what happens when we leave Earth entirely? If our planet's mountains are limited by gravity and tectonic activity, what happens on a world where those rules no longer apply? To witness the ultimate mountains of the Solar System, we must turn our telescopes toward the red deserts of Mars. Mars is a smaller world than Earth, possessing only about one-third of our planet's surface gravity. It also lacks active plate tectonics, meaning its outer crust remains fixed in place over volcanic hotspots for hundreds of millions of years.

Because the Martian crust does not drift, volcanic plumes can erupt through the exact same spot on the surface for unimaginable stretches of time. Without moving plates to carry the volcanic pile away, the lava simply stacks higher and higher over geological eras. And because Martian gravity is so much weaker, the crust can support vastly heavier structures before the rock begins to buckle. The result of these unique planetary conditions is the colossal volcanic monster known as Olympus Mons.

Olympus Mons is a planetary wonder that defies easy comprehension. It towers approximately twenty-one to twenty-two kilometers above the surrounding Martian plains. That is more than two and a half times the height of Mount Everest above sea level, and more than twice the total base-to-peak height of Mauna Kea. If you placed Olympus Mons on the surface of North America, its circular base would cover an entire region comparable in size to the state of Arizona or the country of France. Its outer caldera alone spans dozens of kilometers across.

What would it look like if you were an astronaut standing on the gentle slopes of Olympus Mons? You might expect a terrifying, jagged cliff resembling the Himalayan peaks, but the reality is wonderfully strange. Because Olympus Mons is an enormous shield volcano, its average slope is astonishingly gentle, only a few degrees. The mountain is so gigantic that its base extends beyond the horizon of the curved Martian landscape. If you stood on its outer flanks, you would not even realize you were climbing a colossal mountain; the slope would simply tilt upward into an orange sky, disappearing over the curvature of the planet.

Even beyond Mars, our Solar System holds other bizarre geological spires. On the giant asteroid Vesta, there is an impact crater named Rheasilvia. Rising from the center of this crater is a central rebound peak that rivals Olympus Mons in sheer elevation, soaring roughly twenty to twenty-five kilometers above the crater floor. On a tiny celestial body where gravity is a tiny fraction of Earth's, stone structures can climb to heights that would be completely impossible on our dense, heavy home planet. The cosmos is filled with monuments that humble our earthly notions of scale.

Now, let us return from the cold deserts of Mars and the vacuum of the asteroid belt back to our home world. When we look at Earth from the silence of space, something truly magical happens to our perception. From an orbit hundreds of kilometers above the atmosphere, the towering peak of Mount Everest, the deep-rooted mass of Mauna Kea, and the equatorial bulge of Chimborazo all seem to disappear. Our planet looks like a smooth, glistening blue jewel suspended in an ocean of darkness.

In fact, if you shrank the Earth down to the size of a standard billiard ball, our planet would actually feel remarkably smooth. The difference between the highest peak on land and the deepest trench in the ocean is less than twenty kilometers. On a planet with a diameter of more than twelve thousand seven hundred kilometers, that entire vertical variation represents a fraction of a single percent. The jagged peaks that have tested human courage and claimed hundreds of brave lives are nothing more than microscopic textures on the skin of our world.

This brings us back to the question we asked at the very beginning. Which mountain is truly the highest? If your definition honors the historic human struggle against thin air and bitter cold, the answer will always be Mount Everest. It remains the ultimate benchmark of human endurance on dry land. If your definition respects the pure architectural scale of geology, rising from the foundation of the ocean floor, then Mauna Kea is the towering monarch of our planet. And if your metric celebrates our relationship with the wider universe, looking outward toward the cosmos, Mount Chimborazo stands proudly as our planet's closest touchpoint to the stars.

None of these answers is wrong. Each represents a different perspective, a different ruler, and a different way of looking at our home. That is perhaps the most profound lesson that science has to teach us. The universe does not exist in rigid, pre-labeled categories. The world is a continuous, fluid reality, and the questions we ask determine the answers we receive. When we change the baseline, we do not change the mountain; we change our understanding of ourselves and our place within the landscape.

Think about how this applies to your own life as you go about your day. How often do we judge our progress, our happiness, or our success by a single, arbitrary ruler? We compare ourselves against the expectations of society, much like measuring everything strictly against sea level. We forget that if we measure from our unique foundations, or look at how close we are reaching toward our personal aspirations, the entire picture shifts. Just like Earth's mountains, your worth and your journey look completely different depending on the baseline you choose.

=== 3부: 숏츠 대본 3편 ===

[숏츠 1] 제목: Why Everest Isn't Actually the Tallest Mountain

Did you know that Mount Everest is not the tallest mountain on Earth? We have all been taught that Everest holds the crown at 8,848 meters above sea level. But that record depends entirely on a hidden trick: measuring from the ocean's surface. What happens if you measure a mountain the way you would measure a skyscraper, from its actual physical foundation up to its peak? If you drain the Pacific Ocean, a completely different titan reveals itself. That mountain is Mauna Kea in Hawaii. Above the water, Mauna Kea looks like a gentle volcanic peak rising about 4,207 meters into the clouds. But its true base rests on the abyssal ocean floor, nearly six thousand meters beneath the waves. From its underwater base to its snow-dusted volcanic summit, Mauna Kea measures over 10,210 meters in total vertical height. That makes it more than a thousand meters taller than Mount Everest from base to peak. Everest is essentially a high spire sitting on an already elevated continental plateau, while Mauna Kea is a single, colossal shield volcano anchored directly into the oceanic crust. Its immense weight even bends the crust of our planet downward. So the next time someone asks you to name the tallest mountain on Earth, ask them a simple question: are you measuring from the sea, or are you measuring from the ground? [숏츠 2] 제목: The Mountain That Is Closest to the Stars

If you want to stand on the point of Earth that is physically closest to the Moon and stars, you should not book a flight to Mount Everest. Instead, you need to travel to the highlands of Ecuador and climb Mount Chimborazo. On paper, Chimborazo seems modest. Its summit rises 6,263 meters above sea level, making it more than two thousand meters shorter than Everest. Yet, if you measure distance from the center of the Earth, Chimborazo easily defeats every other peak on our planet. Why? Because the Earth is not a perfect sphere. Due to the centrifugal force of our planet's rotation, the Earth bulges around its waistline, making the equator about twenty-one kilometers thicker than the poles. Mount Chimborazo sits just one degree south of the equator, resting directly on top of this massive equatorial bulge. Everest, by contrast, sits much farther north at twenty-eight degrees latitude, where the planet's radius is significantly narrower. When geophysicists measure the distance from Earth's core to the mountain summits, Chimborazo's peak sits over two thousand meters farther from the center of the planet than Everest's peak. Standing on Chimborazo means your head is closer to the cosmos than any other person walking on dry land. The universe is full of surprises once you change your point of reference. [숏츠 3] 제목: Why Earth Can Never Have a 20-Kilometer Mountain

Have you ever wondered why our mountains stop growing? Mount Everest reaches just under nine kilometers above sea level, and no mountain on modern Earth comes close to twenty kilometers. Yet on Mars, Olympus Mons towers an unbelievable twenty-two kilometers into the thin atmosphere, nearly three times taller than Everest. Why can Mars build mountain monsters while Earth cannot? The answer comes down to gravity and continental drift. On Earth, our planet's strong gravitational pull exerts crushing pressure on the roots of giant mountains. Geologists calculate that if an Earth mountain grew past ten or twelve kilometers, the sheer weight would cause the rocks deep beneath it to heat up and deform like soft plastic, making the mountain sink into the mantle. Furthermore, our moving tectonic plates constantly shift mountains away from volcanic hotspots, while rain and glaciers tear them down from above. Mars, on the other hand, is a geological paradise for giants. Its surface gravity is only about one-third of Earth's, allowing the crust to support massive loads without buckling. Moreover, Mars has no moving tectonic plates, so a single volcano can sit over a mantle plume for hundreds of millions of years, stacking lava higher and higher into the sky.

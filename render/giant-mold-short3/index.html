Look out your window
at a tall oak
or pine tree.
It seems completely natural
that trees dominate the
skyline of our planet.
But for millions of
years, Earth had no
wood, no canopy,
and no leafy shade.
No tree had yet
grown taller than a
few meters, and no
forest had ever cast
a shadow on the ground.
In that landscape,
life found a very
different way to touch
the sky.
More than four hundred
million years ago,
towering columns known
as Prototaxites rose from
the barren ground.
Standing as tall as
eight meters, nearly
twenty-six feet, these
spires were most likely
giant fungi.
Why would a fungus
invest so much energy
into building a tower
as tall as a modern house?
A tall tower is
expensive to build.
It needs a lot
of food and a
lot of time.
One hypothesis is spore
dispersal.
Down near the ground,
friction slows the wind
to a gentle breeze.
By lifting its reproductive
tissue high above the
ankle-high mosses,
Prototaxites could have
released millions of
microscopic spores into
faster, higher air currents,
carrying its offspring
across a continent.
It is the same
trick that dandelions and
puffball mushrooms use
today, only on a
far grander scale.
Another possibility is
that the tower worked
like a storage tank.
In a harsh world
where rainfall was
unpredictable, a thick
column could hold water
and nutrients through long
dry spells.
Think of it like
a camel's hump,
except as high as a house.
Nobody knows for sure
which idea is right,
and both could be partly true.
Scientists can only test
these ideas by studying
fossils and living fungi,
so the debate is still open.
What we do know
is that these skyscrapers
stood on the land
for roughly forty million
years.
That is more than
one hundred times longer
than modern humans have
existed.

titans finally vanished

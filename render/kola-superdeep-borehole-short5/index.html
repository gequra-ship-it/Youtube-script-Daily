A rusted metal disc
barely wider than a
dinner plate sits bolted
to broken concrete in
an Arctic ghost town.
If you walked across
the abandoned ruins of
the Kola Peninsula,
you might kick this
twenty-three centimeter
steel cap without giving
it a second thought.
Yet beneath those heavy
industrial bolts lies a
pitch-black vertical shaft
dropping twelve thousand
two hundred sixty-two meters
straight down.
It is the deepest
artificial hole ever drilled
into our planet,
plunging farther down than
Mount Everest rises into
the sky.
It is even deeper
than the Mariana Trench,
about ten thousand nine
hundred meters deep.
For scale, Everest rises
about eight thousand eight
hundred meters.
You could set the
whole mountain inside the
hole and still have
more than three kilometers
of empty shaft beneath it.
Soviet geologists started
drilling on May
twenty-fourth, 1970.
Their goal was fifteen
thousand meters.
For more than twenty
years, Soviet engineers
pushed to reach deeper
into the crust.
They battled crushing
pressure, gas, and rock
that flowed like plastic,
until the extreme heat
finally forced them to
stop in the early
nineteen nineties.
Along the way they
found fossils of twenty-four
species of plankton about
six kilometers down.
They found liquid water
where none should have been.
They found hydrogen bubbling
through the drilling mud.
And they found a
temperature of one hundred
eighty degrees Celsius,
nearly double what they
had predicted.
Today, the drilling rig
is gone, the research
outpost is a windswept
skeleton of rusted rebar,
and the hole is sealed shut.
That small, silent metal
disc is all that
stands over a
twelve-kilometer wound
in the planet.
Yet even that enormous
effort reached only about
zero point two percent
of the way to
the center of the Earth.
We have sent probes
beyond the edge of
our solar system,
yet the world beneath
our shoes remains almost
entirely untouched.

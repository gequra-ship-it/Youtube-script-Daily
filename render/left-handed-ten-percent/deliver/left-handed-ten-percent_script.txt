===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Look around you, in a coffee shop, an office, or a crowded train. Roughly nine out of every ten people you see do most things with their right hand. The tenth person is left-handed. Large studies put that share at about ten percent, and left-handers turn up on every inhabited continent. So here is a strange question. Why not half and half? And if right-handedness works so well, why didn't left-handedness simply disappear?

Look at the objects around you, and you'll see who they were designed for. Kitchen scissors, can openers, spiral notebooks, computer mice and power tools are mostly shaped for the right hand. Left-handers bump elbows at dinner tables and smudge fresh ink as their hand drags across the page. And yet the trait has never faded away. To understand why, we need genetics, neuroscience, and a surprising amount of fighting. Let's start at the very beginning.

About thirty thousand years ago, people in Ice Age Europe pressed their hands against cave walls and blew pigment around them, leaving outlines that still survive. Researchers studied more than five hundred of these hand stencils from twenty-six caves in France and Spain. About three quarters were left hands. That fits a simple picture: most artists held the pigment in their right hand and pressed the free left hand against the rock. Even in the Ice Age, the right hand was usually the busy one.

The evidence goes back much further than art. In a cave pit in northern Spain called the Sima de los Huesos, researchers studied the front teeth of early relatives of the Neanderthals, who lived more than four hundred thousand years ago. These people seem to have gripped meat or hide between their teeth and sliced it with a stone tool. Now and then, the blade nicked the enamel. The angle of those tiny scratches shows which hand held the tool. In all fifteen individuals they studied, it was the right.

Neanderthal teeth tell the same story, and so do their arm bones, which grew thicker on the side that worked harder. Together, these clues suggest that roughly nine out of ten Neanderthals were right-handed, about the same share as people today. So the ratio you see on a modern train is not a product of schools or scissors. It seems to be older than our own species.

Human languages carry a record of how people felt about the two hands. In Latin, the word for left was sinister, and in English it came to mean threatening or evil. The Latin word for right, dexter, gave us dexterity, meaning skill. In French, gauche means left, and it also means clumsy. Droit means right, and it is also the French word for law. Again and again, language linked the right side with what is correct, and the left with what is suspect.

Religious art and custom often followed the same pattern. In many Christian paintings of the Last Judgment, the saved stand at the right hand of Christ, and the damned are sent to his left, following a parable in the Gospel of Matthew. In many Muslim and Hindu communities, the right hand is used for eating and greeting, while the left is kept for washing. None of this says anything about left-handed people. But it shows how deep the preference for the right runs in human culture.

For a long time, that preference was enforced. Well into the twentieth century, many schools in Europe, North America and East Asia made left-handed children write with their right hand. Some teachers tied a child's left hand behind the chair, or rapped their knuckles with a ruler. Many of those children grew up as switched right-handers, writing with one hand while doing other tasks with the other.

Then, as the pressure eased, the numbers changed. Surveys of Americans born around 1900 found only about three percent who called themselves left-handed. Among people born in the second half of the century, the share climbed to roughly eleven or twelve percent. Some people worried that left-handedness was spreading like a fashion. It wasn't. The biology hadn't changed. Children were simply no longer being forced to hide it.

So what decides which hand a baby will prefer? For decades, scientists hoped to find a single left-handed gene. If such a gene existed, two left-handed parents should have mostly left-handed children. They don't. When both parents are left-handed, the chance that their child will be left-handed is about twenty-six percent. That's higher than average, but far from a sure thing. Something more complicated is going on.

Identical twins make the puzzle sharper. They share essentially the same DNA. If handedness were written plainly in the genes, identical twins would always match. Yet in roughly one pair out of every five, one twin is right-handed and the other is left-handed. Twin studies suggest that genes explain only about a quarter of the differences in handedness between people. The rest has to come from somewhere else.

Let's put those numbers side by side. With two left-handed parents, the odds of a left-handed child are about twenty-six percent. Among identical twins, about one pair in five disagrees. And genes account for only about a quarter of the variation. Handedness clearly runs in families, but it doesn't follow a simple rule.

In 2019, researchers at the University of Oxford looked at the genomes of about four hundred thousand people in the UK Biobank, including roughly thirty-eight thousand left-handers. They found a handful of genetic regions linked to left-handedness. None of them works like a switch. Each one only nudges the odds a little. Later, even larger studies found more of these small signals. Handedness, it turns out, is shaped by many genes with tiny effects.

Several of the regions in the Oxford study sit in genes involved in brain development, and some help build microtubules. Microtubules are tiny tubes that act like a scaffold inside our cells. Related cell machinery helps an early embryo tell its left side from its right, which is why your heart usually sits on the left and your liver on the right. Scientists suspect that this same left-right toolkit also plays a part in which hand we prefer.

Handedness also shows up surprisingly early. In ultrasound studies in Belfast, about eighty-five percent of fetuses at ten weeks moved their right arm more than their left. Later in pregnancy, about ninety-five percent were seen sucking their right thumb. The researchers then followed some of these children until they were ten to twelve years old. All sixty who had sucked their right thumb grew up right-handed. Of the fifteen who had sucked their left thumb, ten became left-handers.

For a long time, scientists assumed that hand preference starts in the brain. But a preference can be seen from around the eighth week of pregnancy, before the motor cortex is connected to the spinal cord. In 2017, a team at Ruhr University Bochum in Germany studied fetal spinal cords from the eighth to the twelfth week. Genes were more active on one side than the other, in the segments that control the arms and legs. Hand preference may begin, at least in part, in the spine.

Still, the brain matters a great deal. Its wiring is crossed: the left hemisphere mostly controls the right side of the body, and the right hemisphere controls the left. In the large majority of right-handers, the main language areas sit in the left hemisphere. They include Broca's area, which helps produce speech, and Wernicke's area, which helps us understand it. The side of the brain that steers the right hand also tends to run language.

Why would hand control and language end up on the same side? One hypothesis is that language began partly with gestures. As our ancestors made tools and hunted together, signals made with the hands may have mattered. If the left hemisphere was already handling precise right-hand movements, it may also have taken charge of gestures, and later of speech. The idea is still debated, but it would explain why hands and words seem to share a neighborhood in the brain.

So is a left-hander's brain simply a mirror image? Not at all. Most left-handers still process language mainly in the left hemisphere, just like right-handers. But about three in ten do not. In some of them, language sits on the right side. In others, it is spread across both. Some studies also found that people without a strong hand preference tend to have a larger corpus callosum, the thick bundle of fibers that joins the two hemispheres. On average, left-handed brains are a little more varied.

Are we alone in this? Chimpanzees certainly have favorite hands. In captivity, one large study found about twice as many right-handed chimps as left-handed ones. In the wild, the picture is messier. At some sites, chimps tend to use their right hand for soaking up water with leaves, and their left hand for fishing termites out with sticks. No other ape is known to show anything close to our steady nine-to-one split.

Other animals have their own quirks. Studies of pet cats found that males tend to favor their left paw and females their right. In a study of about three hundred Australian parrots, more birds held food with their left foot than with their right, and every sulphur-crested cockatoo observed was left-footed. Red and eastern grey kangaroos mostly use their left paw for grooming and feeding. Still, among primates, nothing matches the strong right-sided lean of humans.

So why would humans lean so hard to one side? One answer is cooperation. Our ancestors lived in close-knit groups that shared tools, weapons and skills. If most people used the same hand, a tool made by one person fit everyone else. A child could copy an adult's movements without flipping them in their head. Being on the same side as everyone else was simply easier.

In 2012, Daniel Abrams and Mark Panaggio built a mathematical model of this idea. In their model, cooperation pushes a population toward everyone using the same hand. If cooperation were the only force, left-handedness would slowly disappear, and we would live in a fully right-handed world. But the model also includes a second force pulling the other way: competition. The balance between the two can produce a steady minority of left-handers, like the one we see today.

Picture a fight between two ancient warriors. Most fighters spent their lives facing right-handed opponents, and their reflexes were tuned to blows from familiar angles. A left-handed opponent changes everything. The strikes come from the other side, and the timing feels wrong. This is called the fighting hypothesis. In close combat, being left-handed may have offered the advantage of surprise.

Biologists call this negative frequency-dependent selection. A trait is valuable precisely because it is rare. If left-handers became common, everyone would train against them, and the surprise would vanish. So the advantage shrinks as their numbers grow, and the trait can never take over. Cooperation pulls toward the right, competition keeps a small left-handed group alive, and the population settles somewhere in between.

Is there any evidence for this? In 2005, the French researchers Charlotte Faurie and Michel Raymond compared traditional societies around the world. Among the Dioula of Burkina Faso, where homicide was very rare, only about three point four percent of people were left-handed. Among the Eipo of New Guinea, where violence was common when they were studied, the share was about twenty-seven percent. Across the societies, higher homicide rates went along with more left-handers. It's a correlation, not proof, but it fits the fighting hypothesis.

You can still see this advantage today, in sports. In boxing and mixed martial arts, left-handed fighters, often called southpaws, are more common than their ten percent share would predict. In fencing, the overrepresentation is even stronger. A left-handed boxer spends most of their training facing right-handers, so a right-hander holds no surprises. The right-hander, meanwhile, rarely gets to practice against a lefty.

The edge appears in other sports where you face an opponent directly, such as tennis, table tennis, cricket and baseball. Rafael Nadal, one of the most successful tennis players in history, played left-handed, even though he writes with his right hand. But in sports without a direct opponent, like swimming or running, left-handers have no special advantage. The edge exists only when someone is trying to read your moves.

Outside the arena, left-handers have also left their mark. Leonardo da Vinci was left-handed, and he filled his notebooks with mirror writing that runs from right to left across the page. Online lists of famous left-handers often include Albert Einstein too, but there is no good evidence for that. Photos show him writing with his right hand. Those lists are full of guesses, so it's worth checking before you share them.

You may have heard that left-handers are more creative because they are right-brained. The idea that people are either left-brained or right-brained is mostly a myth. Some studies have found that left-handers do slightly better on certain tests of divergent thinking, which means coming up with many different answers to an open question. Other studies found no difference at all. If there is an effect, it is small.

Left-handers have also faced scarier claims. In 1991, the psychologists Diane Halpern and Stanley Coren reported that left-handers died about nine years earlier than right-handers. The headline spread around the world. But the study had a big flaw. Because older generations had been forced to switch hands, there were few left-handers among the old. That made left-handers look as if they died younger. Later studies found little or no difference.

There is a smaller, more practical risk. Many machines, power tools and safety controls are designed for right-handed use. Some studies have reported that left-handers have more accidents with tools and machines, although not every study agrees. It's a reminder that the world was not designed with everyone in mind.

Researchers have also found that left-handedness is somewhat more common among people with certain conditions, such as schizophrenia, and some studies report links with dyslexia. That does not mean left-handedness causes these conditions. One idea is that both may reflect differences in how the brain's two sides develop before birth. The vast majority of left-handers have none of these conditions.

Then there are the presidents. Left-handers make up about a tenth of the population, yet several recent American presidents, including Gerald Ford, George H. W. Bush, Bill Clinton and Barack Obama, were left-handed. In the 1992 election, Bush, Clinton and Ross Perot are all often described as left-handed. Is it a coincidence? With numbers this small, nobody can say for sure.

A 2010 study by Daniel Casasanto and Kyle Jasmin looked at the final presidential debates of 2004 and 2008. The right-handed candidates, John Kerry and George W. Bush, tended to gesture with their right hand for positive statements and their left hand for negative ones. The left-handed candidates, Barack Obama and John McCain, did the opposite. It seems the dominant hand is tied to how we express what we think is good.

In the 1980s, the neurologist Norman Geschwind and his colleague Albert Galaburda proposed another idea. They suggested that higher levels of testosterone in the womb slow the growth of the left hemisphere, which could make left-handedness more likely. The hypothesis drew a lot of attention, but later tests gave mixed results. Today it is seen as, at most, one small piece of the puzzle.

Conditions around birth may also play a role. Some studies link left-handedness to low birth weight, premature birth and difficult deliveries. These links are small, and most left-handers were born without any complications. But they point to the same conclusion. Handedness is not fixed entirely by genes. It's shaped by many small influences, some of them before we're born.

Around the world, the reported share of left-handers varies. A 2020 meta-analysis covering more than two million people put the overall figure at about ten point six percent. But where children are still pushed to use their right hand, far fewer people report being left-handed. That doesn't mean their genes are different. It means culture still decides which hand many people write with.

In fact, handedness is not a simple switch. Scientists measure it on a scale, often with a questionnaire called the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory. Some people use one hand for almost everything. Others write with their left hand but throw a ball or use scissors with their right. People who are truly equally skilled with both hands are rare, about one percent of the population.

Why have a dominant side at all? Specializing can be efficient. If both halves of the brain did exactly the same jobs, they would duplicate a lot of work. By dividing tasks, one hemisphere can focus on fine control and language, while the other handles things like spatial attention. Many scientists think this division of labor is one reason our brains became lopsided.

So let's return to our question. Why didn't humans settle at fifty-fifty? Because a half-and-half group would lose the benefits of sharing the same tools and the same movements. Cooperation pushed us toward one side. Then why not one hundred percent? Because a lone left-handed fighter would have the advantage of surprise over everyone. Competition kept the left hand alive. About ten percent may be where those two forces balance.

Today, the world is slowly making room for left-handers. You can buy left-handed scissors, guitars, keyboards and sports gear. Every year on August thirteenth, International Left-Handers Day draws attention to the small daily challenges of living in a right-handed world. Children are no longer tied to their chairs. And handedness is increasingly seen as one more part of human variety.

The next time you see someone writing with their left hand, think of the long story behind it. Ice Age hands on cave walls. Scratches on teeth more than four hundred thousand years old. A balance between working together and fighting. Whichever hand you write with, you are part of that story. If this changed the way you look at your own hands, subscribing to Miya's Lab helps us bring you the next strange question. And tell us in the comments: which hand do you use, and were you ever asked to switch?

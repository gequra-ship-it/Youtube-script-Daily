Imagine a ship breaking
apart on a reef
in the middle of
the night, far from
any shore you know.
The island it struck
is called Tromelin:
a strip of sand
and coral about seventeen
hundred meters long,
with no trees and no river.
To understand this story,
we have to travel
back to 1761.
A French East India
Company ship named the
Utile was sailing across
the Indian Ocean.
Its captain, Jean de
La Fargue, had taken
on well over a
hundred enslaved Malagasy
men and women in
Madagascar, even though
he was not authorized
to trade in people.
They were packed below
deck, bound for the
French colony of Isle
de France, known today
as Mauritius.
To the men who
ran the ship,
the people in the
hold were cargo.
Navigating the ocean in
the eighteenth century
was a dangerous gamble.
There was no satellite
and no radio,
and a small error
could put a ship
on a reef.
Charts were inaccurate,
and captains relied on
dead reckoning, an estimate
of position from speed
and heading.
On the night of
July thirty-first, 1761,
the navigators believed
they were in open
water, far from any danger.
They were wrong.
In the darkness lay
a ring of coral
reef, and nobody on
board knew it was there.
The Utile ran hard aground.
Waves pounded the hull,
and the ship began
to break apart.
The sailors scrambled for
the upper decks and
the boats, while the
enslaved people below were
trapped in the dark.
Many of them did
not make it out.
Those who did fought
through the churning surf.
When the sun rose
the next morning,
the survivors found
themselves standing on
a flat, barren sandbank.
More than one hundred
and twenty French crew
members, and around sixty
Malagasy men and women,
had made it to shore.
Those who survived the
surf did not yet
know what awaited them.

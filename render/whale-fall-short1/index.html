200 TONS
4 STAGES
1987
Picture a creature weighing
up to two hundred
tons, sinking into total
darkness.
Its death is about
to feed a desert.
The real miracle happens
when the carcass does
not wash up on
a beach, but sinks
into the open sea.
Marine biologists call
this a whale fall.
Many carcasses float for
a while, buoyed by
the gases of decomposition.
Once those gases escape,
or if the whale
was lean and dense
to begin with,
the body sinks.
Slowly, the carcass begins
a silent descent,
traveling thousands of
meters down into perpetual
darkness.
To understand a whale
fall, you must understand
the deep seafloor.
The abyssal plains cover
a huge share of
our planet, and much
of that ground is
a biological desert.
Sunlight never reaches
it, temperatures hover
near freezing, and the
pressure is crushing.
Food is scarce.
Most animals live on
marine snow, which is
tiny flakes of dead
plankton and debris drifting
down from above.
For this starved world,
a whale is a
windfall beyond comprehension.
After hours or even
days of falling,
the whale touches down
on the muddy plain.
The impact likely kicks
up thick clouds of silt.
In an instant,
a flat, barren landscape
has gained a mountain of food.
Scientists divide the life
of a whale fall
into four overlapping
stages, and the first
begins right away:
the mobile-scavenger stage.
Although the deep ocean
seems empty, news travels
fast through chemical
currents.
Scent trails from the
carcass spread through
the cold water,
and scavengers with highly
sensitive chemical senses
can pick them up
from far away.
One by one,
they begin swimming against
the current toward the source.
A banquet has just
been delivered to a
seafloor that has gone
hungry for a very long time.
Scientists did not even
know this world existed
until 1987.
That year, researchers
diving in the submersible
Alvin, in the Santa
Catalina Basin off
California, came across
the skeleton of a
gray whale resting on
the seafloor, swarming
with strange life.
It was the first
whale fall community that
scientists studied up close.
Since then, researchers
have studied whale falls
by finding them with
robotic submarines and
by revisiting them again
and again over the years.
By now, the entire
seafloor around the body
is waking up,
and the first guests
are not shy.

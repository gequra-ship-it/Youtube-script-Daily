===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Could a planet really orbit a supermassive black hole, close enough to slow its time by tens of thousands of times, and survive? Physics says only under extreme conditions. What kind of black hole would it take?

A planet simply cannot survive at the precipice of an ordinary, non-rotating Schwarzschild black hole. In a non-rotating system, the region needed for a time-dilation factor of tens of thousands to one lies so close to the event horizon that circular orbits become completely unstable.

The theoretical breakthrough came in 1963, when the New Zealand mathematician Roy Kerr solved Einstein's field equations for a spinning mass. A spinning Kerr black hole drags the structure of space and time around with it, like honey stirred by a high-speed blender.

This dragging of reality is known as frame-dragging, or the Lense-Thirring effect. Close to a Kerr black hole, space itself is swept around at nearly the speed of light. The minimum distance at which a planet can safely orbit without plunging inward moves dramatically closer to the event horizon.

However, spin alone is not enough. The black hole must also be unfathomably large. If you placed a planet next to a stellar black hole ten times the mass of the Sun, the pull on the near side of the planet would be far stronger than the pull on the far side. These differences, called tidal forces, would stretch the planet like taffy and tear it to pieces. But if the black hole has the mass of one hundred million Suns, the tidal stretch across a planet becomes remarkably gentle.

The theoretical physicist Kip Thorne, a Nobel laureate, calculated what kind of spin would be required for the three-hour to twenty-three-year ratio on a surviving world. The supermassive black hole must spin at about 99.999999999999 percent of its theoretical maximum rate.

He showed that while the conditions required are extraordinarily rare, demanding a precise combination of mass, spin, and orbital radius, not a single law of physics is broken. The universe does not forbid a world where three hours equal twenty-three years.

It demands an extreme laboratory of gravity that pushes reality to its limits. The full story, with the giant waves and the one-way ticket through time, is on Miya's Lab.

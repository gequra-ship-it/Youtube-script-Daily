A year after the
Minnesota frogs hit the
news, the chemical
explanation was failing.
Then a young researcher
named Pieter Johnson went
looking in a different place.
Instead of staying inside
a laboratory, Johnson went
straight into the ponds
of Santa Clara County,
California, where deformed
frogs had also been found.
He surveyed about thirty
ponds.
He compared the water,
the animals, and the
exact places where deformed
frogs appeared.
This was patient field
detective work.
That is the power
of going outside.
A laboratory can test
one chemical at a
time, but a pond
contains everything at
once: water, snails,
tadpoles, birds, and often
a farm next door.
Johnson followed the animals
instead of guessing at
the cause.
A pattern emerged.
Deformed amphibians turned
up only in ponds
that also held a
certain kind of snail.
That was a strange clue.
A chemical spill does
not need a snail
to do its damage.
Then researchers looked
closely at the deformed
frogs and saw something
startling.
Around the pelvic region
and the limb buds
were clusters of tiny cysts.
Inside each capsule lived
a microscopic parasitic
flatworm.
Its scientific name is
Ribeiroia ondatrae.
The culprit was not
a chemical spill.
It was not nuclear waste.
It was a parasite.
To understand how a
worm this small could
do this, you have
to know that Ribeiroia
is a trematode.
That means it cannot
live its whole life
inside one animal.
It needs three different
hosts to complete its cycle.
Its journey begins at
the bottom of a
muddy pond, in a
humble freshwater ramshorn
snail.
There is also an
important historical detail.
When scientists resurveyed
old collection sites,
they found that
malformations at six of
eight sites were linked
to Ribeiroia, and the
oldest case went back to 1946.
So this was not
a brand-new problem.
It was an old relationship.
That raises a puzzle.
If the parasite has
been around for so
long, why did the
1990s bring such a
startling wave of deformities?
Remember that question,
because the answer involves
farmers, fertilizer, and
a lot of algae.

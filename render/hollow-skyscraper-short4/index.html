Imagine standing on a
hill with a strong
wind pushing at your back.
Now imagine that wind
pushing against a wall
hundreds of meters tall.
There is still a puzzle.
When wind pushes against
the side of the
tower, the core wants
to bend one way,
while the outer parts
of the wings stand
independently.
How do you force
these separate pieces to
work as one team?
Picture a skier racing
down a snowy slope.
With arms tucked in,
a bump knocks them
off balance.
But spread the ski
poles out and plant
them in the snow,
and the skier gets
a wide, steady base
that keeps the body
from tumbling.
Tall buildings use the
same idea, called outriggers.
Burj Khalifa reportedly
ties its vertical structure
together at the mechanical
floors with outrigger walls,
which are meant to
make the whole building
stiffer.
When wind pushes on
the tower, those walls
help pass the push
from the core out
to the rest of the structure.
The side facing the
wind is pulled into
tension, while the far
side is squeezed into
compression.
The building resists the
storm with leverage,
not with brute mass.
Baker's team called this
a buttressed core.
Think of a Gothic cathedral.
Its tall walls would
lean outward if not
for the stone buttresses
pressed against them.
Baker used almost exactly
that comparison himself.
That tension and compression
are simple ideas.
Pull on a rope
and it is in tension.
Squeeze a pillar and
it is in compression.
A tower in the
wind does both at
once, one side pulling
and the other side squeezing.
With the right structure,
the tower can turn
a sideways push into
a pulling force on
one side and a
squeezing force on the other.
Those are exactly the
forces that concrete and
steel handle best.
The skier and the
tower share a lesson.
Strength is not only
about being strong.
It is about where
you place your support,
and how far from
the center you plant it.
Next time you see
a very tall tower,
look at its shape.
Behind the glass,
its stability comes from
the structure hidden inside.

from the foundation to
the spire, is in

Picture a Carboniferous
swamp, thick with towering
trees.
Have you ever considered
that the lump of
coal burning in a
winter furnace was once
a living tree in
a swamp like that?
Over millions of years,
those unrotted trunks were
crushed under immense heat
and pressure, storing solar
energy that would power
the Industrial Revolution
hundreds of millions of
years later.
At the same time,
oxygen levels in the
Carboniferous sky rose
high, which scientists
suspect helped insects
grow huge.
Dragonfly relatives with
wingspans of about seventy
centimeters darted through
the humid air,
while giant millipede-like
creatures crawled through
the thick, spongy ferns.
Eventually, the swampy
climate dried and shifted,
ushering in the Permian
Period.
The scattered continents
drifted together, slowly
colliding to form the
colossal supercontinent
of Pangaea.
With such a massive
landmass, moisture could
not penetrate deep inland,
creating sprawling interior
deserts of red sand
and baked stone.
Here, the ancestors of
mammals, known as synapsids,
began their ascent.
Creatures like Dimetrodon
walked the arid plains,
sporting a magnificent
skin sail along their
backs that may have
helped regulate body
temperature under the
blistering desert sun.
Yet, this thriving world
was marching toward the
greatest catastrophe the
biosphere would ever
experience.
At the close of
the Permian, roughly 252
million years ago,
immense volcanic fissures
opened in what is
now Siberia, disgorging
millions of cubic kilometers
of molten basalt over
hundreds of thousands of
years.
Scientists think the magma
also burned through buried
coal, spewing sulfur
dioxide, greenhouse gases,
and acidic ash into
the upper atmosphere.
The oceans turned acidic
and lost their dissolved
oxygen, while global
temperatures spiked violently.
How could anything survive
when roughly ninety percent
of all ocean species
simply ceased to exist?
The Great Dying,
or the Permian-Triassic
extinction event, brought
life on Earth closer
to absolute annihilation
than at any other
moment in planetary history.
On land, an estimated
seventy percent of
terrestrial vertebrate
species disappeared into
the ash-choked dust.
Many of the dominant
synapsids were decimated,
and the ancient forests
collapsed.
For millions of years,
the world was a
silent wasteland, populated
by only a tiny
handful of hardy survivors.
Life came very close
to the end,
and it kept going anyway.

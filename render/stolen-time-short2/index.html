Gravity bends time,
and your phone proves
it every single day.
You do not need
to travel to the
edge of the galaxy
to see that this is real.
Your smartphone uses it
every day to give
you driving directions.
GPS satellites orbit about
twenty thousand kilometers
up, where gravity is weaker.
Because of that,
their atomic clocks run
about forty-five
microseconds faster per
day than clocks on the ground.
Their high speed slows
them back down by
about seven microseconds,
so the net difference
is roughly thirty-eight.
That sounds tiny.
But if engineers did
not correct for it,
GPS positions would drift
by about ten kilometers
every single day.
On Earth, the difference
is only millionths of
a second, because our
planet is a tiny
cosmic pebble.
So what happens if
we put a world
beside the most extreme
object nature allows?
To make three hours
equal twenty-three Earth
years, the time dilation
factor must reach roughly
sixty-seven thousand.
One hour on the
planet must match about
sixty-seven thousand hours
far away.
Enter the realm of
supermassive black holes.
A black hole born
from a collapsing star
typically holds about five
to a few tens of Suns.
But supermassive black
holes hold millions,
or even billions, of Suns.
Near such a monster,
spacetime is not a
gentle hill.
It plunges into a
bottomless pit.
There, time dilation grows
from microseconds to human
lifetimes.
Every black hole is
wrapped in a boundary
called the event horizon.
It is the point
of no return.
Beyond it, the escape
speed exceeds the speed
of light, so nothing
can climb back out:
no radio wave,
no laser, no particle.
And here is the strange part.
If you could hover
ever closer to the
event horizon of a
non-rotating black hole,
time dilation would grow
without limit.
To a far-away observer,
your watch would slow
down and seem to freeze.
And that is only
the beginning of how
far this bending can go.

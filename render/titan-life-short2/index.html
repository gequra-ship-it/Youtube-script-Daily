JANUARY 2005
For a long time,
Titan remained shrouded
in mystery because its
golden smog blocked our
view of the surface
in visible light.
What lay hidden beneath
that veil?
That changed when the
Cassini spacecraft arrived
at Saturn and released
the Huygens probe toward
the surface.
In January of 2005,
Huygens drifted down beneath
its parachute, and its
cameras revealed coastlines,
drainage channels, and
a landscape strewn with
rounded pebbles.
Other Cassini instruments
later mapped fields of
sand dunes, rivers, and lakes.
Scientists were stunned
to see how much
of it looked like Earth.
Astronomers had assumed
that a moon so
cold would be quiet.
The surprise was that
weather and erosion,
two processes we think
of as belonging to
Earth, were shaping this
distant place as well.
Titan turned out to
be not a frozen
relic, but a place
where things are still
happening.
High in Titan's upper
atmosphere, sunlight and
energetic particles smash
into nitrogen and methane
molecules, splitting them
apart.
These fragments recombine
into complex organic
compounds, forming the
orange haze.
Scientists call these
compounds tholins.
Slowly, over countless
millennia, grains of this
carbon-rich material drift
down to carpet the
moon's surface.
Sunlight and energetic
particles act like a
hammer on the molecules
in the upper air,
and the broken pieces
reassemble into larger,
more complicated
carbon-based compounds.
That tells us the
raw ingredients of
chemistry, the kind of
building blocks that life
on Earth also depends
on, are being made
there naturally.
But think of a
kitchen full of flour,
eggs, and sugar.
The ingredients are all
there, but there is
no guarantee anyone is
baking a cake.
Could these organic
molecules ever assemble
themselves into something
that copies itself,
without liquid water?
No one knows.
That is the reason
scientists keep looking.
A moon that manufactures
complex carbon compounds
in its sky is
a remarkable place to
study how chemistry might
climb toward biology.
Imagine those carbon-rich
grains drifting slowly
down through the haze,
settling like dust on
a shelf, layer after
layer, for longer than
humans have existed.

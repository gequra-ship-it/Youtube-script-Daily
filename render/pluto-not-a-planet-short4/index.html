What happens when an
ancient scientific word
finally needs a strict
definition?
In the summer of
2005, astronomers announced
Eris, a frozen world
far beyond Neptune that
was about twenty-seven
percent heavier than Pluto.
Mike Brown's team even
nicknamed it Xena,
and Eris has a
small moon of its
own, named Dysnomia.
Many people first called
Eris the tenth planet.
Meanwhile, telescopes were
finding more icy worlds
beyond Neptune, such as
Haumea and Makemake.
A crisis began.
The word planet had
never had a strict
scientific definition.
If Pluto was a
planet, did that mean
Eris was planet number ten?
What about Sedna and Makemake?
Would children have to
memorize hundreds of planet
names?
In August 2006,
thousands of astronomers
gathered in Prague for
a general assembly of
the International
Astronomical Union.
At first, a proposal
even suggested twelve planets.
But after days of
debate, the astronomers
wrote three golden rules
for planethood.
One: orbit the Sun.
Two: have enough gravity
to be round.
Pluto passed both.
But Pluto failed the
third rule completely.
Three: clear the
neighborhood around your
orbit.
Pluto shares its path
with thousands of icy
neighbors in the Kuiper
Belt, and it is
locked in a resonance
with Neptune.
On August 24,
2006, the astronomers voted.
Pluto became a dwarf
planet, and so did
Eris and the asteroid-belt
world Ceres.
Our solar system officially
had eight planets.
Only a few hundred
astronomers cast the final
votes, and the decision
stunned the world.
Children wrote letters
of protest.
In 2007, New Mexico,
where Clyde Tombaugh had
lived, even declared Pluto
a planet in its skies.

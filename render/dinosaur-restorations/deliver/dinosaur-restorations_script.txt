===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

In a park in south London stands a strange, bulky creature made of concrete, with a horn on its nose. More than a hundred and seventy years ago, this was what the best scientists of the day thought a dinosaur looked like. They were wrong, in many surprising ways. Today, let's follow how pictures of extinct animals changed, from swamp monsters to feathered, colorful animals, and why every generation was so sure it had finally got it right.

Long before paleontology existed, people found enormous bones in the ground and tried to explain them. Some historians suggest that ancient Greeks saw the bones of giant ancient mammals as the remains of heroes or monsters from their myths. In China, fossil bones and teeth were long sold as dragon bones for traditional medicine.

The modern story begins in the 1820s in Sussex, England, with a doctor named Gideon Mantell. According to the traditional story, his wife Mary Ann found some unusual fossil teeth. Mantell noticed that they looked like the teeth of a modern iguana, only much bigger. So he imagined the animal as a giant lizard, and in 1825 he named it Iguanodon, meaning iguana tooth. That simple comparison shaped the way the animal was pictured for decades.

Among the bones Mantell later studied was a cone-shaped spike. He guessed that it was a horn, and in his sketches it ended up on the tip of the animal's nose, like a rhinoceros. Early pictures showed Iguanodon as a huge four-legged lizard with a spike on its snout. It was a reasonable guess from a few scattered bones. It was also completely wrong.

The horn was finally explained in 1878, deep in a coal mine at Bernissart in Belgium. Miners found dozens of Iguanodon skeletons, many of them nearly complete. When the scientist Louis Dollo studied them, the mystery spike turned up on the hand. It was a thumb spike, not a nose horn. And the skeletons suggested an animal that could rise up on its strong hind legs, not a lizard sprawled on four.

Before that discovery, London built the world's first life-sized dinosaur sculptures. In 1854, the artist Benjamin Waterhouse Hawkins unveiled them in the grounds of the Crystal Palace, working with the anatomist Richard Owen. Owen saw dinosaurs as advanced reptiles with sturdy, almost mammal-like legs, so the sculptures look like heavy, elephant-shaped lizards. They are still there today, and they are now protected as historic monuments.

Hawkins also knew how to make headlines. On New Year's Eve, 1853, he held a dinner for scientists and guests inside the mold of one of his Iguanodon models. Men in formal suits sat at a table inside the body of a half-finished dinosaur, eating and making speeches. It was a celebration of science, even though the shape they were celebrating would soon look very old-fashioned.

Let's put the three versions side by side. In 1825, Mantell named Iguanodon from a handful of teeth and imagined a giant iguana. In 1854, the Crystal Palace sculpture showed a heavy four-legged beast with a horn on its nose. And after the Bernissart skeletons were found in 1878, the horn moved to the thumb, and the animal stood up on its hind legs.

In America, the race to find dinosaurs turned into a bitter feud known as the Bone Wars. Edward Drinker Cope and Othniel Charles Marsh competed for decades to find and name new fossils in the American West. They raced each other into print, attacked each other's work, and were accused of bribery and of damaging fossil sites to keep them from the other. In their hurry, both men made famous mistakes.

In 1869, Cope described Elasmosaurus, a marine reptile with an enormously long neck. But in his reconstruction, he put the skull on the wrong end, at the tip of the tail. His mentor, Joseph Leidy, pointed out the error. Cope tried to recall and destroy the printed copies and published a corrected version. Marsh, his rival, kept bringing up the mistake for decades.

Marsh had his own famous slip. In 1879 he named a giant long-necked dinosaur Brontosaurus, the thunder lizard. Its skeleton was found without a skull, so his reconstruction borrowed a short, boxy skull based on a different dinosaur, Camarasaurus. For about a century, museum mounts of this kind of sauropod wore the wrong kind of head. The longer, more slender head was only widely accepted in the late 1970s.

Then there was Stegosaurus, named by Marsh in 1877. Its large, flat bony plates were found scattered near the backbone, so at first he thought they lay flat along the back, like tiles on a roof. That is where the name comes from: Stegosaurus means roof lizard. Later and better skeletons showed that the plates stood upright, in two rows along the back.

Stegosaurus also gave rise to a famous myth. Its brain was tiny for such a big animal. But there was a large space in the spine near the hips, and Marsh suggested it might have held a second nerve center that helped control the back legs and tail. The idea spread as the dinosaur with two brains. Today, many scientists think that space held something else, possibly a store of energy-rich tissue like the one found in birds.

Around 1900, paleoart found its first great master, the American painter Charles R. Knight. He studied living animals closely and worked with scientists to bring skeletons back to life. His 1897 painting of two leaping predators, known as Leaping Laelaps, showed dinosaurs as lively, fighting animals. His museum murals shaped how millions pictured the prehistoric world.

But artists followed the science of their time. For much of the twentieth century, many experts thought giant sauropods like Brontosaurus and Diplodocus were too heavy to move easily on land. So they were often painted standing in lakes and swamps, with the water taking some of their weight and only their long necks rising above the surface. Dinosaurs in general were seen as slow, dim, cold-blooded animals.

Physics eventually sank that idea. In 1951, the British scientist Kenneth Kermack pointed out a problem. An animal standing deep underwater with only its head at the surface would have water pressing hard against its chest. Its lungs could barely expand, so it would struggle to breathe. Later studies of sauropod feet, legs and trackways also pointed to animals that walked on dry land.

Big predators were drawn just as wrongly. For decades, Tyrannosaurus rex was shown standing upright, like a giant kangaroo, with its heavy tail resting on the ground. Museums mounted skeletons this way, and movie monsters copied the pose. It looked powerful, but it did not fit the skeleton. To stand like that, the animal would have had to bend or even break its own joints.

Footprints helped settle the question. Thousands of dinosaur trackways have been found around the world, and drag marks from tails are extremely rare. If dinosaurs had dragged their tails, the ancient mud should be full of grooves. Instead, the tracks show that dinosaurs carried their tails up off the ground, balancing the body like a seesaw over the hips.

Mistakes were not only made with dinosaurs. In 1909, Charles Walcott discovered the Burgess Shale in the Canadian Rockies, full of strange animals more than five hundred million years old. Many of them looked so alien that scientists struggled to place them. Some of the results were, looking back, very funny.

Take Anomalocaris. Its spiny front appendages were first described as the bodies of a kind of shrimp. Its round mouth, with plates arranged in a ring, was named as a jellyfish called Peytoia. And a crushed body fossil was treated as a sea cucumber. For decades, these pieces were described as separate animals.

In the 1980s, the paleontologists Harry Whittington and Derek Briggs showed that these pieces belonged together. Whittington found one of the shrimp-like appendages still attached near the mouth. The parts formed a single animal: a swimming predator with large eyes, flaps along its body and two grasping front limbs. It was one of the largest animals of its time, probably up to about forty centimeters long.

Then there is Hallucigenia, a tiny worm-like animal whose name comes from the word hallucination. In 1977, Simon Conway Morris reconstructed it walking on seven pairs of stiff spines, with a row of soft tentacles waving from its back. Scientists wondered how any animal could walk on stiff, pointed spikes.

The answer was that it had been drawn upside down. In the early 1990s, new fossils from China showed that the spines were on its back, for defense, and the soft tentacles were really legs. Then, in 2015, a closer study found its real head, with eyes and a mouth, at the other end. The blob once taken for a head was probably a smear of fluid from the decaying body. The animal had been upside down and backwards.

Back on land, the big change came in the 1960s. In Montana, the Yale paleontologist John Ostrom studied a new predator he named Deinonychus in 1969. It was light, with a stiffened tail for balance and a large curved claw on each foot. Ostrom argued that this was an active, agile hunter, not a sluggish lizard. His work helped start what became known as the Dinosaur Renaissance.

Ostrom's student Robert Bakker pushed the idea further. He argued that dinosaurs were warm-blooded, active animals, closer to birds and mammals than to lizards. He drew them running and leaping. Many colleagues were skeptical, and the details are still debated today. But the old image of dinosaurs as slow, dull reptiles started to fade.

Many people first saw this new image in 1993, in the film Jurassic Park. Advised by paleontologists, including Jack Horner, the filmmakers showed dinosaurs as fast, alert animals, with tails held high off the ground.

No animal shows how pictures can change better than Spinosaurus. In 1912, fossils of a huge predator were found in the Egyptian desert, and the German paleontologist Ernst Stromer described them in 1915. The animal had long jaws and spines on its back at least one point six five meters long, which seemed to hold up a large sail.

Stromer's fossils were kept in a museum in Munich. Stromer was a critic of the Nazi regime, and tension with the museum's Nazi director meant the fossils were not moved to safety. On the night of the twenty-fourth to the twenty-fifth of April, 1944, Allied bombs hit the museum, and the Spinosaurus fossils were destroyed. Only Stromer's drawings and descriptions remained.

For decades, Spinosaurus was known mostly from those drawings and a few scraps. Artists often gave it the body of a typical large two-legged predator, added a sail on its back and a long, crocodile-like snout. In the 2001 film Jurassic Park Three, it was shown defeating a Tyrannosaurus. To many fans, it became the greatest land predator of all.

Then new fossils from Morocco changed the picture. In 2014, a team led by Nizar Ibrahim described a partial skeleton with surprisingly short hind legs and dense bones. They proposed that Spinosaurus spent much of its life in rivers, catching fish. Some other scientists disputed parts of this reconstruction, and the argument was only beginning.

In 2020, the same team described its tail. It was tall and flat, with long spines forming something like a fin, a bit like the tail of a newt. They argued it could push the animal through water. Other researchers have since argued that Spinosaurus waded in shallow water more like a heron, or was a slow surface swimmer. The debate continues, and that is exactly the point: the picture is still changing.

In just over a century, Spinosaurus has been redrawn again and again. In 1915, Stromer described a giant predator with a sail. In 1944, the original fossils were lost in the war. In 2014, new fossils gave it short hind legs and a life in the water. And in 2020, a paddle-like tail made it look more like a swimmer than ever. Scientists are still arguing about how it really lived.

Another habit in paleoart was what artists call shrink-wrapping. Many restorations were made by drawing a skeleton and then stretching a thin layer of skin tightly over it. Every opening in the skull and every bump of bone showed through. The animals looked starved, with no fat, no muscle bulk and no soft tissue.

In 2012, the paleoartists John Conway and C. M. Kosemen, with the paleontologist Darren Naish, published a book called All Yesterdays. To show the problem, they drew living animals the same shrink-wrapped way, using only their skeletons. A swan or a cat drawn like this looked like a strange, bony monster. Future scientists, they suggested, would get our animals very wrong if they used our old methods.

That brings up a question that is still argued about: did big predatory dinosaurs have lips? Movies usually show rows of bare teeth, like a crocodile. But a 2023 study compared tooth wear and skull shapes, and concluded that many theropods, including Tyrannosaurus, probably had lips that covered their teeth when the mouth was closed, more like a monitor lizard.

The biggest visual change came from northeastern China. In 1996, a farmer in Liaoning Province found Sinosauropteryx, a small predatory dinosaur with a fringe of simple, hair-like filaments along its back and tail. It was the first non-bird dinosaur found with feather-like coverings. In the years that followed, many more feathered dinosaurs came out of the same rocks.

The scaly, lizard-like raptors of nineties films soon looked outdated. In 2007, scientists found quill knobs on the forearm bone of a Velociraptor. In living birds, these small bumps anchor large wing feathers. So Velociraptor almost certainly had feathered arms. It was not a scaly monster, but a feathered, bird-like hunter about the size of a turkey.

For a long time, the colors in paleoart were pure guesswork. Then, in 2010, researchers looked at fossil feathers under powerful electron microscopes. They found tiny structures called melanosomes, which hold pigment. Different shapes of melanosomes give different colors in living birds. By comparing them, scientists could start to estimate the real colors of extinct animals.

Sinosauropteryx was one of the first dinosaurs to get a color. The 2010 study found that its tail had alternating bands, reddish-brown and pale, a striped tail a little like a red panda's. A later study, in 2017, suggested it was darker on top and lighter underneath, with a dark band across the eyes, like a bandit mask. For the first time, artists were painting a dinosaur's colors from evidence.

Soon other dinosaurs followed. Anchiornis, a small feathered dinosaur from China, was found to have a mostly gray and black body, white wing feathers with black tips, and a reddish crown of feathers on its head. And in 2012, a study of Microraptor found that its black feathers probably had a glossy, iridescent sheen, like a modern crow or starling.

Here is how quickly the colors arrived. In 2010, Sinosauropteryx got a striped, reddish-brown tail. The same year, Anchiornis got gray, black and white feathers with a reddish crest. In 2012, Microraptor got glossy black, iridescent feathers. Within a few years, dinosaurs went from pure guesswork to animals with colors backed by evidence.

After these discoveries, some artists put feathers on almost every dinosaur, and giant tyrannosaurs began to look like huge, fluffy birds. But in 2017, a study of skin impressions from Tyrannosaurus and its relatives found small scales on the neck, hips and tail. So adult Tyrannosaurus was probably mostly scaly. One idea is that a very large body holds heat easily and doesn't need an insulating coat, though younger animals might have been fuzzier.

Scans have also changed how we see sauropods. Their huge neck bones were full of air spaces, connected to a system of air sacs similar to those of modern birds. That made their skeletons much lighter than they looked, while staying strong. Instead of heavy, swamp-bound giants, sauropods now appear as well-built land animals, with long necks that could reach a wide area of food without moving their bodies.

Another surprise came from studying how dinosaurs grew. By looking at bone tissue under the microscope, scientists can tell whether an animal was young or fully grown. In 2009, Jack Horner and Mark Goodwin argued that two dome-headed dinosaurs, Dracorex and Stygimoloch, were really young forms of Pachycephalosaurus. As the animals grew, their skulls changed shape so much that they had been given different names.

In 2010, a similar idea was proposed for Triceratops. Torosaurus, with its larger frill pierced by two openings, might be a fully grown Triceratops. This idea is still debated, and many paleontologists think they were separate animals. But it shows that some dinosaurs may have changed dramatically as they aged, and a single restoration can only show one moment in an animal's life.

Look back over the last hundred and seventy years. We started with concrete lizards in a London park, with horns on their noses. We passed through swamp-bound giants and tail-dragging monsters. Now we have scanned skeletons, fossil colors and computer models. And at every stage, people believed they were close to the final answer.

So what will people a hundred years from now think of our pictures? They may laugh at our feathers, our lips, or our Spinosaurus. Being willing to be proven wrong is not a weakness of science. It is how science works.

Mantell's horn and Cope's misplaced head were not failures, but first attempts. Which old restoration surprised you most? Tell us in the comments. And if you enjoyed this journey, subscribing to Miya's Lab helps us bring you the next story of how we learned to see the past.

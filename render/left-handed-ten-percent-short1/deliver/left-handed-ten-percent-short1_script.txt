===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

About one person in ten is left-handed, and that ratio seems to be older than our own species. So here is the puzzle. If sharing the same hand makes life easier, why didn't left-handers simply disappear? One possible answer starts with a fight.

First, why would most people favor one side at all? One answer is cooperation. Our ancestors lived in close-knit groups that shared tools, weapons and skills. If most people used the same hand, a tool made by one person fit everyone else, and a child could copy an adult's movements without flipping them around.

In 2012, Daniel Abrams and Mark Panaggio built a mathematical model of this idea. If cooperation were the only force, left-handedness would slowly disappear. But their model also includes a second force pulling the other way: competition. The balance between the two can produce a steady minority of left-handers.

Picture a fight between two ancient warriors. Most fighters spent their lives facing right-handed opponents, and their reflexes were tuned to blows from familiar angles. A left-handed opponent changes everything. The strikes come from the other side, and the timing feels wrong. This is called the fighting hypothesis.

Biologists call this negative frequency-dependent selection. A trait is valuable precisely because it is rare. If left-handers became common, everyone would train against them, and the surprise would vanish. So the advantage shrinks as their numbers grow, and the trait can never take over.

Is there any evidence? In 2005, the French researchers Charlotte Faurie and Michel Raymond compared traditional societies. Among the Dioula of Burkina Faso, where homicide was very rare, about three point four percent were left-handed. Among the Eipo of New Guinea, where violence was common, about twenty-seven percent. It's a correlation, not proof.

You can still see the edge today, in sports where you face an opponent. In boxing and mixed martial arts, left-handed fighters, often called southpaws, are more common than their ten percent share would predict. In swimming or running, they have no special advantage. The edge exists only when someone is trying to read your moves.

So cooperation pulls us toward one side, and competition keeps a small left-handed group alive. About ten percent may be where those two forces balance. The full story, from cave walls to twin studies, is on Miya's Lab.

Is Mount Everest really
the tallest mountain on Earth?
Everyone learns the answer
in school, but the
real answer depends on
where you place your ruler.
Measure from sea level,
and Everest wins.
Measure from the true
base of the mountain,
where its rock begins,
and another giant steps
forward.
Measure from the center
of the planet,
and it is a
different mountain again.
Three rulers, three winners.
So which one is
the real champion,
and are there mountains
on other worlds that
make all of them look small?
Imagine for a moment
that the ocean waters
were completely drained away.
Suddenly, Mount Everest
is no longer alone
at the top.
Other silent giants step
forward from the deep
ocean floor and the
equatorial bulge of our
spinning globe.
Welcome to Miya's Note,
where today we explore
what truly makes a
mountain the tallest in
the world.
Let us begin high
in the frozen realm
of the Himalayas.
Picture sharp granite,
blinding white glaciers,
and winds that roar
like jet engines.
Mount Everest straddles
the border between Nepal
and China, rising to
an officially recognized
8,848 meters above sea level.
At the summit,
the air holds barely
one-third of the oxygen
found at the coast.
Climbers enter what
high-altitude doctors call
the death zone,
where the body slowly
starves of oxygen.
Yet the stone beneath
a climber's boots holds
a secret.
Near the summit,
a band of limestone
holds marine fossils,
the remains of sea
creatures that lived in
a shallow ocean hundreds
of millions of years ago.
How did ocean floor
end up on the
rooftop of the world?
Tens of millions of
years ago, India drifted
northward and collided
with Eurasia.
The crust crumpled and
folded, shoving ancient
seabeds into the sky,
and the Himalayas still
rise by a few
millimeters each year.
By altitude above sea
level, Everest is the
undisputed king of dry land.
But why did we
decide that sea level
should be the judge?

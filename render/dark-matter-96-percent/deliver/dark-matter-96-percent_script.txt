===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Look around the room you are in right now. Touch the desk in front of you, feel the cool air filling your lungs, or gaze at the glowing screen in your hands. Everything you see, touch, and experience feels solidly real. It feels like the entire story of reality. But what if all of this is merely an insignificant fraction of what actually exists? Modern astrophysics tells us something deeply unsettling. Everything humanity has ever studied, from the smallest atom to the most colossal galaxy, accounts for only about five percent of the cosmos.

Think about that ratio for a moment. Every grain of sand on Earth, every drop of ocean water, and every breath you take is made of normal atomic matter. So is our Sun, along with somewhere between one hundred billion and four hundred billion other stars in our galaxy alone. Even the glowing clouds of cosmic dust and the thin gas drifting between galaxies belong to this small family. Yet, when scientists calculate the total mass and energy of the cosmos, all ordinary matter adds up to just under five percent. The remaining ninety-five percent is invisible, silent, and largely unknown.

Here is an even stranger twist. Even inside that five percent, the stars we love to photograph are only a sliver. When astronomers add up all the starlight in the cosmos, they find that stars hold well under one percent of the total. Most ordinary matter is not in stars at all. It is thin, hot gas drifting in and around galaxies, far too faint to see with the naked eye. The glittering night sky, the sight that inspired every poem and every myth, is a thin layer of glitter on a much deeper and much darker ocean.

How did we realize that most of reality was missing? Nobody has ever seen dark matter or dark energy. We found them the way a detective finds a hidden culprit: not by seeing the culprit, but by noticing what it does to everything around it. The first clue arrived in 1933, when the astronomer Fritz Zwicky studied the Coma Cluster, a huge swarm of galaxies bound together by gravity. The galaxies were moving so fast that the visible matter in the cluster could not possibly hold them together.

Zwicky concluded that the cluster must contain far more mass than anyone could see, and he called the missing ingredient dunkle Materie, which is German for dark matter. For decades, his idea was largely set aside. Science often moves like this. A strange measurement sits quietly in the records until a second, independent clue arrives, and then a third. The second clue came from a much more familiar place: the slowly spinning pinwheels we call spiral galaxies.

According to the established laws of physics, stars near the outer rim of a galaxy should move much slower than stars near the crowded center. It works just like our solar system, where distant planets drift lazily while inner worlds race around the Sun. Mercury speeds along at roughly forty-seven kilometers per second, while distant Neptune plods along at less than six. If a galaxy were held together only by the matter we can see, its outskirts should behave the same way.

Think of a child swinging a ball on a string. The faster the ball spins, the harder the string has to pull to keep it from flying away. In a galaxy, gravity is the string. A star moving at a certain speed needs a certain amount of pull to stay on its curved path. If the pull is too weak, the star flies off in a straight line. Astronomers can calculate the pull from the visible matter, so they know exactly how fast the stars at the rim should be able to go.

Our own galaxy is a good example. The Sun carries us around the center of the Milky Way at more than two hundred kilometers per second, and one lap takes more than two hundred million years. Compare that with the planets. Mercury moves at about forty-seven kilometers per second. Neptune crawls at about five. In a solar system, the farther out you go, the slower the orbit. That is the rule that scientists expected every galaxy to follow.

Then, beginning around 1970, the astronomer Vera Rubin and her colleague Kent Ford measured how fast stars and glowing gas orbit in the Andromeda galaxy, and later in many other spiral galaxies. When they measured the actual speed of the outer stars, they encountered a profound shock. The outer stars were not slowing down. They were moving astonishingly fast, and their speeds stayed almost flat all the way to the edge of the visible disk.

Astronomers are skeptics by training, so the result was tested again. Radio telescopes tracked clouds of hydrogen gas that reach far beyond the visible edge of a galaxy, and the speeds out there stayed high as well. Different instruments, different teams, and different galaxies kept returning the same answer. The pattern was no accident and no measurement error. Whatever was holding those galaxies together extended well beyond the glowing stars.

In fact, these stars were moving so rapidly that the visible gravity of the galaxy could not possibly hold them. By all ordinary logic, these swirling galaxies should have torn themselves apart and flung their stars into the dark void long ago. Yet, they remain beautifully intact. Scientists suspect that an immense, invisible halo of mysterious mass surrounds each galaxy, providing the extra gravitational glue needed to hold everything together. Because this substance emits no light, absorbs no light, and reflects no light, we simply call it dark matter.

The word dark can be misleading. Dark matter is not a black cloud or a shadow. It is more like perfectly clear glass. Light passes straight through it, so it is invisible to every kind of telescope, whether it collects radio waves, visible light, or X-rays. The only way it announces itself is through gravity. In that sense, dark matter is not so much dark as transparent, and astronomers have learned to find it the way you might find a window, by watching what it does to the light behind it.

That is exactly what gravitational lensing does. Einstein's theory of general relativity says that heavy objects curve space, and light follows the curve. When astronomers look at massive clusters of galaxies, the light from galaxies far behind them is stretched into glowing arcs, as if seen through a thick lens. The bending is stronger than the visible matter can explain. The extra mass is clearly there. It simply does not shine.

One of the most striking pieces of evidence comes from the Bullet Cluster, where two huge galaxy clusters collided. In 2006, astronomers compared two maps. One showed the hot gas, which holds most of the ordinary matter in the clusters and glows in X-rays. The other showed where the mass is, measured through lensing. The gas had been slowed by the crash, but most of the mass had passed straight through. Many physicists consider this one of the strongest pieces of evidence that dark matter is real.

Let us line up the clues. In 1933, Fritz Zwicky found galaxies moving too fast inside the Coma Cluster. Around 1970, Vera Rubin and Kent Ford found spiral galaxies whose outer stars spin too fast. In 2006, the Bullet Cluster showed that most of the mass sits apart from the gas. Three different kinds of evidence, spread across three different decades, all pointing to one missing ingredient.

What could dark matter actually be? It accounts for roughly twenty-seven percent of the entire universe, dwarfing everything we know by more than five times. Put another way, of all the matter in the cosmos, more than eighty percent is dark. It is not made of protons, neutrons, or electrons. Astronomers also checked whether it could be ordinary but faint objects, such as dim stars or small black holes. Surveys searching for them found far too few to account for the missing mass.

Astronomers have even tried to weigh our own galaxy. Estimates of the Milky Way's total mass come out near one trillion times the mass of the Sun, though the uncertainty is large. The stars and gas we can see account for only a small part of that weight. By most estimates, the majority of our home galaxy's mass lies in a vast dark halo that reaches far beyond the bright spiral disk. We live on a glowing island that floats in a much larger, invisible sea.

If a cloud of dark matter passed through your living room right now, you would not see it, hear it, or feel it touch your skin. In fact, it probably is passing through right now. Estimates suggest that near our Sun, there is roughly a third of the mass of a single proton in every cubic centimeter of space. It is spread incredibly thin, and it interacts with our world almost entirely through gravity. Billions of particles could pass through your body every second without leaving a trace.

Leading hypotheses suggest it may consist of undiscovered subatomic particles that barely interact with normal matter at all. Physicists have names for the suspects, such as weakly interacting massive particles and axions. A small group of scientists proposes something different: that gravity itself behaves differently on galactic scales. That idea explains some galaxy measurements, but most cosmologists favor dark matter because it explains many different observations at once. But no one knows for sure.

It might sound as if we know nothing about dark matter, but physicists have learned a surprising amount by watching what it does. It appears to move slowly, which is why it is often called cold dark matter. It seems to barely collide with itself or with light. And it has stayed around for billions of years rather than decaying away. Those clues rule out many suspects, yet the exact identity of the particle remains an open question.

To catch a dark matter particle, scientists build detectors deep underground, where thick rock shields them from cosmic rays. One of them, called LUX-ZEPLIN, sits nearly one and a half kilometers down in a former gold mine in South Dakota. Its tanks of liquid xenon wait for a dark matter particle to bump into a single atom and produce a tiny flash of light. So far, no experiment has clearly detected one. Every empty result narrows the search, but the culprit stays hidden.

Dark matter may also have shaped the structure of the universe itself. Computer simulations suggest that after the Big Bang, dark matter clumped together first, building an enormous web of invisible filaments. Ordinary gas then fell into the thickest knots of that web and lit up as stars and galaxies. In this picture, every galaxy is a lantern hanging on a dark scaffold, and the bright cosmos we admire sits on a skeleton we cannot see.

Dark matter may also explain why galaxies exist at all. For the first few hundred thousand years, ordinary matter was tangled up with intense light, which kept it from clumping together. Dark matter feels no such push from light. It was free to start gathering almost immediately, and its growing gravity gave ordinary gas a head start when the universe finally became transparent. Without that invisible head start, scientists think galaxies might have formed much later, or not at all.

A third, independent clue comes from the afterglow of the Big Bang. About three hundred and eighty thousand years after the beginning, the universe became transparent, and that ancient light still reaches us today as faint microwaves. The European Space Agency's Planck satellite mapped tiny ripples in that light across the entire sky. The pattern of the ripples depends on how much ordinary matter, dark matter, and dark energy existed. Fitting the pattern leads to the same budget again.

As if a missing twenty-seven percent were not baffling enough, the universe had an even bigger surprise waiting for us. In 1929, Edwin Hubble showed that distant galaxies are rushing away from us, which means space itself is expanding. For decades, scientists assumed that gravity would gradually slow down this expansion that began with the Big Bang, the way gravity slows a ball thrown into the air. The only question was by how much.

In the 1990s, two teams of astronomers set out to measure that slowdown. Their tool was a special kind of exploding star, a Type Ia supernova, whose brightness is well known, so how dim it looks reveals how far away it is. When researchers observed distant exploding stars across cosmic time, they discovered the exact opposite of what they expected. The supernovae were fainter, and therefore farther away, than a slowing universe would allow. The leaders of the teams shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2011.

Why were those supernovae so useful? A Type Ia supernova typically happens when a white dwarf, the dense leftover core of a Sun-like star, gains enough material to reach a critical mass and explode. Because the trigger is so similar each time, these explosions reach nearly the same peak brightness once astronomers calibrate them. That makes them standard candles. Just as a streetlight of known brightness looks dimmer farther down the road, a fainter supernova means a greater distance.

The expansion of our universe is not slowing down. It is speeding up. Something is actively pushing the fabric of spacetime apart, overcoming the mutual gravitational pull of hundreds of billions of galaxies. Measurements suggest that the expansion switched from slowing to speeding up roughly six billion years ago, before our Sun and Earth had even formed. For the first half of cosmic history, gravity was winning. Today, something else is.

To explain this relentless cosmic acceleration, scientists introduced the concept of dark energy. Dark energy makes up roughly sixty-eight percent of everything that exists. Unlike matter, which clumps together into stars and planets, dark energy appears to be smoothly woven into the very structure of empty space itself. As the universe expands and creates more empty space, more of this mysterious energy emerges, accelerating the cosmic expansion even faster.

Why does dark energy rule the universe today, when matter once dominated? Think of a drop of ink in a bathtub that keeps growing. As space expands, matter spreads out and becomes thinner, so its gravity weakens. But if dark energy is a property of space itself, its density does not thin out. Long ago, matter was dense enough to win the tug of war. As the universe grew, matter thinned out, and the steady push of dark energy took over.

One leading idea has an old pedigree. In 1917, Albert Einstein added a term called the cosmological constant to his equations, representing a fixed amount of energy in empty space. Today, many physicists think something like it could be driving the acceleration. But there is a catch. When theorists use quantum physics to estimate how much energy empty space should hold, the answer disagrees with the measured value by dozens of orders of magnitude. It is one of the biggest mismatches between theory and observation in all of science.

And the story may not be finished. Recent maps of millions of galaxies from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, known as DESI, hint that dark energy might be changing slowly over time. The hint is intriguing, but it is not settled, and it will take more data to find out whether it is real. It represents the dominant force of our universe, yet its true nature remains one of the deepest mysteries in all of science.

Let us tally the cosmic budget. Planck's measurements of the early universe give about five percent for ordinary matter, about twenty-seven percent for dark matter, and about sixty-eight percent for dark energy. Add the dark parts together, and you get roughly ninety-five percent of everything. The exact decimals shift a little between analyses, which is why you will also see ninety-six. But the message is the same.

Put these pieces together, and the complete picture of our reality becomes astonishingly humbling. Normal matter makes up roughly five percent. Dark matter claims about twenty-seven percent. And dark energy commands an overwhelming sixty-eight percent. Together, dark matter and dark energy make up roughly ninety-five to ninety-six percent of the total cosmic pie. Everything in every textbook, every telescope image, and every living thing fits inside that thin slice.

Imagine walking into an enormous library and opening a book of one hundred pages. You can read only five of them. The other ninety-five pages are written in invisible ink. You know they are there, because the story makes no sense without them. You can even tell how heavy they are. But you cannot read a single word. That is where modern cosmology stands: confident about the shape of the story, and honest about the pages we cannot yet read.

We have been humbled like this before. Just a century ago, many astronomers believed that the Milky Way was the entire universe. Then, in 1924, Edwin Hubble found a pulsing star in the Andromeda nebula and showed that it lay far beyond our galaxy. Andromeda was not a cloud inside our home. It was another galaxy, and there were countless more. In a single discovery, the known universe grew by a factor too large to picture. The dark universe may be the next such leap.

New instruments are already hunting for clues. The European Space Agency's Euclid telescope, launched in 2023, is mapping the shapes of billions of galaxies to trace how dark matter and dark energy have changed across cosmic time. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile, named in honor of the astronomer who measured those spinning galaxies, repeatedly photographs the whole southern sky. Each new map tests whether our cosmic recipe holds up or needs rewriting.

The answer matters for the fate of the universe. If dark energy keeps behaving like a constant, then in the very distant future, far-off galaxies will slip beyond a horizon from which their light can never reach us. Astronomers of that remote era might see only our own nearby neighbors, and they might never guess that a vast cosmos of galaxies once filled the sky. We are lucky to live in an age when the evidence is still visible.

What happens next depends on what dark energy turns out to be. If it stays constant, the universe will keep expanding forever, growing colder and emptier. If it were to strengthen, some models allow a dramatic end called the Big Rip, in which even atoms could eventually be torn apart. If it were to weaken, the expansion could slow down someday. Right now, the evidence does not choose among these futures, and each one remains a possibility.

There is also something quietly beautiful about that five percent. Most of the atoms in your body heavier than hydrogen and helium were forged inside stars, or in the explosions and collisions of stars. The calcium in your bones and the iron in your blood are stardust. You are made of the rarest ingredient in the universe, and that rare ingredient has somehow learned to look up, measure the cosmos, and ask what the other ninety-five percent could be.

When you step outside tonight and look up at the night sky, take a quiet breath and look past the glowing pinpricks of light. The stars and galaxies are not the main show. They are merely tiny specks of bright foam floating on top of a vast, unseen ocean. We are conscious creatures made of a rare, luminous minority, striving to decipher the dark sea that cradles us.

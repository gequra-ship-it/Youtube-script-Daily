What if it rained
on another world,
but not a single
drop was water?
Imagine floating down
through an orange haze
toward a moon of
Saturn, with clouds of
liquid methane above you
and rivers carving valleys
below.
The temperature outside
is around minus one
hundred and eighty degrees
Celsius, cold enough to
turn water ice into rock.
This is Titan,
and NASA takes seriously
the question of whether
something could be alive
there.
If you stripped away
Saturn's rings and stared
solely at Titan,
you might mistake it
for an eerie, orange marble.
It is the only
moon in our entire
solar system wrapped in
a thick, dense atmosphere.
If you stood on
its frozen surface,
the air pressing against
your shoulders would be
about one and a
half times the pressure
at Earth's sea level.
Nitrogen dominates its
golden haze, just like
our own air.
Yet stepping out without
a specialized suit would
be deadly.
Ordinary water ice there
is as hard as granite.
How could such a
brutally cold world ever
harbor liquid lakes and
flowing rivers?
While water on Titan
is frozen solid,
hydrocarbons like methane
and ethane stay liquid
at that temperature.
Clouds of methane gather
in the hazy amber
sky, condense, and pour
down as alien rain.
These showers feed rivers
that carve winding valleys
across the terrain,
eventually spilling into
immense polar seas.
On Titan, a lake
is made of something
we would call a fuel.
The rain falls,
the rivers run,
and the seas fill,
all following the same
pattern as Earth's water
cycle, but with a
different substance playing
the role of water.
Picture it once more:
an orange haze,
methane clouds, rivers
carving valleys, and a
cold that turns water
ice into rock.
It sounds like science
fiction, yet it is
a real place in
our own solar system.
Which brings us to
the question of how
anyone knows all this.
For a long time,
nobody could see the
surface at all.

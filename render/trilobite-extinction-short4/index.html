What happens when an
entire ocean becomes too
acidic for the animals
that live in it?
About two hundred and
fifty-two million years
ago, planet Earth went
through the Great Dying,
the most severe extinction
in the known history of life.
Estimates say that between
roughly eighty and
ninety-six percent of marine
species disappeared.
It also brought down
the final curtain on
the trilobites.
The trouble began in
what is now Siberia,
where massive volcanic
eruptions flooded the
landscape with basalt.
Scientists think the magma
also burned through coal
and carbonate rocks,
releasing huge amounts
of carbon dioxide into
the air.
The climate warmed sharply,
and the oceans heated
up with it.
As that carbon dioxide
dissolved into the sea,
the water became more acidic.
For animals with shells
made of calcium carbonate,
that was a serious problem.
Building and repairing
a hard shell takes
the right chemistry in
the surrounding water.
It is like trying
to build a stone
house while the mortar
dissolves in the rain.
Trilobites relied on exactly
that kind of armor.
And there was a second blow.
Warm water holds less
oxygen than cold water.
As the seas heated
up, ocean circulation likely
slowed, and dead zones
with almost no oxygen
spread across the seafloor,
some perhaps filled with
poisonous hydrogen sulfide.
Trilobites were bottom
dwellers, and the mud
beneath them turned toxic.
Scientists have studied
this crisis in rocks
from many parts of
the world, and the
pattern repeats: a sudden
shift in the chemistry
of the sea,
followed by the loss
of animals that could
not adjust fast enough.
Researchers still debate
how much each factor
mattered, and that is
the honest state of
the science.
But the combination of
warming, acidity, and
suffocation was a crisis
that nothing with a
shell on the seafloor
could easily escape.
Picture one of the
last trilobites, curled
into its armor for
the final time in
a poisoned sea.

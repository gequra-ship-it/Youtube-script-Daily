Human civilization feels
ancient to us,
but what if I
told you that our
entire history is barely
a blink next to
the story of the trilobites?
Modern humans have lived
on Earth for roughly
three hundred thousand years.
Even if you count
all our toolmaking
ancestors, we have walked
the land for only
a few million.
The mighty dinosaurs,
famous for their endurance,
roamed the continents for
around one hundred and
sixty-five million years.
Yet trilobites outlived
them all.
They crawled across
prehistoric seafloors for
roughly two hundred and
seventy million years,
from the early Cambrian
period all the way
to the end of the Permian.
Think about that span
of time for a moment.
Trilobites lived through
the drifting and colliding
of continents.
They survived at least
two of the great
mass extinctions, including
an ice age that
locked up water in
glaciers and made sea
levels plummet worldwide.
Paleontologists have
described more than twenty
thousand species.
Some were only a
few millimeters long.
Others stretched past half
a meter, and the
biggest, Isotelus rex,
reached about seventy
centimeters.
They built eyes out
of crystal.
They learned to roll
themselves into armored balls.
They settled the seafloor
from cold seas to
warm tropical shallows,
and they left fossils
on every continent.
Their body plan was
simple but flexible:
a head shield,
a segmented middle,
and a tail,
with three lobes running
down the back.
That modular design could
be reshaped again and
again, which is one
reason the group could
fill so many roles
in the sea.
By almost any measure,
this was one of
the longest-lived and most
successful groups of animals
that ever existed.
We are only just
beginning to learn how
to measure our own
species against that record.
So how could a
creature survive that long,
through ice ages and
predator arms races,
and then vanish forever?

catastrophe that finally

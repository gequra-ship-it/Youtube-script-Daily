More than one hundred
and fifty people,
one small boat,
and an island with no water.
What do you do?
Those who did fought
through the churning surf.
When the sun rose
the next morning,
the survivors found
themselves standing on
a flat, barren sandbank.
More than one hundred
and twenty French crew
members, and around sixty
Malagasy men and women,
had made it to shore.
Relief quickly turned to
dread.
The island was utterly barren.
There was not a
single tree to give
shade from the equatorial sun.
There was no fruit,
no edible roots,
and, most frightening of
all, no stream of fresh water.
How long could anyone
last in an open-air
prison made of blinding
white sand?
Water was the first challenge.
Without it, death would
come within days.
The survivors began to
dig into the sand.
At first, the holes
filled with seawater.
But a well dug
deep enough reached a
layer of water that
was slightly salty,
yet drinkable.
On small sand islands,
rain can soak into
the ground and float
as a thin layer
above the heavier seawater
below.
That is the layer
they reached.
That single crude well
became their lifeline,
and it kept them alive.
With water secured,
the French crew began
planning their escape.
They salvaged timber,
sails, and iron nails
from the wreck that
washed ashore.
Within about two months,
they used these scraps
to build a small boat.
They named it the Providence.
The Malagasy survivors
helped where they could,
and they, too,
hoped to leave.
But the moment of
completion brought a cruel
revelation.
The boat was too
small to carry everyone.
Only the French sailors
would board.
The Malagasy survivors
were told they had
to remain behind.
The hardest decision of
the whole story was
still ahead.

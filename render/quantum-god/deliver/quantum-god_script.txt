===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Take a look at the clock on your wall. Tick, tick, tick, every second the same as the last. For thousands of years, many thinkers pictured the whole universe the same way: a great clockwork, wound up once and running by fixed rules. If the universe is a clock, it seems natural to ask who built it. So here is the question that has followed humanity through every age: does God exist? Today we will not shout an answer. We will do something stranger. We will walk down into the smallest scale of nature, where the clockwork starts to look very different, and see what quantum mechanics really says about order, cause, and design.

In eighteen fourteen, the French mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace described a thought experiment. Imagine an intellect that knew the position and motion of every particle in the universe, and all the forces acting on them. For such a mind, he wrote, nothing would be uncertain. The future would be as clear as the past. Later readers called this mind Laplace's demon. It is the purest form of determinism: every effect follows from an earlier cause, all the way back. And for many people, a universe that perfect seemed to point to a designer standing behind it all.

Long before Laplace, thinkers had turned that feeling into arguments. In the thirteenth century, Thomas Aquinas listed five ways of reasoning toward God in his Summa Theologica. Several of them lean on cause and design. Everything that moves is moved by something else, so there must be a first mover. Every effect has a cause, so there must be a first cause. And the orderly workings of nature suggest a mind that guides them. In sixteen eighty-seven, Isaac Newton published his laws of motion, and he himself believed that the beautiful order of the solar system came from an intelligent being. Keep these three arguments in mind. Quantum mechanics will test each one.

The first crack appeared in nineteen hundred. The German physicist Max Planck was trying to explain the glow of hot objects. The old physics gave nonsense answers. Planck found that the numbers worked only if energy comes in tiny separate packets, which later came to be called quanta. He did not like what this implied, and neither did many others. But the idea kept working. Over the next twenty-five years, a new theory was built from it, one that described atoms with astonishing accuracy. It also described a world that behaves in ways our everyday senses never prepare us for.

In October of nineteen twenty-seven, many of the greatest physicists on Earth met in Brussels for the Solvay Conference. Albert Einstein was there, uneasy about the new theory. Niels Bohr was there, defending it. Einstein had written to his friend Max Born the year before that quantum mechanics was impressive, but that an inner voice told him it was not yet the real thing. He was convinced, he wrote, that God does not throw dice. According to accounts passed down, Bohr answered that Einstein should stop telling God what to do. Whether or not those exact words were spoken, the argument was real. It was about whether nature is truly random.

The experiment at the heart of that argument is surprisingly simple. Fire tiny pieces of matter, electrons, at a wall with two narrow slits, and place a screen behind it. If electrons were little bullets, you would expect two stripes on the screen. But in nineteen sixty-one, the physicist Claus Jonsson sent electrons through slits and saw something else: a pattern of many light and dark bands, the signature of waves overlapping. Matter was behaving like a wave. And that raised an uncomfortable question. If each electron is a single thing, what exactly is it interfering with?

It gets stranger. In nineteen eighty-nine, a team in Japan led by Akira Tonomura sent electrons through the apparatus one at a time. Each electron landed on the screen as a single dot, like a particle. But as thousands of dots piled up, the striped wave pattern slowly appeared. Each electron arrived as one point, yet the pattern needed waves. In standard quantum theory, the electron is described by a wave of possibilities that spreads through both openings. Many physicists put it loosely by saying the electron goes through both slits. How to picture that is still debated. The pattern itself is a measured fact.

Now add a detector at the slits to find out which path each electron takes. The moment the experiment records that information, the striped pattern disappears, and two plain bands remain. Learning the path destroys the interference. Does this mean a human mind changes reality just by looking? That is the most popular misunderstanding in all of physics. What matters is not a person's attention. What matters is whether the path information gets recorded in the physical world. Even an automatic detector that nobody ever reads is enough to wash the pattern away.

This is why physicists use the word measurement, not mind. In real laboratories, the observer is a photon detector, a bit of gas, a speck of dust, a piece of glass. Nobody has found an experiment showing that consciousness plays a special role. This matters for our question, because some people hoped the observer was a doorway for God, an eternal watcher keeping the world solid. The experiments simply do not point that way. Reality at the small scale is strange, but it does not need a person, or a deity, to look at it before it behaves.

In nineteen twenty-seven, the German physicist Werner Heisenberg published the uncertainty principle, and the same year Earle Kennard gave it its exact mathematical form. It says that the position of a particle and its momentum cannot both be sharply defined at once. The more tightly you pin one down, the fuzzier the other becomes. Many people think this is only about clumsy instruments disturbing the particle. It goes deeper. In standard quantum theory, a particle simply does not have a perfectly sharp position and a perfectly sharp momentum together. The fuzziness belongs to nature itself, and it has been confirmed in countless experiments.

Now think about Laplace's demon again. It needed to know the exact position and momentum of every particle. If the uncertainty principle is built into nature, that perfect knowledge is not just hard, it is not available at all. What does that do to an all-knowing God? Thinkers answer in different ways. Some argue that an all-knowing being knows everything that can be known, and a value that does not exist is nothing to know. Others reject the whole comparison. And some physical theories add hidden details underneath. So this is a real philosophical debate. Physics does not settle it, but it does change what the debate is about.

Here is another place where nature seems to roll dice. Take an unstable atom, such as carbon-fourteen, the kind archaeologists use to date ancient bones. Physics can tell you the chance that it decays during the next hour with great precision. But it cannot tell you when that one atom will go. Two identical atoms sitting side by side can decay minutes or millions of years apart. In standard quantum theory, there is no hidden clock inside the nucleus and no trigger that can be found. The randomness appears to be real. And a chain of causes, one domino striking the next, no longer describes how the smallest things behave.

Look at how different the clocks of decay can be. The half-life is the time in which half of a large sample of atoms will have decayed. For radon-two-twenty-two, it is about three point eight days. For carbon-fourteen, it is about fifty-seven hundred thirty years. And for uranium-two-thirty-eight, it is about four point four seven billion years, close to the age of the Earth itself. Yet in every case, the half-life says nothing about any single atom. The group behaves with iron regularity. The individual atom keeps its own timing a secret, even from the equations.

Now for an honest correction. Quantum mechanics predicts its results magnificently, but physicists still disagree about what it means. Some readings keep true randomness. Others do not. In the de Broglie and Bohm version, particles have definite positions, guided by a wave, and the future is determined, though the guiding wave is nonlocal and the hidden details can never be seen. In the many-worlds version, nothing is random at all, and every outcome happens. So quantum theory does not force a universe of pure chance. What it does is end the simple, local, mechanical clockwork that Laplace imagined. That is a smaller claim than some headlines make, and a more accurate one.

Now the vacuum, the emptiest place there is. In quantum field theory, empty space is not a blank stage. It has a lowest energy state, and its fields are never perfectly still. In nineteen forty-seven, Willis Lamb and Robert Retherford measured a tiny shift in the energy levels of hydrogen that the old theory could not explain, and quantum theory could. In nineteen forty-eight, Hendrik Casimir predicted that two metal plates in a vacuum would feel a faint force, and in nineteen ninety-seven, Steve Lamoreaux measured it precisely. So the vacuum is a physical thing with real effects, not an absence.

Religions and philosophers have long said that nothing comes from nothing. How does a busy vacuum bear on this? Since the nineteen seventies, some physicists, such as Edward Tryon and Alexander Vilenkin, proposed that the universe could arise from a quantum fluctuation or a tunneling event. These are speculative ideas, not established facts. And the nothing in them is a quantum state with laws and fields, not the philosophical nothing, which critics point out. What physics honestly says is this: we do not know what, if anything, came before the Big Bang. Quantum theory gives us ways to ask the question. It has not yet given us the answer.

In nineteen thirty-five, Einstein, Boris Podolsky, and Nathan Rosen published a famous paper asking whether quantum mechanics could be a complete description of reality. They pointed to a strange case: two particles prepared together, then separated, whose measured properties stay linked no matter the distance. We now call this entanglement. Einstein disliked it. In a later letter, he called it spooky action at a distance. He suspected the particles carried hidden instructions from the start, like two sealed envelopes holding matching cards. For three decades, nobody could tell whether he was right. It looked like a debate that could never be settled by experiment.

Then, in nineteen sixty-four, the Northern Irish physicist John Stewart Bell found a way. He showed that any theory of local hidden instructions must obey a mathematical limit on how strongly the measurements can agree. Quantum theory predicted the limit would be broken. In the early nineteen seventies, John Clauser and Stuart Freedman ran the first test. In nineteen eighty-two, Alain Aspect closed more gaps. In twenty fifteen, three groups ran loophole-free tests. All of them found the limit broken, just as quantum theory predicted. In twenty twenty-two, Aspect, Clauser, and Anton Zeilinger shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for this work.

What did these experiments really rule out? Local realism: the idea that particles carry definite properties of their own, and that nothing can influence them faster than light. At least one of those two assumptions has to go. Some physicists drop definite properties before measurement. Others accept a kind of nonlocal connection, as in the Bohm picture. But here is the key point. Entanglement cannot be used to send a message faster than light, and Einstein's relativity survives. So the universe is more tightly connected than the clockwork picture allowed, but it is not lawless, and it is not telepathy.

If tiny things are so strange, why does a chair look solid? A big part of the answer is decoherence, worked out by H. Dieter Zeh in nineteen seventy and developed by Wojciech Zurek and others. When a quantum system interacts with its surroundings, with air molecules and photons, its delicate wave behavior leaks out into the environment, very fast. The result looks like ordinary, definite, classical behavior. No mind is needed, only countless blind collisions. A fair note: many physicists say decoherence explains why we do not see superpositions in daily life, but not, by itself, why one single outcome happens. That deeper question is still open.

This brings us back to the cosmic observer. The idea that things exist only while perceived is old. The eighteenth-century philosopher George Berkeley argued it, and said God's constant perception keeps the world in being. Some people now borrow quantum language to defend that picture. But the physics does not require it. Decoherence works the same in a laboratory, in a gas cloud, and in deep space where nobody is looking. So quantum mechanics gives no support to an observer-God that holds reality together. It also does not rule out the idea as philosophy. It simply makes the physical argument unnecessary.

Then there is the most famous animal in physics. In nineteen thirty-five, Erwin Schrodinger described a cat sealed in a box with a radioactive atom, a detector, and a vial of poison. If the atom decays, the cat dies. Quantum theory, applied naively, would describe the cat as both alive and dead until the box is opened. Schrodinger did not offer this as a fact. He offered it as a warning: a way to show how strange the theory sounds when it is stretched to everyday objects. No real cat is ever found half alive. Decoherence explains why the box acts like a measuring device almost instantly.

In nineteen fifty-seven, Hugh Everett the Third proposed a radical idea. What if the wave function never collapses at all? Instead, every possible outcome happens, each in its own branch of an ever-growing reality. This many-worlds view takes the equations at face value, and it has serious supporters. It also has serious critics, and no experiment can yet tell it apart from its rivals. So when someone says physics has proven there are endless copies of you, be careful. That is an interpretation, one among several. What is solid is the mathematics. What it means, philosophically and theologically, is still argued over.

In nineteen sixty-one, the physicist Eugene Wigner, who later won a Nobel Prize, posed a puzzle. A friend measures a quantum system inside a sealed laboratory. For the friend, there is one definite result. But for Wigner, outside, the whole lab might still be described as a superposition. In twenty nineteen, researchers in Scotland built a small version of this with entangled photons. Their results are consistent with the claim that different observers can disagree about facts, but only under certain assumptions, and physicists still argue about those assumptions. The honest summary is that nobody knows whether there is one objective story of the world.

Picture a casino with billions of roulette wheels. Each spin is unpredictable. Yet over trillions of spins, the totals become so regular that the casino can predict its profit to the dollar. Something like this happens in matter. Individual atoms behave probabilistically, but a chair contains an unimaginable number of them, so their average behavior is steady. That is part of why the large world looks like Laplace's clock. For the design argument, this is interesting. Stable order does not need every detail to be fixed in advance. Whether that order still points to a designer is a question physics cannot answer, but it does show the order is not fragile.

Quantum randomness may even have shaped the sky. The leading theory of the early universe, cosmic inflation, proposes that tiny quantum fluctuations were stretched to enormous size, and became the seeds of galaxies. In nineteen ninety-two, NASA's COBE satellite detected faint ripples in the afterglow of the Big Bang, differing by only about one part in one hundred thousand. John Mather and George Smoot received the Nobel Prize for this work in two thousand six. Inflation itself is not yet confirmed, and it remains a leading idea, not a certainty. But the ripples are real. The great web of galaxies seems to grow from the smallest, quietest wobbles.

Could quantum effects reach into biology? Per-Olov Lowdin suggested in nineteen sixty-three that a proton in DNA might tunnel between positions, causing a copying mistake. Recent computer models say this is plausible. But it is a hypothesis. We do not know how often it happens in living cells, if at all. What we do know is that most mutations come from ordinary sources: copying errors, chemical damage, and radiation. And evolution works through natural selection, which is not random at all, since it keeps what survives. So be careful with claims that evolution runs on quantum accidents. Perhaps a few contribute. The science is still open.

Many believers hoped quantum randomness would rescue free will. If the world is not fully determined, perhaps people can truly choose. But think about it. If a choice is decided by a random event, it is not chosen by you, it just happens. Free will would need something more than chance. Philosophers still disagree about this. Some argue that free will fits with determinism, and others that it needs indeterminism. And we have no good evidence that single quantum events steer our decisions at all. Quantum physics, then, neither saves free will nor destroys it. It simply does not settle the matter.

Now a warning. Since the nineteen seventies, many books and seminars have claimed that quantum physics proves the power of positive thinking, or that your thoughts shape physical reality. Experiments do not support those claims. The observer in physics is any physical system that records information, not a hopeful state of mind. Quantum entanglement does not prove cosmic love, and it cannot carry telepathy. This matters for our question as well. Bad arguments for God and bad arguments against God both borrow the same quantum language. The way to tell a good argument from a bad one is to ask what the experiment actually measured.

In nineteen seventy-four, Stephen Hawking showed that quantum effects near the edge of a black hole should make it glow faintly. This is now called Hawking radiation. The black hole slowly loses energy, and over unimaginable time, it should evaporate. For a black hole as heavy as the Sun, that would take far longer than the current age of the universe, about thirteen point eight billion years. Nobody has detected this radiation directly from a real black hole, so it remains a theoretical prediction. But it is a good example of quantum theory and gravity meeting, and it is one of the places where physicists are still hunting for the deeper truth.

What is the end of the story? The leading forecast, based on today's observations of accelerating expansion, is a very long, cold fade. Stars will form for a long while, then run out of fuel. The smallest stars, the red dwarfs, can burn for trillions of years. After that, only remnants will remain: dim embers, black holes, and thin, cold space. Scientists call this possibility the heat death of the universe. It is a prediction, not a certainty, and new discoveries could change it. Religions often promise a dramatic ending. The physics suggests a quiet one, and it asks us to find meaning in the short, bright time we have.

So, does quantum mechanics prove that God does not exist? No, and it is important to say so plainly. Science studies the natural world, using measurements and tests. It can challenge particular arguments, such as a perfectly predictable universe, a simple chain of causes, or a fragile design. It cannot rule out every idea of God, and physicists themselves hold a wide range of views. A famous story says that Laplace told Napoleon he had no need of that hypothesis. Even if the story is only an anecdote, it captures a real point. Physics explains how nature works without invoking God. Whether that is the end of the question is something physics leaves to you.

Let us return to where we began. You still have a clock on the wall, but now you know the world beneath it is far stranger than clockwork. Almost every atom in your body heavier than hydrogen was forged inside stars, long before the Earth existed. We are the universe, grown able to ask about itself. Tonight, look up at the stars and wonder. Subscribing and liking is what lets Miya's Lab keep asking the next strange question. Whatever you conclude about God, keep your curiosity honest, and keep it brave. The truth, as far as we can reach it, is deeper than any comfortable story.

Now, let us turn
our eyes from the
world of the infinitely
small to the world
of extreme speed.
Just as science created
the ultimate blade to
study the microscopic fabric
of life, it also
built humanity's most
astonishing guns.
But why would a
physicist or a planetary
scientist ever need a
gun in the first place?
What could researchers
possibly gain by shooting
things at mind-boggling
velocities?
To understand this,
you must look up
at the night sky.
Out in the cold
vacuum of orbit,
thousands of dead
satellites, spent rocket
boosters, and tiny fragments
of shattered metal are
circling our planet at
orbital velocities.
Even natural micrometeorites
drift through the solar
system at speeds ranging
from eleven to over
seventy kilometers per second.
When two objects collide
at those speeds,
normal everyday physics
completely breaks down.
Imagine standing on a
spacecraft.
A tiny fleck of
paint or a stray
screw floating in orbit
does not hit your
hull like a pebble
hitting a windshield.
Because kinetic energy
scales with the square
of velocity, doubling an
object's speed quadruples
its destructive power.
At orbital speeds,
even a fleck of
paint can chip a
spacecraft window.
To design shields that
can protect astronauts
on the International Space
Station, scientists needed
a way to recreate
these terrifying impacts
on Earth.
Ordinary firearms are
completely helpless here.
A standard rifle bullet
fired from a hunting
rifle or a military
weapon travels at roughly
eight hundred to one
thousand meters per second.
That might feel blazing
fast to our human
senses, but in the
realm of orbital mechanics,
it is a sluggish crawl.
The reason traditional
guns cannot shoot much
faster is rooted in
thermodynamics.
A bullet is propelled
by the expanding gas
of burning gunpowder,
and that expanding gas
cannot push the bullet
any faster than the
speed of sound within
the hot gas itself.
To shatter this sound
barrier, scientists invented
one of the most
imposing machines in
experimental ballistics:
the two-stage light-gas gun.
Stepping into a
hypervelocity impact
laboratory feels like
walking into the engine
room of a colossal submarine.
Stretching across the floor
is a massive,
steel-barreled contraption
that can measure tens
of meters in length,
secured to heavy concrete
foundations to absorb
violent recoils.

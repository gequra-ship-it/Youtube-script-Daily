Did you know that
every hospital scanner
uses the exact trick
you would need to
understand a fourth dimension?
When a patient lies
down inside a magnetic
resonance imaging scanner
or a computed tomography
machine, the medical
technology does something
truly remarkable.
It does not simply
snap a single photograph
of the internal body
all at once.
Instead, the machine builds
its picture from a
stack of razor-thin
two-dimensional digital
cross-sections, often
hundreds of them.
When a radiologist sits
down at their computer
screen, they scroll smoothly
through these flat
black-and-white images.
On the monitor,
vital organs appear out
of empty darkness,
expand into wide detailed
cross-sections, shift their
contours, and vanish as
the scan moves deeper.
A single flat image
looks like an abstract
puzzle of gray shapes.
Yet by mentally stacking
hundreds of these flat
planes together in sequence,
the physician reconstructs
the full three-dimensional
reality of human flesh
and bone.
Doctors do this every
day, scrolling through
slice after slice of
a single knee until
a hidden injury appears
somewhere in the stack.
In that clinical moment,
the doctor is acting
exactly like a
higher-dimensional observer.
They are taking
lower-dimensional fragments
and integrating them into
a higher physical truth.
Now pause and consider
what this implies about
our own cosmic surroundings.
If human engineers can
map a three-dimensional
organ by studying flat
two-dimensional slices,
could our entire universe
be a single
three-dimensional slice
of a grander structure?
Nothing proves that it
is, but the idea
shows how a hidden
dimension could sit in
plain sight.
What feels like total
reality to our senses
might simply be the
page of a book
we have not learned
how to turn.
Every scan in every
hospital is a small
reminder that flat slices
can reveal a hidden whole.

Why does the idea
of eating human flesh
turn your stomach before
you even think about it?
Biology may have part
of the answer.
Many researchers think
disgust is not just a mood.
They describe it as
a kind of behavioral
immune system.
It pushes you away
from things that carry
disease: rotten food,
waste, sickness.
Brain imaging studies have
found that a region
called the insula becomes
active when people feel
disgust.
So that shudder may
be doing a job.
Here is a thought
experiment, and to be
clear, it is an
imagined scenario, not
a record of real people.
Picture two groups of
early humans, hundreds
of thousands of years ago.
Group A has no
strong aversion to eating
their dead.
Group B feels an
overwhelming revulsion.
Over many generations,
which group would you
expect to suffer more
from prion disease and
infection?
In this story,
Group A is hit
harder, and Group B
stays healthier.
That difference could,
over time, favor a
stronger aversion.
There is another benefit.
A group that refuses
to treat its members
as food can build
deeper trust.
People who see one
another as allies,
not emergency rations,
can hunt together and
raise children together.
Disgust, empathy, and taboo
may have supported each other.
We cannot prove that
sequence, and scientists
still debate it.
Genetics adds one more clue.
In 2003, a team
led by the researcher
Simon Mead studied a
gene called PRNP,
which makes the prion protein.
At position 129,
people carry one of
two versions.
Those with one of
each, called heterozygotes,
seemed better protected.
Among Fore women who
had survived kuru,
most were heterozygous.
The team then looked
at populations around the
world.
They found that both
versions are common in
many regions, and they
argued that this balance
may reflect natural
selection, perhaps from
deadly kuru-like epidemics
in the distant past.
That is an interpretation,
not proof, and later
researchers have challenged
parts of the statistics.
So treat it as
a fascinating possibility.
The taboo is also
cultural, and cultures differ.
But beneath the stories
we tell, there may
be a quiet biological logic.
Disgust, empathy, and taboo
may all have the
same old story behind them.

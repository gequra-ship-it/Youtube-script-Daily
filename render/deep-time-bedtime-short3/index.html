541 MILLION YEARS AGO
TENS OF MILLIONS OF YEARS
For billions of years,
life stayed small and quiet.
Then, approximately 541
million years ago,
the quiet shattered,
marking the beginning of
the Paleozoic Era and
the legendary Cambrian Period.
What sparked this sudden
explosion of evolutionary
creativity?
In the span of
tens of millions of
years, nearly every major
animal body plan known
today burst into existence
beneath the waves.
Animals grew protective
mineralized shells, sharp
exoskeletons, and the first
complex compound eyes to
scan the murky waters.
The ocean floor became
a competitive arena of
hunters and prey,
filled with bizarre
creatures that look like
they fell from another galaxy.
Among them was Anomalocaris,
a formidable predator with
sweeping grasping appendages
and circular mouthparts,
gliding gracefully above
the seafloor.
Meanwhile, small trilobites
scuttled across the ocean
mud, rolling into tight
balls whenever shadows
passed overhead.
Nearby swam a tiny,
translucent creature called
Pikaia, with a stiff
rod along its back.
Scientists see it as
an early relative of
animals with backbones,
including you.
As the Cambrian slipped
into the Ordovician and
Silurian periods, life
began to test the
boundaries of its watery
cradle.
Primitive jawless fish
navigated vast coral-like
reefs, while sea scorpions
grew to immense lengths
in the shallows.
Yet the continents remained
barren deserts of gray
rock, silent and stark
under the raw sun.
Then, slowly, microscopic
plant spores and primitive
moss-like bryophytes began
clinging to damp riverbeds
along the shoreline.
They were among the
first green scouts to
gain a foothold upon
dry land, helping to
build the planet's earliest
soils.
By the Devonian Period,
often called the Age
of Fishes, the oceans
teemed with heavily armored
terrors like Dunkleosteus,
a prehistoric giant with
bony shearing plates for
teeth.
But the real drama
was unfolding at the
muddy margins of freshwater
lakes.
Descendants of lobe-finned
fish began using their
muscular, jointed fins
to prop themselves up
in shallow, weed-choked
waters.
Over generations, creatures
like Tiktaalik evolved
sturdy fins with wrist-like
bones, strong enough to
lift their heads out
of the water and
gulp the fresh open air.
Life was about to
leave the water.

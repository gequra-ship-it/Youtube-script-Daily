Imagine a shark as
long as a city bus.
In movies, Megalodon bites
cruise ships in half.
The real animal needs
no exaggeration, because
the scale of this
ancient giant is shocking
enough.
A modern great white
shark rarely grows much
longer than six meters,
so beside Megalodon it
would look small,
reaching only about a
third of that length.
Picture the two swimming
side by side:
one the size of
a large car,
the other the size
of a bus.
Scientists estimate that
Megalodon reached fifteen
meters or more,
and may have weighed
as much as fifty metric tons.
A single tooth could
be about as long
as an adult human
hand, shaped like a
triangle, with fine
serrations along both edges.
Hundreds of them lined
its jaws.
How do we know all this?
Here is the strange part.
Sharks have skeletons made
of cartilage, not bone,
and cartilage rots away
after death.
So there is no
complete Megalodon skeleton
in any museum.
Nearly everything we know
comes from fossil teeth
and a few rare
fossilized vertebrae.
Scientists measure those
teeth, compare them with
the teeth of living
sharks, and estimate the
size of the animal
that owned them.
That is why you
will always hear the
word estimate.
Studies of its jaws
suggest a bite force
among the strongest ever
estimated for any animal,
enough to crush the
bones of large prey.
And it did not
live in one small
corner of the sea.
Its teeth have been
found in rocks on
many continents, from warm
coastal waters of the
Miocene epoch to the
early Pliocene.
Megalodon patrolled the
world's oceans for more
than fifteen million years.
Now think about that.
An animal this large,
this powerful, with this
many millions of years
of success, simply
disappeared.
If size and strength
were enough to guarantee
survival, Megalodon would
still be out there.
So what went wrong?
The answer is not
a single catastrophe.
It was a slow
chain of changes across
the whole planet,
and it is the

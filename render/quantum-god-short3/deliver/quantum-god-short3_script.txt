===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Does God play dice with the universe? Einstein famously said no. A century later, quantum physics gives a careful answer that is more surprising than a simple yes or no, and it starts with a single atom that decays for no reason anyone can find.

Einstein wrote to his friend Max Born in nineteen twenty-six that quantum mechanics was impressive, but that an inner voice told him it was not yet the real thing. He was convinced, he wrote, that God does not throw dice.

According to accounts passed down, Niels Bohr answered that Einstein should stop telling God what to do. Whether or not those exact words were spoken, the argument was real. It was about whether nature is truly random.

Take an unstable atom such as carbon-fourteen. Physics can tell you the chance that it decays during the next hour with great precision. But it cannot tell you when that one atom will go. In standard quantum theory, there is no hidden clock inside the nucleus. Two identical atoms sitting side by side can decay minutes or millions of years apart.

Now an honest correction. Physicists still disagree about what quantum mechanics means. In the Bohm version, particles have definite positions and the future is determined. In the many-worlds version, nothing is random, and every outcome happens.

Even with random atoms, a chair stays solid. Individual atoms behave probabilistically, but a chair contains an unimaginable number of them, so their average behavior is steady. Stable order does not need every detail fixed in advance.

So does quantum mechanics prove that God does not exist? No. Science can challenge particular arguments, such as a perfectly predictable universe. It cannot rule out every idea of God, and physicists themselves hold a wide range of views. A famous story says Laplace told Napoleon he had no need of that hypothesis. Even if it is only an anecdote, it captures a real point: physics explains how nature works without invoking God.

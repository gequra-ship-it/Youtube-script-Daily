Nothing is never really
nothing.
Here is what physics
says about empty space
and chance.
Consider the radioactive
decay of an unstable
atom, such as
carbon-fourteen or
uranium-two-thirty-eight.
You can calculate the
half-life of a vast
collection of such atoms
with extraordinary
statistical accuracy.
You know that within
a specific timeframe,
half of them will decay.
But if you ask:
Why did this specific
atom decay at this
exact microsecond while
its neighbor remained
completely stable?
Standard quantum mechanics
gives a shocking answer:
There is no reason.
There is no internal
clock ticking down,
and no hidden cause
that anyone has ever found.
The timing appears to
be fundamentally random.
Some physicists explore
theories with hidden
variables, but experiments
have not revealed any.
Perhaps the most staggering
discovery of modern quantum
field theory is that
the vacuum of empty
space is not actually empty.
If you strip away
every star, every atom,
and every photon of
radiation, the remaining
void still seethes with
activity.
In a common popular
picture, based on the
uncertainty relation between
energy and time,
particles borrow energy
from nothingness, pop into
existence for a tiny
fraction of a second,
and vanish again.
These are called quantum
fluctuations, and they
are not speculative fairy
tales.
Their effects are measured
in precision laboratories
through phenomena like
the Casimir effect and
the Lamb shift.
As if random decay
were not unsettling enough,
the role of the
observer plunges physics
into an even deeper puzzle.
In the standard Copenhagen
interpretation of quantum
mechanics, subatomic systems
exist in a blend
of overlapping possibilities
called a superposition.
An electron is spinning
up and down at
the same time,
until a measurement forces
it into a single
definite result.
This raises a paradox
that haunted early quantum
pioneers: Does the universe
exist when no one is watching?
If reality requires
measurement to become
definite, what was measuring
the cosmos during the
billions of years before
life emerged?
These are open puzzles,
and physicists still argue
about them.

What kills you is
not the black hole
itself, but the difference
in pull across your body.
The size of the
black hole makes all
the difference between
peaceful wonder and instant
destruction.
Fall toward a stellar-mass
black hole, an object
only a few times
heavier than our Sun,
and your body is
destroyed before you even
reach the boundary.
Why does mass matter so much?
The answer lies in
tidal forces, the very
same physics that creates
ocean tides on our planet.
Gravity weakens with
distance, which means that
any object falling
feet-first feels a stronger
gravitational pull at its
feet than at its head.
On Earth, this difference
across the height of
a human body is
so microscopic that our
senses cannot detect it.
But near a small,
stellar-mass black hole,
the event horizon is
tiny, perhaps only thirty
kilometers across.
Because the center is
so extraordinarily close
to the surface,
the steepness of the
gravitational slope across
just two meters of
space becomes catastrophic.
If you fell into
a small black hole,
the difference between
the pull on your
boots and the pull
on your helmet would
be millions of times
Earth's gravity.
At the exact same
time, the gravitational
field lines would converge
inward toward the center,
squeezing your shoulders
tightly together.
Physicists have a
wonderfully descriptive,
almost humorous term for
this horrifying process:
spaghettification.
Your body would be
stretched into a thin,
fragile thread of matter,
pulled apart cell by
cell, long before your
boots touched the horizon.
Now swap in a
supermassive black hole.
At its horizon the
ride is gentle.
But as you draw
near the central
singularity, the
gravitational field lines
converge with absolute
brutality.
The tidal forces that
were harmless at the
horizon grow rapidly
stronger with every
millimeter you fall.
First, you feel a
faint, persistent tension
stretching your spine,
as if someone were
pulling your boots while
holding your shoulders.
Within fractions of a
second, that gentle tug
turns into an irresistible,
tearing force.
The difference in
gravitational acceleration
between the top of
your head and the
soles of your feet
spikes into thousands of
gravities, then millions.
Your spacesuit is shredded,
the bonds between your
molecules are pulled apart,
and finally, the very
atoms of your body
are stretched into long,
thin ribbons of matter.

Now picture the tower's
weight as a river
that needs a channel.
Engineers call that channel
the load path,
and a great load
path never has a gap.
Now, how do you
carry all that weight
up through 828 meters
of mostly empty space?
Engineers call the route
the load path.
Every kilogram, from a
chair on a high
floor to the marble
in a bathroom,
needs an unbroken path
straight down to the
foundation.
At the heart of
the tower stands its
spine, a hexagonal core
of reinforced concrete
running up through the middle.
In fact, the tower
is built of high-performance
concrete from the foundation
all the way up
to level 156.
Designers chose concrete
for its mass,
stiffness and strength,
and for how well
it blocks sound between
floors.
But lifting it was
a challenge.
Baker described the core
as torsionally stiff,
like an axle.
That means it strongly
resists twisting.
But a lone spine,
however stiff, is still
a tall, thin stick.
In a strong wind
it would bend.
It needed help.
Crews pumped it up
through pipes to the
156th floor, a vertical
pumping record of 606 meters.
Thick, heavy concrete
resists every meter of
that climb, so the
mix had to be
tested until it kept
flowing under enormous
pressure.
The help comes from
the tower's famous shape.
Look down on it
from above and you
see a letter Y:
a central hexagonal core,
with three wings spreading
out around it.
Baker's team called this
a buttressed core.
Think of a Gothic cathedral.
Its tall walls would
lean outward if not
for the stone buttresses
pressed against them.
Baker used almost exactly
that comparison himself.
The three wings act
as stabilizers.
They brace the core,
and they brace one
another, so that they
resist the wind together.
Pushing the structure
outward also widens the
tower's stance, and a
wide stance is what
keeps something tall from
tipping.
A thin vertical stick
has become a stable
tripod with a hollow heart.
The tower does not
need to be solid
to be strong.
It only needs its
material in the right places.

How long would our
fastest spacecraft need
to reach even the
nearest star?
Longer than all of
recorded human history.
To understand our absolute
imprisonment, we must first
look at the greatest
physical achievement of
human exploration: Voyager 1.
Launched in 1977,
this small metal probe
has been hurtling through
the icy vacuum at
blistering speeds of over
38,000 miles per hour
for nearly half a century.
It has traveled farther
than any physical object
created by human hands
in history.
Yet, on the cosmic
scale, it has barely
stepped off our front porch.
It has not even
fully left the outer
gravitational grip of our
sun, wandering through
an endless desert of
absolute nothingness.
At that pace,
it would need about
75,000 years just to
reach Proxima Centauri,
our nearest neighboring star.
Think about that timeframe
for a moment.
Written history is only
about five thousand years old.
And Proxima Centauri is
merely our next-door
neighbor, sitting just
4.2 light-years away,
in a galaxy spanning
over 100,000 light-years.
If getting to our
closest neighbor takes
that long, how could
we ever hope to
reach the edge of
the universe itself?
You might logically argue
that our current propulsion
technology is simply too
primitive.
Perhaps in the distant
future, we will build
engines that approach the
ultimate speed of light.
But here lies the
first brutal law of
physics dictated by Albert
Einstein: the speed of
light is not just
a speed; it is
the universal speed limit
of cause and effect.
Traveling at approximately
186,000 miles per second,
light is the absolute
fastest that any piece
of information or matter
can move through space.
Nothing with mass can
ever exceed it,
or even reach it.
As an object with
mass accelerates closer
to the speed of
light, an inescapable
physical nightmare occurs:
the energy needed to
make it go even
a little faster grows
without limit.
To reach the speed
of light itself,
you would need an
infinite amount of energy.
There is not enough
energy in all the
stars, galaxies, and black
holes in the entire
known universe combined
to accelerate a single
human starship to that
exact speed.
Physics itself slams the
door shut, locking us
behind the iron bars
of sub-luminal velocity
forever.
So the first wall
is a speed limit.
But it is not
the only wall,
and it is not
even the strangest one.

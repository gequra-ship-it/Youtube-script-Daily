Look under a damp
flowerpot in your garden
and you will find a pillbug.
It is barely the
size of a fingernail,
and when you touch
it, it curls into
a neat little ball.
Now imagine that same
kind of creature,
magnified dozens of times
over.
That is not a monster movie.
It is a real
animal, and it lives
in the deep ocean.
It is called the giant isopod.
Down on the muddy
seafloor, where no sunlight
has ever reached,
the giant isopod grows
up to about forty
centimeters long.
That is roughly the
size of a house cat.
It is pale and
armored, with segmented
plates along its back
and large, reflective eyes.
Seeing one for the
first time can be unsettling.
But it is not a hunter.
It is a scavenger.
It crawls slowly across
the sediment, searching
for whatever organic debris
drifts down from the
ocean above.
It moves with an
ancient, measured patience,
as if time itself
had slowed down around it.
Here is the strange part.
The place where it
lives has almost nothing
to eat.
No plants, no sunlight,
just a thin dust
of dead plankton that
biologists call marine snow.
So why would an
animal grow so big
where food is so rare?
Part of the answer
may be temperature.
The deep sea sits
between one and four
degrees Celsius, so chemical
reactions in the body
slow to a crawl.
Cells may divide at
a leisurely pace,
and some deep-sea animals
are thought to take
years to reach maturity.
With no deadline,
they simply keep growing.
Part of the answer
may be efficiency.
In biology, a large
body burns fuel more
efficiently than a small one.
A tiny shrew must
eat almost nonstop,
but a big,
slow animal can wait.
Captive giant isopods in
aquariums have been reported
to live for years
without taking a single
bite of food.
Their bodies may work
like living storage tanks.
When a meal finally
arrives, they gorge
themselves until they can
barely walk, and then
they quietly wait for
the next one.
So the next time
you find a pillbug
in your garden,
remember its enormous
cousin, resting in the
darkness miles below the
waves.
Scientists consider these
explanations hypotheses,
and no single one
covers every giant.

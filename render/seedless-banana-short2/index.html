Humanity has already lived
through one banana extinction.
The banana your grandparents
ate was not the
one you eat today.
This brings us to
the first shocking secret
hidden within your banana:
the banana you eat
today is not the
banana your grandparents ate.
In fact, humanity has
already experienced a
catastrophic, planet-wide
banana extinction once before.
During the first half
of the twentieth century,
the entire global market
was dominated by a
completely different variety
known as the Gros
Michel, affectionately
nicknamed "Big Mike." And
by all historical accounts,
it was vastly superior
to anything on our
shelves today.
Many who tasted it
say the Gros Michel
was sweeter and richer
than the fruit we know today.
It had a thick,
rugged skin that let
whole bunches survive long
ocean voyages on the stem.
Consumers in North America
and Europe fell in
love with its creamy flavor.
But the Gros Michel's
total reliance on
monoculture cloning proved
to be its fatal undoing.
A soil fungus called
Fusarium
oxysporum—specifically
Tropical Race 1—was first
reported in Panama in
the 1890s, and by
the 1920s it was
spreading through the
plantations of Central
America.
It attacked the plant
through its roots,
invading the vascular
tissues that carry water
and nutrients.
The disease became
infamously known worldwide
as the Panama Disease.
If a disease infects
an ecosystem with high
genetic diversity, some
individuals possess natural
mutations that allow them
to survive, stopping the
spread.
But on Gros Michel
plantations, every single
plant was a genetically
identical clone.
If a fungus possessed
the molecular key to
kill one plant,
it possessed the key
to kill them all.
The fungal spores could
persist invisibly in the
soil for decades,
and no fungicide could
clear them.
By the 1960s,
the Gros Michel had
been wiped out as
a commercial crop.
When every plant is
the same, one fungus
can end them all.

14 DEGREES
Did you know that
only about fourteen degrees
Celsius separates a living
body from the edge of death?
The universe around us
swings across enormous
extremes, from near absolute
zero in deep space
to millions of degrees
inside stars.
Even on Earth,
the recorded lows reach
about minus eighty-nine
degrees in Antarctica,
and the recorded highs
pass fifty-six degrees
in Death Valley.
Yet inside your chest,
your core temperature must
stay close to thirty-seven
degrees Celsius.
If it rises about
five degrees, to forty-two,
essential proteins begin
to fail and organs
start to shut down.
If it falls about
nine degrees, to
twenty-eight, the heart
can slip into a
deadly rhythm and the
risk of cardiac arrest
rises sharply.
Add those two gaps
together, and you get
a narrow window of
roughly fourteen degrees.
There are rare survivors
outside that window,
but it shows how
little room your body
really has.
Your brain guards that
margin around the clock.
It sweats, shivers,
widens and narrows blood
vessels, and pushes you
to seek shade or
warmth, all without you
thinking about it.
Think about what that
means for an ordinary day.
A fever of just
a few degrees makes
you shaky and miserable,
and a few degrees
of chill makes you
shiver uncontrollably.
Those everyday feelings
are warning signals from
the same narrow margin.
Because the margin is
so narrow, humans had
to build protection around
themselves.
We tamed fire long
ago, sewed clothing from
animal hides, and built
shelters.
Later we invented heating
and air conditioning.
Every coat, heater,
and cooling system is
a kind of extension
of the body.
Even a skyscraper in
a desert or a
house in a snowy
town is partly a
machine for holding your
internal temperature steady.
Think about the oddness
of this.
A species this sensitive
now lives from polar
ice to equatorial deserts.
We are fragile,
but that fragility pushed
us to invent.

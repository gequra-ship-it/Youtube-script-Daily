Imagine living in a
place where dinner arrives
once in a few
months, if you are lucky.
That is the deep
sea, and it is
where some of the
biggest invertebrates on
Earth live.
Here is how it works.
The deep ocean has
no kelp forests,
no blooming algae and
no sunlit reefs.
Nearly all the food
comes from the surface,
far above.
Bits of dead plankton,
shed scales and other
debris drift downward like
a gentle, endless dust.
Marine biologists call
it marine snow.
By the time marine
snow reaches the seabed,
most of its nutrition
has already been eaten
by animals on the way down.
What is left is
a scattering of crumbs
across a watery desert.
Once in a rare
while, a real feast arrives.
A whale dies,
and its carcass sinks
into the mud.
Scientists call this a
whale fall.
It is a sudden
banquet, but whale falls
can be scattered miles
apart and separated by
vast stretches of empty time.
So how does an
animal survive when meals
come months, or even
years, apart?
One surprising idea is
that it becomes big.
There is a rule
in biology called Kleiber's
law.
As an animal grows
larger, the energy it
needs per gram of
body tissue goes down.
A large body burns
fuel more efficiently than
a small one.
Think of a big
mug of coffee,
which stays hot much
longer than a tiny
espresso cup.
A small creature loses
energy constantly, so a
shrew has to eat
almost nonstop.
A large, slow animal
can simply wait.
Cold helps too.
The deep sea hovers
between one and four
degrees Celsius, and cold
slows metabolism dramatically.
The animals burn very
little energy while they wait.
Captive giant isopods in
aquariums have been reported
to survive for years
without taking a single bite.
Their big bodies may
hold reserves of fat
and nutrients.
When food finally appears,
they eat until they
can barely walk,
then settle back into
the long quiet.
Size may solve another
problem as well.
A tiny scavenger is
limited to a few
square inches of mud.
A giant isopod can
cover far more ground,
tasting the water for
drifting nutrients.
So perhaps the secret
of the deep is
not how fast you
can hunt, but how
slowly you can burn.
Scientists treat this as
a leading explanation,
not a closed case.

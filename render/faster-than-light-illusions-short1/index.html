Can a laser pointer
break the speed of light?
Imagine you are standing
on your rooftop on
a clear night,
looking up at the full Moon.
The Moon sits roughly
380,000 kilometers away
from where you are standing.
Now, imagine you hold
a laser pointer capable
of casting an intensely
bright red dot onto
the lunar surface.
If you hold your
hand steady, the light
takes about 1.3 seconds
to travel from your
roof to the dusty
craters of the Moon.
But what happens if
you suddenly flick your
wrist and sweep the
beam from one side
of the lunar disk
to the other?
If you sweep your
hand across an angle
of about half a
degree in a fraction
of a second,
that tiny red dot
will race across the
entire face of the Moon.
Because the Moon is
thousands of kilometers
wide, a swift turn
of your wrist causes
the dot to travel
across the lunar terrain
at speeds exceeding hundreds
of thousands of kilometers
per second.
If you performed this
same thought experiment
on a screen placed
millions of kilometers
away, the illuminated dot
would easily sweep across
the surface faster than
299,792 kilometers per second.
Has your laser pointer
just broken the ultimate
law of physics?
The truth is that
the red dot is
an optical illusion of motion.
The dot on the
left side of the
Moon and the dot
on the right side
are not the same
physical entity.
When you shine a
laser, you are not
waving a rigid stick
of light across space.
You are spraying an
endless stream of
independent photon bullets
outward from your device.
The photons hitting the
left side of the
Moon arrived from Earth,
and the photons hitting
the right side arrived
a moment later from
another burst.
Because no physical object
is traveling sideways across
the surface of the
Moon, no law of
physics is violated.
More importantly, no
information is traveling
from the first crater
to the second crater.
If an astronaut standing
at the first location
wanted to send a
message to an astronaut
at the second location
using that swept dot,
they could not do so.
The information traveled
strictly from your hand
to the Moon at
the speed of light.
The sideways movement of
the dot is merely
an illusion created by
your perspective.

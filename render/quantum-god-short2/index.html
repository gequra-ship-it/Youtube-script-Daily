Imagine two sealed
envelopes, each holding
a card, and the
cards always match no
matter how far apart
you open them.
Einstein thought nature
worked like that.
Then a physicist from
Northern Ireland found
a way to test
it, and the answer
surprised the world.
In nineteen thirty-five,
Einstein, Boris Podolsky,
and Nathan Rosen published
a famous paper asking
whether quantum mechanics
could be a complete
description of reality.
They pointed to two
particles prepared together
and then separated,
whose measured properties
stay linked no matter
the distance.
We now call this entanglement.
Einstein disliked it.
In a later letter,
he called it spooky
action at a distance.
He suspected the particles
carried hidden instructions
from the start,
like the matching cards
in sealed envelopes.
For three decades,
nobody could tell whether
he was right.
It looked like a
debate that could never
be settled by experiment.
The two pictures seemed
to give the same
predictions, so it felt
like a matter of taste.
Then, in nineteen
sixty-four, John Stewart
Bell found a way.
He showed that any
theory of local hidden
instructions must obey
a mathematical limit on
how strongly the
measurements can agree.
Quantum theory predicted
that limit would be broken.
John Clauser and Stuart
Freedman ran the first
test in the early
nineteen seventies.
In nineteen eighty-two,
Alain Aspect closed more gaps.
In twenty fifteen,
three groups ran
loophole-free tests.
All of them found
the limit broken,
just as quantum theory
predicted.
In twenty twenty-two,
Aspect, Clauser, and Anton
Zeilinger shared the Nobel
Prize in Physics for
this work.
Entanglement cannot be
used to send a
message faster than light,
so Einstein's relativity
survives.
At least one old
assumption has to go:
either particles do not
carry definite properties
before measurement, or
there is a kind
of nonlocal connection.
The universe is more
connected than a clockwork,
but it is not telepathy.
What does this mean
for arguments about cause
and design?

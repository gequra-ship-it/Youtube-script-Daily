What happens if we
look at a mountain
the way an engineer
looks at a skyscraper?
An architect does not
measure a building from
a street curb across
town; they measure from
the foundation up to
the spire.
Apply that same logic
to the mountains of
our world, and a
different contender takes
the crown.
We must leave the
freezing snows of the
Himalayas and cross
thousands of kilometers
of open water to
the islands of Hawaii.
Rising from the floor
of the North Pacific
Ocean is the massive
volcanic giant Mauna Kea.
To a traveler on
a sandy Hawaiian beach,
it looks like a
gentle, broad-shouldered
mountain rising into the
tropical clouds.
Its summit reaches 4,207
meters above sea level,
impressive, but modest
next to Everest.
It even hosts astronomical
observatories near its
peak, because the air
above it is remarkably
dry and clear.
But that visible summit
is only the tip
of an unimaginably massive
volcanic structure.
The true base of
Mauna Kea does not
sit on dry land.
It rests on the
deep ocean floor,
about six thousand meters
below the surface of
the Pacific.
If you could drain
the ocean, you would
see a monumental volcanic
tower rising from the abyss.
Measured from its underwater
base to its summit,
Mauna Kea rises roughly
10,210 meters.
That is well over
a thousand meters taller
than Mount Everest.
If you stood on
the seafloor at its
base and looked up,
you would face a
stone wall more than
ten kilometers high.
How did such a
colossal giant form without
a collision of continents?
While Everest was born
from a head-on crash
between two continents,
Mauna Kea was born
from a mantle plume,
often called a volcanic
hotspot.
Deep within the Earth,
a column of intense
heat melts rock and
punches through the oceanic
crust like a welder's torch.
Over millions of years,
fluid basaltic lava erupted
onto the ocean floor
and cooled in the
frigid water.
Layer after layer stacked
up, slowly building an
enormous shield volcano.

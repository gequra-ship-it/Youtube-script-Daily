Did you know the
ninth planet was found
by a young Kansas
farm boy with no
college degree?
Clyde Tombaugh grew up
on a farm,
and he built his
own telescopes out of
old farm machinery parts.
Then a hailstorm destroyed
his family's crops,
and there was no
money left for college.
So he mailed his
drawings of Jupiter and
Mars to Lowell Observatory
in Arizona.
The astronomers were
impressed, and in 1929
they hired him.
His job was mind-numbing.
Night after night,
in freezing temperatures,
he photographed tiny patches
of the dark sky,
each one more than
once, days apart.
Then he sat in
a dark room with
a machine called a
blink comparator.
It flipped back and
forth between two glass
photographs of the same patch.
Distant stars stood
completely still, but
anything that moved would
seem to jump.
Imagine hunting through
millions of microscopic
dots that way.
Lowell had spent years
searching for a mysterious
Planet X, but he
died in 1916,
fourteen years before anyone
found it.
Tombaugh was comparing
plates taken on January
23 and January 29,
1930, when on February
18, 1930, Tombaugh finally
saw one speck jump.
The discovery was announced
on March 13,
which would have been
Lowell's seventy-fifth
birthday, and the world
cheered.
An eleven-year-old girl
in England, Venetia Burney,
suggested the name Pluto,
after the Roman god
of the underworld.
And the first two
letters honor Percival
Lowell, the man who
started the search.
But early astronomers
believed Pluto was a
massive giant, perhaps
even larger than Earth.
Little did they know,
the supposed giant would
keep shrinking with every
new measurement.
Imagine finding a planet
with homemade tools and
a patient eye.
Tombaugh died in 1997,
and a small portion
of his ashes later
flew aboard NASA's New
Horizons spacecraft, past
the world he found.

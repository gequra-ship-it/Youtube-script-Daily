For decades, science
textbooks taught a simple
rule: bacteria are large
and alive, while viruses
are unimaginably tiny and
simple.
But in the early
two thousands, researchers
made a discovery that
shattered this rule.
They found giant viruses,
and they were unlike
anything science had ever
seen.
One was found in
a water cooling tower.
Another was found in
sediments off the coast
of Chile.
Named Mimivirus and
Pandoravirus, these giants
were so massive that
they could be seen
under an ordinary light
microscope.
They were bigger than
some species of bacteria.
But their physical size
was not even the
most shocking revelation.
A typical virus carries
only a handful of
genes, sometimes fewer
than ten.
Mimivirus carries about
a thousand genes,
and Pandoravirus more than
two thousand.
Inside their genomes,
scientists found genes
for building proteins and
repairing DNA, machinery
that biology once assumed
belonged exclusively to
living cells.
For a moment,
imagine being the researcher
looking through the
microscope.
Something that was supposed
to be invisible was
sitting right there in
view, as large as
a small bacterium.
It forced biologists to
ask whether the old
line between viruses and
living cells was as
sharp as they had believed.
The two giants were
found in very different
places, which hints that
such viruses may be
widespread.
Since then, researchers
have gone looking for
more, and more have turned up.
This discovery breathed
new life into an
audacious hypothesis: what
if viruses were originally
fully functional,
independent cells billions
of years ago?
What if they were
ancient organisms that
adopted a parasitic
lifestyle and slowly
discarded their own
metabolic engines, shrinking
into biological minimalists
over eons?
Some scientists suspect
that giant viruses are
caught halfway through
this great regression.
Others think they are
master thieves that
collected genes from the
hosts they infected.
The debate is still open.

The drill pipe came
back up from twelve
thousand meters, and the
hole it left behind
started closing on its own.
Soviet engineers were ready
for hard, brittle granite,
but the planet had
prepared a different problem.
Down at twelve kilometers
beneath the Arctic tundra,
the temperature climbed
to one hundred eighty
degrees Celsius, nearly
double what the models
predicted.
Scientists had expected
roughly one hundred degrees.
The rock above pressed
down with about three
thousand times the pressure
of the air around us.
Under that heat and
the crushing weight,
the ancient stone stopped
acting like brittle rock.
Geologists reported that
it behaved more like
hot plastic or dense
modeling clay.
Every time the crew
winched the long drill
string back to the
surface to swap out
worn bits, the surrounding
rock tended to creep
inward and squeeze the
borehole narrower.
The crew had to
re-drill rock that had
crept back into place
just to reach the
old bottom again.
Imagine digging a tunnel
in a material that
heals itself behind you.
That was the Kola
crew's daily reality.
The drillers were not
just fighting friction.
They were fighting a
shifting planetary crust
that kept erasing their
progress.
And the heat was
only one of their problems.
In the autumn of
1984, five kilometers of
drill pipe snapped off
inside the hole.
The team backtracked to
seven thousand meters,
drilled a new branch,
and spent five years
working their way back down.
In 1989 they finally
reached twelve thousand
two hundred sixty-two meters.
That is deeper than
the Mariana Trench is deep.
It is still the
deepest artificial hole
ever drilled straight down,
and nothing since has
gone farther in a
vertical line.
But the bits wore
out fast in the
heat, and the team
could not push the
hole any deeper.
By 1992, with funding
gone and the heat
still unbeaten, the deepest
expedition in human history
stopped for good.

===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Long before you ever held a crayon, you may have already picked a favorite hand. The first hints show up in ultrasound scans of fetuses just ten weeks into pregnancy. So where does handedness come from? The answer turns out to be stranger than a single gene.

In ultrasound studies in Belfast, about eighty-five percent of fetuses at ten weeks moved their right arm more than their left. Later in pregnancy, about ninety-five percent were seen sucking their right thumb. The researchers then followed some of these children until they were ten to twelve years old. All sixty who had sucked their right thumb grew up right-handed. Of the fifteen who had sucked their left thumb, ten became left-handers.

For a long time, scientists assumed that hand preference starts in the brain. But a preference can be seen from around the eighth week of pregnancy, before the motor cortex is connected to the spinal cord. In 2017, a team at Ruhr University Bochum in Germany studied fetal spinal cords from the eighth to the twelfth week. Genes were more active on one side than the other, in the segments that control the arms and legs.

Some of the genes linked to left-handedness help build microtubules, tiny tubes that act like a scaffold inside our cells. Related cell machinery helps an early embryo tell its left side from its right, which is why your heart usually sits on the left and your liver on the right. Scientists suspect this same toolkit also plays a part in which hand we prefer.

So is there a left-handed gene? No. When both parents are left-handed, the chance that their child will be left-handed is only about twenty-six percent. Among identical twins, who share essentially the same DNA, roughly one pair in five disagrees. In 2019, Oxford researchers scanned about four hundred thousand genomes and found a handful of regions. None of them works like a switch. Each one only nudges the odds a little.

Twin studies suggest that genes explain only about a quarter of the differences in handedness. The rest comes from many small influences, some of them before we are born. Conditions around birth may play a role too, although most left-handers were born without any complications.

So the hand you write with may have been taking shape long before your first breath. Why only one in ten of us ends up left-handed is a longer story, and it is waiting on Miya's Lab.

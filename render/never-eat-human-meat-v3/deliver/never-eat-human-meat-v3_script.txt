===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Across human history, most societies have shared one rule: you do not eat other people. We usually treat it as a moral boundary. But some scientists argue that biology may have helped build it. Today we follow the evidence, from a mysterious disease in Papua New Guinea to the calories inside a human body.

Our story begins in the 1950s, in the eastern highlands of Papua New Guinea. The Fore people lived there, largely cut off from the outside world. When researchers arrived, they found a disease that kept killing people. Victims began to tremble, lost their balance, and slowly lost control of their bodies.

The Fore called it kuru, a word that means trembling. Outsiders sometimes called it the laughing sickness, because patients could burst into sudden, uncontrolled laughter. After the first symptoms, most patients died within about a year. By the end, they could no longer walk or swallow. In total, kuru killed roughly 2,700 people.

Doctors were baffled. Kuru did not behave like flu, cholera, or malaria. Patients had no fever, and there was no sign of the usual inflammation. It also struck unevenly. At its peak, kuru was eight to nine times more common among women and children than among men.

The answer lay in how the Fore mourned. When a relative died, the family did not bury the body. Instead, they ate it, as an act of grief and respect. Anthropologists call this mortuary cannibalism. For the Fore, it was a way of caring for the dead.

The body was shared by custom. Men usually ate the muscle. Women and children usually ate the brain and other organs. That detail matters, because the brain is where the infectious agent was most concentrated. A ritual of love had become a path for a deadly infection.

Under the microscope, the brains of kuru victims looked full of holes, like a sponge. Nerve cells had died, leaving empty spaces in the tissue. This pattern gives the whole family of diseases its name: transmissible spongiform encephalopathy, which means a contagious, sponge-like brain disease.

For decades, scientists believed every infection needed genetic material, DNA or RNA, to copy itself. Kuru broke that rule. The agent was unusually hard to destroy with heat, radiation, and disinfectants. In 1982, neurologist Stanley Prusiner proposed an infectious particle made of protein alone. He named it the prion.

Proteins work only when they fold into the right shape. A healthy brain makes a normal prion protein, called PrP-C. A misfolded version, called PrP-Sc, has a different shape. When it meets a normal copy, it can push that copy into the same wrong shape. It spreads without dividing, like a molecular zombie.

That starts a slow chain reaction. One misfolded protein converts another, which converts more. The misfolded proteins clump together, and the nerve cells around them die. The body has no good way to clear them away, so the damage keeps adding up until the brain can no longer work.

You might ask: could you just cook the meat? Unfortunately, no. Ordinary germs like Salmonella are killed by normal cooking heat. Prions are far tougher. They can survive standard sterilization, and in lab studies some infectivity remained even after heating to 600 degrees Celsius. Formalin does not reliably destroy them either.

Your immune system does not help much either. A prion is made from a protein your own body makes, just folded wrongly, so it does not look foreign. Researchers see no antibody response and no inflammation in these diseases. The infection can travel quietly toward the brain without setting off any alarm.

Then there is the waiting. Many infections show up within days. Kuru could stay silent for years, and in some cases for more than fifty years. Someone could be exposed as a child, feel perfectly healthy for decades, and still fall ill much later. Once symptoms begin, kuru has been fatal.

The world saw a similar story in the 1990s with mad cow disease. Cattle were fed meat-and-bone meal made from the remains of other cattle. The result was bovine spongiform encephalopathy, and it later passed to people as variant Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease. Feeding an animal its own species turned out to be a dangerous experiment.

Now to a different question. Did people ever eat each other simply for food? Archaeologist James Cole, at the University of Brighton, decided to count the calories. His 2017 study, published in Scientific Reports, used body composition data to estimate how much energy a human body actually contains.

The numbers are modest. An adult man holds roughly 126,000 calories in total. Only about 32,000 of those are in the muscle, the part that works like meat. The heart gives about 650 calories, and the brain and spinal cord about 2,700.

Compare that with other prey. Deer muscle holds about 163,000 calories, five times more than human muscle. A woolly rhinoceros offers about 1.26 million, and a woolly mammoth about 3.6 million. For hunters, a large animal was a far better meal than a person.

Hunting people was also dangerous. Humans use tools, plan ahead, and fight back. A hunt for another person risks injury and revenge, for a small calorie reward. This suggests that when people did eat people, food may not have been the main reason.

Biology adds another warning. Some pollutants, such as mercury and certain industrial chemicals, build up as they move up the food chain. Scientists call this biomagnification. Humans eat high on the food chain, so human tissue can carry these substances. How much would depend on the person.

Germs from other humans are already adapted to human bodies. The species barrier protects us from many animal germs, because their biology differs from ours. With human meat, that barrier is gone. How much risk depends on the pathogen, but the protection is missing.

Even our feelings may carry this lesson. Disgust is not just a social habit. Research links it to avoiding contamination and disease, and brain imaging studies connect it to a region called the insula. It is a fast, automatic signal that helps keep us away from things that could make us sick.

Here is a thought experiment, not a proven history. Imagine two ancient groups. In one, people tolerate eating their dead. In the other, people feel strong disgust at it. If prion disease spread in the first group, it would suffer more. Over many generations, avoidance could be favored by natural selection.

Some genetic evidence points the same way. In 2003, researchers studied the prion protein gene, called PRNP. People who carry two different versions at position 129 are more resistant to prion disease. The pattern of these versions around the world is consistent with ancient kuru-like epidemics.

In Papua New Guinea, the epidemic ended once the practice stopped. After an Australian administration ban, the Fore largely stopped eating the dead around 1960. Because of the long incubation period, people kept falling ill for decades. The last known death from kuru was in 2009.

The lessons reached modern hospitals. Because prions resist ordinary sterilization, health agencies set special rules for instruments used on high-risk tissue, including stronger chemical treatments and longer high-temperature cycles. In the past, prion disease has also spread through some medical routes, which is why those rules exist.

So what does this add up to? Cannibalism carries real biological risks: prion disease, no species barrier, and a poor calorie return. These facts do not explain every taboo, and cultures differ. But they suggest that the rule against eating people may rest on more than morality alone.

Next time you hear about cannibalism in a legend or a horror film, remember the quieter horror. It lives in a protein that folds the wrong way, in a disease that waits for decades, and in a ledger of calories that never added up. The evidence suggests our ancestors had good reasons to say: do not eat another person.

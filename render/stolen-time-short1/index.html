What if you spent
three hours on an
alien planet, and came
back to find that
more than twenty-three
years had passed on Earth?
Is that just a
wild fantasy, or is
it grounded in real,
peer-reviewed physics?
Imagine descending onto
an alien ocean world,
where colossal,
skyscraper-high tidal waves
sweep across a shallow
planetary sea.
You spend a mere
three hours walking through
its water, collecting
mineral samples.
But when your shuttle
climbs back into the
void, you discover an
agonizing truth.
On Earth, more than
twenty-three years have
vanished.
The answer is that
this extreme time distortion
is theoretically possible.
To make three hours
equal twenty-three Earth
years, the time-dilation
ratio must reach an
astronomical value: roughly
one hour on the
planet for every sixty-seven
thousand hours outside.
But would you feel
this slowing of time
inside your own body?
The answer is no.
General relativity tells
us that local time
always ticks at the
same rate, one second
per second, for any observer.
Think about the emotional
reality.
You could sit on
the wing of your
lander, open a ration
pack, and discuss your
plans for just one hour.
In that one hour,
about seven point six
years of Earth history
would march forward.
By the time you
finished your meal,
your spouse would have
mourned your absence,
and your infants would
have learned to speak,
started school, and grown
into adolescents without you.
Now imagine docking with
your mothership, waiting
in a stable high
orbit outside the time-warp
zone.
You expect a colleague
who watched your descent
just three hours ago.
Instead, the airlock opens
to reveal a person
with gray hair and
deep wrinkles, and eyes
filled with the exhaustion
of twenty-three lonely years.
This is the brutal
asymmetry of time travel
allowed by Einstein's physics.
Science fiction often
portrays time travel as
a two-way street.
But the real universe
permits only a one-way
ticket into the future.
By descending into the
gravitational shadow of
a supermassive black hole,
you have vaulted decades
forward on the cosmic
calendar.
But you can never go back.
So what kind of
world could do this,
and what black hole
would it need?

A frog is creeping
toward a tiny wiggling lure.
Everything seems to be
going the frog's way.
Watch what happens next.
The frog has turned
its whole body toward
the larva.
It has lowered its head.
It moves closer,
step by step,
and the larva keeps
waving its antennae and jaws.
Then the frog strikes.
A frog's tongue leaves
its mouth in a
fraction of a second,
far too fast to
follow with your eyes.
The tongue is wet and sticky.
It snaps back with
the prey attached,
and the meal is
gone before the insect
understands what happened.
For almost any insect,
this is the end.
But in the Epomis
experiments, the larva
usually avoids the tongue.
The authors write that
it almost always dodges
the strike.
So the sticky tip
lands on empty ground,
and the frog is
left open and close
to its target.
Then the larva does
something no ordinary insect
would dare.
It uses that moment
to grab on to
the amphibian's body with
its jaws.
Look closely at those jaws.
They are long,
curved, and sharp.
Once they sink in,
they are very hard
to pull out.
The frog shakes its head.
It kicks and scrapes
at its own skin.
But the larva is
tiny, and it is
holding on at a
place the frog cannot
easily reach.
The tongue, the frog's
best weapon, is now useless.
Sometimes the frog tries
another answer.
In seven of the
trials, the amphibian gulped
the larva down and
later spat it out, alive.
In one case,
a young toad kept
a larva in its
stomach for about two
hours before it came back up.
The larva then simply
attached itself to the
skin of the animal
that had swallowed it.
The team used 420
larvae and ran 382
encounters with five kinds
of amphibians.
In about seventy percent
of them, the larva
lured its victim before
attacking.
In the rest,
the meeting was accidental,
but it still ended
badly for the amphibian.
The authors report a
success rate of close
to one hundred percent
for the larvae.
One detail matters.
The amphibians were young,
only two to three
weeks after metamorphosis.
They were small,
but still much bigger
than the larvae.
And in that instant,
the hunter is no
longer the hunter.

If an explosion in
space produces no shockwave
and no sound,
does that mean you
could safely watch a
nuclear blast from a
spacecraft?
Absolutely not.
In some ways,
being near a nuclear
detonation in space is
more dangerous than on
Earth, because there is
nothing to stop its radiation.
On our planet,
the dense atmosphere acts
as a shield.
Within a few meters
of a blast,
the surrounding air absorbs
the first burst of
X-rays, converting them
into heat and a
mechanical shockwave.
But in the vacuum
of space, there is
nothing to stop that
initial fury.
Roughly seventy to eighty
percent of the bomb's
energy erupts as X-rays
and gamma rays.
These rays cross the
void at the speed
of light without losing
strength to air.
If your spacecraft is
anywhere in the line
of sight, this invisible
wave strikes your hull
almost instantly.
What happens next is
called blow-off impulse.
The thin outer layer
of your ship's metal
skin absorbs so much
energy in a fraction
of a second that
it vaporizes into gas.
That gas bursts outward,
and the recoil slams
a violent shock into
the hull beneath it.
You are never hit
by a blast wave of air.
Yet your ship can
be damaged by its
own vaporized metal.
The shock can crack
structures and damage
delicate electronics from
the inside.
Engineers who design
spacecraft take this threat
seriously.
Shielding, distance, and
hardened electronics all
change how much damage
a craft would suffer.
Real outcomes depend on
the weapon, the range,
and how well the
spacecraft is protected.
Think about how strange
that is.
On Earth, a blast
is mostly about pushing air.
In space, the weapon
has nothing to push,
so nearly all of
its harm travels as
light you cannot see,
and it arrives before
you could react.
How far away would
you need to be to survive?
The honest answer depends
on many details.

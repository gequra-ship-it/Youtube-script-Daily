Why were the giants
the first to fall?
To answer that,
you have to picture
the world after the fires.
The sky is gray
and thick with soot.
The sun is a
dim smear, if you
can see it at all.
For years the days
feel like twilight.
Scientists think soot from
burning forests, mixed
with sulfur released from
vaporized rock on the
seafloor, formed a thick
haze that blocked out the sun.
Models suggest the world
lived in a freezing
twilight for years.
With little sunlight
reaching the soil,
how could the plants endure?
Photosynthesis slowed or
stopped across land and
oceans, and the base
of the food chain shattered.
Now think about what
a giant needs.
A giant needs an
enormous amount of food
every day.
When the plants fail,
the plant eaters starve
first, and the hunters
that depended on them
soon follow.
Being huge, which had
been a triumph for
so long, became a trap.
It is a hard
lesson from deep time.
The very thing that
makes a species powerful
in good times can
make it fragile when
the world changes suddenly.
For the dinosaurs,
size had once been
a sign of success.
After the impact,
it meant needing more
food than a broken
world could give.
Now picture a small
animal in the same dark world.
A creature the size
of a rat,
living in a burrow,
can wait out the
worst of the cold.
It does not need much.
A few seeds,
some insects, or whatever
the ash left behind
can keep it alive.
Smaller bodies need less,
and that made all
the difference.
This was not a
victory of strength or
intelligence.
It was a matter
of who could get
by on very little
for a very long
dark stretch, and who
could not.
Scientists suspect this
is a big part
of why the biggest
land animals disappeared
while small ones survived.
That is what makes
the aftermath so striking.
The old rules of
success were turned upside
down, and the winners
were the ones nobody
would have bet on.

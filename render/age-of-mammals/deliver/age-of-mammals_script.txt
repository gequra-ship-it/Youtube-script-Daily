===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Take a quiet look at the palm of your hand. Feel the subtle warmth pulsing just beneath your skin. Where did this steady internal heat actually come from? Every heartbeat carries an unbroken memory of an ancient world. Sixty-six million years ago, our ancestors were hiding in the dirt. They were tiny creatures trembling beneath the feet of giants. Then, in a single blinding flash, the giants vanished forever. How did those fragile shadows rise to rule the entire planet? Welcome to Miya's Lab. Today, we journey through the grand history of the Cenozoic era.

The word Cenozoic comes from Greek words that mean new life. This era began sixty-six million years ago, and it never ended. You are living in it right now. But its story begins in the shadow of an older age, when dinosaurs ruled the land for well over a hundred and fifty million years.

Imagine standing in a silent forest during the late Cretaceous. The trees are towering conifers and broad flowering plants. Huge sauropods shake the earth with every heavy step. Serrated teeth gleam in the shadows beneath the dense ferns. Mammals already exist in this vibrant, dangerous world. Yet they are small, mostly nocturnal, and constantly cautious.

Mammals are older than you might expect. The first mammals appeared roughly two hundred million years ago, early in the age of dinosaurs. So for well over a hundred million years, our relatives lived in the shadows, and most of them stayed small.

Many look like modern shrews or tiny opossums. They scurry under fallen logs while the titans dominate daylight. Their warm fur protects them from the damp chill of night. They listen closely with exceptionally delicate middle ear bones. These sensitive ears once formed parts of an ancient reptile jaw. Over millions of years, those tiny bones drifted and specialized. Today, they are the hammer, the anvil, and the stirrup inside your own ear. In the dark, hearing every snapping twig means surviving another hour.

Now, back to that warmth in your palm. Like birds, mammals make their own body heat, while most reptiles rely on the sun. That inner furnace lets a small animal stay active through a cold night. But the furnace has a price. Mammals must eat often, and a coat of fur helps hold the heat inside.

Then comes the day that divides earthly time into two chapters. A massive asteroid strikes the shallow waters of modern Mexico. The impact unleashes an unimaginable surge of kinetic energy. Shockwaves tear across continents at terrifying speeds. Wildfires may ignite across vast forests. Soot and vaporized rock shoot straight into the upper atmosphere.

The asteroid was roughly ten kilometers wide. The crater it left lies buried beneath the Yucatan Peninsula and the sea off its coast, and it measures about one hundred eighty kilometers across. Around the world, geologists find a thin layer of clay rich in iridium, a metal that is rare in the Earth's crust but common in asteroids. That thin layer marks the moment the old world ended.

A thick, suffocating blanket of darkness envelops the entire globe. Sunlight cannot reach the forest floor for months. Without sunlight, green leaves wither and photosynthesis grinds to a halt. Herbivorous dinosaurs starve as their lush pastures disappear. Soon, the great carnivores collapse from a complete lack of food. Pterosaurs, giant sea reptiles like the mosasaurs, and the coiled ammonites vanish as well. Nearly three-quarters of all living species are wiped out.

Here are the numbers. The asteroid struck about sixty-six million years ago. It hit the shallow sea off what is now Mexico. And about three-quarters of all species disappeared. In a single stroke, the world changed.

Yet beneath the smoking ash, tiny heartbeats continue to thrum. Some small mammals survive deep inside cool underground burrows. Others may shelter along dark riverbanks, eating insects and decaying roots. Being small suddenly becomes the greatest superpower on Earth. You do not need tons of vegetation when your body is the size of a shrew. A few seeds and buried grubs are enough to sustain a family.

When the dust finally clears, the sky reveals a quiet world. After well over a hundred million years, the colossal rulers of the Mesozoic are gone. Only one group of dinosaurs survives into this new dawn. We still see them singing on our telephone wires today as birds. Aside from these feathered survivors, the land stands wide open. This dramatic boundary marks the birth of the Paleocene epoch. Today, there are more than ten thousand species of birds.

Nature suddenly possesses millions of empty ecological stages. Who will claim the silent plains and the regenerating forests? At first, giant flightless birds try to seize the crown. Massive terror birds stalk the open meadows with crushing beaks. Giant snakes slither through the warm tropical swamps. Yet mammals begin to experiment with form and size at dizzying speeds.

Within a few million years, their body sizes expand remarkably. Some lineages develop broad teeth for chewing tough new leaves. Others sharpen their claws and reshape their jaws for hunting.

Mother mammals possess an extraordinary secret advantage. They feed their newborn young with rich, nourishing milk. This means offspring do not depend immediately on foraging outside. Parents can shelter their vulnerable babies inside protected dens. Furthermore, many mammals develop advanced placentas inside their bodies. A placenta allows embryos to grow safely in a controlled environment. Marsupials, like kangaroos, give birth to tiny young that finish growing in a pouch. Even the platypus and the echidna, which lay eggs, still feed their young milk. Their young begin life far more developed and protected than a fragile egg allows.

Here is the map of time we are about to cross. The Paleocene began sixty-six million years ago and lasted until fifty-six million years ago. The Eocene followed, ending thirty-four million years ago. Then came the Oligocene, which ended twenty-three million years ago. That is forty-three million years in only three epochs.

The global climate soon climbs toward an astonishing thermal peak. About fifty-six million years ago, global temperatures jump by roughly five to eight degrees Celsius. Fossils show warm-climate forests and crocodile relatives living close to the poles. This steamy greenhouse world is known as the Eocene epoch. Dense, humid forests stretch far toward the north and south.

In these canopies, early primates leap between mossy branches. They develop grasping hands with flat nails instead of curved claws. Their eyes gradually shift forward to face the same direction. This forward gaze creates stereoscopic vision and precise depth perception. Can you accurately judge the distance to the next swaying branch? Those who miscalculate do not leave descendants behind.

Mammals even take to the sky. By about fifty-two million years ago, early bats are flying on wings made of skin stretched over long fingers. They are the only mammals that truly fly.

Meanwhile, along swampy coastlines, another strange transition begins. Certain four-legged land mammals wade into warm shallow bays. Perhaps they are hunting slow fish or escaping terrestrial predators. Over generations, their bodies grow sleeker and their legs shorten. Their nostrils gradually migrate toward the top of their skulls. Millions of years later, these clumsy paddlers become majestic whales.

Think about that breathtaking transformation for a quiet moment. The largest animal to ever swim the seas began as a walking creature. Life constantly reshapes old tools to conquer entirely new realms. Their closest living relatives are hippopotamuses. And fossils of an early relative, Pakicetus, found in what is now Pakistan, are about fifty million years old. It walked on four legs, yet its ear bones already resemble those of a whale. Later whales such as Basilosaurus grew to about eighteen meters long, yet still carried tiny hind legs.

Eventually, the prolonged tropical fever begins to break. Continents drift relentlessly across the shifting crust of the planet. Antarctica separates from South America and Australia. A fierce, freezing ocean current begins to circle the south pole. Cut off from equatorial warmth, Antarctica slowly freezes into ice. This thermal barrier cools the entire planet step by step.

The lush, continuous rainforests begin to shrink toward the equator. The Oligocene epoch arrives with cooler, significantly drier winds. In this drying world, dense jungle canopies break apart into open woodlands. Animals that relied on soft tropical fruits face sudden food shortages. Tougher shrubs and abrasive plants replace juicy jungle foliage. Mammals must evolve sturdier teeth or perish in the cold.

Some creatures grow truly colossal. Consider the massive indricotheres, ancient cousins of the rhinoceros. They tower meters into the air to browse the highest surviving leaves. They are among the largest land mammals to ever walk the Earth. Yet maintaining such enormous bulk requires vast amounts of vegetation. As the climate continues to cool, weather patterns grow unpredictable. Scientists estimate that the largest stood roughly five meters tall at the shoulder.

Now the map of time continues. The Miocene began twenty-three million years ago and ended five point three million years ago. The Pliocene came next, ending two point six million years ago. And the Pleistocene, the age of the great ice sheets, ended about eleven thousand seven hundred years ago. That is the long road from here to us.

Then comes the Miocene epoch, bringing a revolutionary botanical change. Across broad continents, a new kind of plant spreads. It is humble, resilient, and remarkably durable: true grass. Grass does not grow from the tip like an ordinary forest leaf. It grows from the base, right near the surface of the soil. If an animal bites off the top, the grass simply grows back. This simple evolutionary trick makes grasslands remarkably resilient.

However, grass hides a brutal defense mechanism against hungry herbivores. Its tissues contain microscopic particles of hard silica, essentially glass. Chewing grass is like chewing mouthfuls of fine, abrasive sandpaper. Herbivores with soft, short teeth quickly grind their enamel down. Starvation looms for any creature unprepared for this gritty diet. Natural selection begins forging new dental miracles.

Horses and antelopes evolve remarkably tall, high-crowned teeth. Layers of hard enamel and cementum wear down at different speeds. This uneven wear creates self-sharpening ridges ideal for grinding tough grass. Many scientists link these tall teeth to the gritty diet, though dust and soil on the leaves probably mattered too.

At the same time, open grasslands provide absolutely nowhere to hide. A predator can spot a grazing herd from miles across the flat horizon. Survival transforms into a deadly, high-speed arms race. Herbivores lengthen their lower limb bones to maximize running stride. They rise onto the tips of their toes, which become hooves. Side toes gradually shrink or fuse to form strong, springy levers. Look at the horse: its earliest ancestors were about the size of a small dog, with several toes on each foot.

Predators respond with longer legs, flexible spines, and stealthy hunting tactics. Cats and dogs diversify, refining the art of the sudden, explosive chase. The Earth is beginning to look strikingly familiar to our modern eyes. Majestic herds thunder across vast savannahs under sweeping skies.

Yet dynamic continental collisions are still rearranging the map. Africa gently collides with Eurasia, allowing spectacular animal exchanges. The ancestors of elephants began as animals roughly the size of a pig or a tapir. Now, elephants migrate into Asia and Europe, while carnivores invade Africa. Later, about three million years ago by most estimates, the Isthmus of Panama rises from the depths of the ocean. This slender strip of land connects North and South America.

Opossums and armadillos march boldly northward across the new bridge. Bears, cats, and camels travel southward into lush South American valleys. This Great American Biotic Interchange reshapes ecosystems across two continents. Meanwhile, the rise of Panama redirects major ocean currents worldwide. The Atlantic and Pacific oceans are severed, altering global weather patterns.

The world enters the Pliocene epoch, drifting toward an even colder regime. In East Africa, the drying climate creates dramatic ecological shifts. The great Rift Valley deepens, carving rugged cliffs and deep depressions. Vast continuous rainforests fracture into scattered patches of woodland and savannah. For arboreal primates, this landscape poses an existential crisis. The safe canopy pathways are disappearing season after season.

According to a leading hypothesis, there is only one way to reach the next patch of fruiting trees: cross open ground. Crossing the grass on all fours leaves you blind to stalking predators. A leopard can easily ambush an ape crawling through tall yellow stems. What happens if you stand upright on your hind legs? Suddenly, your eyes rise high above the concealing grass. You can spot a predator prowling far across the plain.

Some scientists suspect that standing upright also exposes less of your body to the scorching midday sun. Cool breezes can circulate freely around your heated torso. Most importantly of all, your two front hands are completely free. They no longer need to bear your weight during every awkward step. You can carry nourishing food back to vulnerable infants. You can pick up a heavy stone or a pointed wooden branch.

Somewhere between six and eight million years ago, by most estimates, the human lineage splits from the one that leads to chimpanzees. Bipedal walking is not merely a different posture; it opens a new path. Hominins take their first tentative steps across the sun-drenched savannah. Over time, their feet develop sturdy arches, and their big toes align forward. With free hands, they begin to explore the physical properties of their world. A broken river pebble reveals a sharp, glass-like edge. With that sharp edge, you can slice meat from an abandoned carcass.

In Tanzania, at a place called Laetoli, a trail of footprints more than three and a half million years old was pressed into volcanic ash. They were made by upright walkers. And in Ethiopia, a famous skeleton nicknamed Lucy, about three point two million years old, shows a body built for walking on two legs.

The oldest widely accepted stone tools, about two point six million years old, were found in Ethiopia. With them, you can smash tough long bones to extract rich, fatty bone marrow. This nutrient-dense marrow provides an explosion of concentrated calories. Few other animals can reach marrow locked inside dense, stony bone. Our ancestors find a way using brains, hands, and fractured stones. Over about three million years, the brains of this lineage roughly triple in volume.

Here is what a big brain costs. Your brain makes up only about two percent of your body weight. Yet it burns roughly twenty percent of your resting energy. And it holds about eighty-six billion nerve cells. A small organ with a large appetite.

As the Pliocene yields to the Pleistocene, the planet enters a deep freeze. Massive sheets of glacial ice advance from the polar north. Gigantic glaciers grind across continents, retreating and advancing in cyclic rhythms. Over the last million years, the ice advances and retreats in cycles of roughly one hundred thousand years. This is the famous Ice Age, a world of harsh extremes. At the peak, ice more than two kilometers thick covers much of what is now Canada. So much water is locked in ice that sea level falls by about one hundred twenty meters.

Woolly mammoths grow dense coats of shaggy fur and layers of insulating fat. Saber-toothed cats stalk heavy prey across wind-swept mammoth steppes. Giant ground sloths weighing several tonnes lumber through forests, stripping branches bare. Small populations of woolly mammoths survive on Wrangel Island until about four thousand years ago, when the pyramids of Egypt are already standing. In this brutally challenging landscape, early humans face ultimate tests of survival.

They do not possess thick fur coats to shield against howling blizzards. They do not have elongated ivory tusks or razor-sharp fangs for defense. Instead, they possess something far more adaptable than physical armor. They learn to harness the dancing flame of fire. Fire provides brilliant light inside pitch-black caves. It helps keep terrifying predators at a distance from the warm circle of embers.

Cooking over flames softens tough fibrous tubers and makes raw meat safer. Many scientists suspect cooked food is far easier to digest, freeing even more energy for developing minds. Around those crackling hearths, something profoundly new awakens on Earth. Humans gather together in the darkness, communicating through complex spoken language.

Our own species, Homo sapiens, appears in Africa roughly three hundred thousand years ago, and later spreads across the world. We are not alone at first. Neanderthals share Europe and western Asia with us for thousands of years, and many people today still carry a small amount of Neanderthal DNA.

They begin to anticipate tomorrow, plan for winter, and remember yesterday. They paint running bison and leaping horses onto dark limestone walls. Some of those paintings, like the ones in Chauvet Cave in France, are more than thirty thousand years old. They bury their dead with care, sometimes with red ochre. Empathy, abstract imagination, and cultural memory take root.

Then, around ten to twelve thousand years ago, the last great ice sheets retreat. The climate stabilizes into a gentler, more stable warmth. Across multiple fertile river valleys, humans may notice a curious phenomenon. Discarded seeds near seasonal campsites sprout into familiar edible plants. Instead of endlessly following migrating herds, why not nurture those seeds? Agriculture begins, and with it, wandering bands settle down into villages.

In the Middle East, people farm wheat and barley, and in China, rice. Permanent walls rise from sun-baked mud and woven reed mats. Surplus grain is stored in clay jars, guarding against unexpected famine. For the first time, not everyone must spend daylight hunting. Some people mold clay pots, others observe the cyclical motion of stars. Towns expand into bustling cities, and cities form organized civilizations.

In Mesopotamia, more than five thousand years ago, writing emerges as small incisions on soft clay tablets to count sheep and grain. Recorded history begins, a mere blink against the vast backdrop of deep time. Think back to that dark morning sixty-six million years ago. The world seemed entirely finished when the great asteroid struck. Smoke smothered the sun, and the undisputed masters of the planet fell.

Yet life did not wave a white flag of defeat. Fragile, shivering mammals stepped out from damp underground burrows. They did not know that their distant lineage would cross stormy oceans. They did not know their descendants would run across grassy plains as wild horses. They could never imagine that their offspring would build telescopes to study stars.

Every challenge the planet delivered became an invitation to adapt. When the world froze, mammals grew thick fur and forged social bonds. When the forests vanished, our ancestors stood tall on two feet to see farther. Now, take another look at your hands resting quietly before you. Remember, you are a mammal. You have hair on your skin, and the three tiny bones in your ear began as part of a jaw. Those five fingers were once shaped for gripping swaying jungle branches.

Your warm skin carries the thermal legacy of creatures that survived an impact winter. Your curious mind inherits millions of years of relentless evolutionary trials. We are not detached visitors walking across the surface of the Earth. We are the living, breathing continuation of the great Cenozoic story. Today, more than six thousand species of mammals share the planet, from bats to elephants to blue whales. Every breath you draw connects you to the first tiny survivor in the ash. How will we choose to write the next chapter of this magnificent legacy?

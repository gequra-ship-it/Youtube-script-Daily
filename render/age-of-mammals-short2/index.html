Why does your brain
burn a fifth of
your resting energy?
Your brain is only
about two percent of
your body weight.
Yet it uses roughly
twenty percent of your
resting energy.
That is a very
expensive organ.
So how did our
ancestors afford it?
To find out,
travel to East Africa,
a few million years ago.
The climate was drying,
and the great Rift
Valley was deepening.
Rainforests broke into
patches of woodland and
savannah.
According to a leading
hypothesis, our ancestors
had to cross open
ground to reach the
next fruit trees.
Crossing tall grass on
all fours leaves you
blind to stalking predators.
Standing upright lifts
your eyes above the
grass, so you can
spot danger far across
the plain.
It also frees your hands.
Hands that can carry
food to infants,
and pick up a heavy stone.
A broken river pebble
has a sharp, glass-like edge.
With it, you can
slice meat from an
abandoned carcass.
The oldest widely accepted
stone tools, about two
point six million years
old, were found in Ethiopia.
And with a heavy
stone, you can smash
tough long bones.
Inside lies rich,
fatty bone marrow that
few other animals can reach.
That jackpot of dense
calories could help pay
for a bigger brain.
Over about three million
years, the brains of
this lineage roughly triple
in volume.
A big brain is
a hungry brain.
Maintaining one demands
a huge share of
your daily energy.
That cost is much
easier to pay with
dense, nutritious food.
Later, fire changed the
menu again.
Cooking over flames softens
tough fibrous tubers and
makes raw meat safer.
Many scientists suspect
cooked food is easier
to digest, freeing even
more energy for developing
minds.
Today, your brain holds
about eighty-six billion
nerve cells.
And it relentlessly burns
twenty percent of your
resting energy.
You are carrying the
metabolic legacy of
stone-wielding survivors.

If you detonated a
nuclear warhead in the
deep vacuum of space,
what would you actually hear?
On Earth, a nuclear
blast is one of
the loudest events imaginable.
The roar can rupture
eardrums many kilometers
away, as millions of
tons of superheated air
violently expand.
But in the vacuum
of space, there is
no air, no moisture,
and no atmosphere.
Sound is simply the
vibration of molecules
bumping into one another,
which means the void
has nothing to carry it.
So if a one-megaton
bomb went off right
in front of you,
it would release the
power of a million
tons of TNT in
absolute silence.
You would not hear
a rumble, a crack,
or even a whisper.
There would only be
a sudden, blinding flash
of light.
Even more bizarrely,
you would not feel
a shockwave either.
Down here, the blast
wave is what flattens
buildings and shatters cities.
But without air molecules
to compress into a
wall of force,
there is no pressure
wave sweeping across the
stars.
Instead, roughly seventy
to eighty percent of
the weapon's energy escapes
at once as high-energy
X-rays and gamma rays,
traveling at the speed
of light.
Those invisible rays can
hit any nearby spacecraft
and vaporize the thin
outer layer of its metal skin.
That layer blows outward,
and the recoil sends
a violent shock through
the rest of the craft.
It is a weapon
whose destructive power
needs no sound at all.
Scientists call this effect
blow-off impulse, and it
is one of the
main ways a nuclear
explosion in space can
damage a satellite.
But did you know
that humanity actually
tried detonating one of
these high above our planet?
In 1962, a warhead
was set off four
hundred kilometers above
the Pacific Ocean.
And its effects were
felt on the ground, far away.

Did you know that
in 1962, humanity detonated
a powerful thermonuclear
warhead in space?
The test was called
Starfish Prime, and it
took place about four
hundred kilometers above
the Pacific Ocean.
The warhead's yield was
1.4 megatons, roughly ninety
times the power of
the bomb dropped on Hiroshima.
When the device detonated
at the edge of
space, people in Hawaii,
about fourteen hundred
kilometers away, watched
the night sky light up.
Witnesses described a
brilliant flash and a
glow spreading across the
heavens.
But the light show
was nothing compared to
the invisible effect that
followed.
As the bomb's gamma
rays plunged into the
upper atmosphere, they
knocked electrons free
from air molecules.
Earth's magnetic field
bent those speeding
electrons, and together
they created an intense
electromagnetic pulse over
a huge area.
In Hawaii, hundreds of
streetlights went dark.
Burglar alarms began ringing
across the islands,
and a telephone link
was knocked out.
Without a gust of
wind or any sound
traveling from space,
an invisible electrical
effect had reached across
the ocean.
Even stranger, red and
green artificial auroras
rippled across the tropical
Pacific sky for many minutes.
Charged particles from
the explosion followed
the lines of Earth's
magnetic field and lit
up gases in the
upper atmosphere.
Remember how remote Hawaii is.
The islands sit in
the middle of the
Pacific, and yet a
bomb detonated far above
the ocean, in the
silence of space,
still left its mark
on the lights and
phones of ordinary people
below.
That test is the
reason scientists now study
high-altitude explosions
so carefully.
It showed that something
detonated in space could
have effects on the
ground that were felt
more than a thousand
kilometers away.
But the most dangerous
consequence of that blast
was only discovered in
the months afterward.
It was invisible,
it circled the entire
planet, and it was
silently damaging machines
high above us.

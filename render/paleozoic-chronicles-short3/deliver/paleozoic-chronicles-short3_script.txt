===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

What was the worst crisis in the history of animal life? About 252 million years ago, roughly 80 percent or more of marine species disappeared, and it all began with fire beneath Siberia. What happened, and who survived?

First, the world that was lost. The Permian Period began about 299 million years ago. Most of the land had joined into one supercontinent, Pangaea, surrounded by a single great ocean called Panthalassa. The top predators of the Late Permian were the gorgonopsians. The largest, Inostrancevia, reached about three and a half meters long, with saber-like canine teeth more than ten centimeters long.

Then, about 252 million years ago, enormous volcanic eruptions in what is now Siberia built a vast region of lava known as the Siberian Traps. Worse, the magma pushed through underground layers of coal and other carbon-rich rock, releasing huge amounts of carbon dioxide and methane.

The planet heated up fast. Evidence from fossil teeth suggests tropical seas warmed by about 8 degrees Celsius, reaching temperatures near 40 degrees at the surface. Warm water holds less oxygen, and large parts of the ocean became starved of it.

This was the end-Permian mass extinction, often called the Great Dying. Estimates vary, but roughly 80 percent or more of marine species disappeared, along with about 70 percent of land vertebrate species. The trilobites, which had lasted for more than 250 million years, were gone forever.

It was not the only disaster of the Paleozoic. At the end of the Ordovician, around 85 percent of marine species were lost. In the Late Devonian, roughly 70 percent of species. And at the end of the Permian, about 81 percent of marine species by one recent estimate, with other estimates going higher. Life came close to the edge, more than once.

So who survived? One famous survivor was Lystrosaurus, a stocky, pig-sized plant-eater with a beak and two tusks, and a distant relative of mammals. It is thought to have burrowed throughout its life. In the earliest Triassic rocks of South Africa's Karoo Basin, Lystrosaurus makes up more than 90 percent of the vertebrate fossils. Burrowing, a flexible diet, and fast growth may all have helped.

After the Great Dying, the Mesozoic began, and with it, the age of dinosaurs. The whole Paleozoic story is on Miya's Lab.

What would you do
if your favorite meal
suddenly moved to the
coldest place on Earth?
That may be exactly
what happened to Megalodon.
For millions of years,
the oceans were warm,
and Megalodon fed on
primitive whales that lived
along warm coastlines.
Then, during the Pliocene
epoch, Earth's climate
began to cool.
Polar ice expanded.
And cold water holds
more dissolved oxygen and
nutrients, which fueled
huge blooms of krill
and plankton near the poles.
Whales responded to the
change.
Baleen whales, once smaller
and spread across warm
seas, evolved enormous
bodies insulated by thick
layers of blubber.
Scientists think the rich
feast of the polar
seas pulled them toward
colder water.
Many whales began making
long seasonal journeys
between polar feeding
grounds and warmer breeding
waters.
The coastlines that once
held whales all year
were becoming less reliable,
and a hunter that
depended on them had
to travel much farther
to find a meal.
Megalodon had a problem.
In 2023, scientists
analyzing the chemistry
of its fossil teeth
reported that it was
partly warm-blooded, keeping
its body about seven
degrees Celsius warmer
than the surrounding water.
That warmth was a
real advantage, but it
has a cost.
One estimate suggests a
large Megalodon needed
close to one hundred
thousand calories every day.
Could a shark of
that size comfortably hunt
in freezing water for
months at a time?
Some researchers suspect
that the cold limited
its range, pushing the
giant toward warmer zones
while its favorite prey
drifted toward the icy poles.
Megalodon had to search
farther and wait longer
for each meal,
burning immense energy
along the way.
The balance between energy
gained and energy spent
was tipping in the
wrong direction.
Scientists do not think
the missing whales were
the only cause.
But when your body
needs that much fuel,
watching your dinner swim
beyond your reach is
a serious threat.
Climate change did not
just cool the water.
It rearranged the whole
menu of the ocean,
and Megalodon was left
on the wrong side of it.

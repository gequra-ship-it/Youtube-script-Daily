===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

An octopus has roughly five hundred million neurons, but most of them are not in its head. They are in its arms. How can an animal think with its whole body?

In humans, most of the thinking happens in the central brain, protected inside the skull. In the octopus, only about one-third of the neurons sit in the central brain, which wraps around the esophagus like a donut. The other two-thirds are spread through the eight arms, where they work as semi-autonomous processing centers.

This means each arm has its own local nervous system that can make simple decisions. If an arm is cut off, it does not just go limp. For a while, it can still react to touch, pull away from harmful stimuli, and even grasp objects with its suckers.

Each of the hundreds of suckers on an arm is a tiny sensory organ. They are packed with receptors that can taste chemicals by touch. When an octopus reaches into a dark crevice, the arm explores, tastes the surface, and helps decide what to grab, whether it is a crab or just a rock.

Scientists often describe this as a partnership between a manager and skilled field workers. The central brain gives broad commands, such as crawl toward that reef. The arms then work out much of the fine detail of how to move, without tangling.

Why did such a strange system evolve? Part of the story may be the shell. The ancestors of modern octopuses gradually shrank their shell, tucked it inside the body, and in octopuses lost it almost entirely. Without armor, an animal becomes a soft, protein-rich meal, so octopuses leaned on speed, camouflage, and clever behavior.

Losing the shell also turned the body into a flexible canvas. An octopus can squeeze through any opening larger than its beak, the only large hard part of its body. Controlling a boneless body takes a lot of computing power, and scientists think that challenge may have helped push the octopus nervous system to become so large and so unusual.

Imagine cracking open what
looks like an ancient
petrified log, only to
find it was never
made of wood at all.
In the nineteenth century,
a geologist named John
William Dawson described
massive fossilized trunks
from Canada, nearly eight
meters long and up
to a meter thick.
Naturally, he assumed they
were the remains of
ancient conifers.
He named the mystery
organism Prototaxites,
which means first yew.
But as scientific tools
advanced, a shocking
realization emerged under
the microscope.
Real wood contains
specialized plant cells,
xylem for pumping water,
and annual growth rings.
Prototaxites had none of
these.
Instead, its trunk was
made of millions of
tightly interwoven tubes,
packed together like dense
biological cables.
It was not a
plant at all,
but most likely a
gargantuan fungus standing
as tall as a
two-story building.
Imagine a pale,
stony pillar rising above
a bare, rocky valley,
with nothing else around it.
And that left scientists
with a baffling riddle.
Plants use sunlight to
grow large through
photosynthesis, but fungi
cannot make their own food.
How could an organism
with no leaves and
no bark gather enough
energy to build the
tallest structure on the
planet?
It sounded impossible,
and it kept the
debate alive for decades.
In 2007, geochemists tested
the carbon isotopes locked
inside the stone,
and the results varied
widely from one fossil
to another.
Plants draw their carbon
from the air,
so their signatures stay
fairly steady.
That fits an organism
that was absorbing food
from decaying matter,
instead of making it
from sunlight.
In 2025, a new
preprint, not yet peer
reviewed, even suggested
that Prototaxites may have
belonged to a branch
of life with no
living relatives.
So the story is
still unfinished.
Scientists are still
arguing, and every new
fossil and every new
test could change the answer.

HALF A CENTURY
Can quantum mechanics send
a message faster than light?
In the quantum realm,
particles can become
entangled, meaning their
physical properties become
inextricably linked
regardless of the distance
separating them.
Imagine creating a pair
of entangled particles
in a laboratory,
keeping one particle on
Earth, and sending the
other particle to an
astronaut near the rings
of Saturn.
According to quantum
mechanics, until you measure
those particles, their
states remain fundamentally
undetermined, existing
in a cloud of
probabilities known as
a superposition.
If one particle spins
upward, the other must
spin downward, but neither
particle has chosen a
definite orientation yet.
In the standard description,
the moment you measure
the particle on Earth
and find that its
spin is pointing up,
the particle near Saturn
is instantly fixed in
the opposite state,
pointing down.
The link appears
instantaneous, seemingly
thousands or millions of
times faster than light
could ever bridge the
distance.
Einstein famously derided
this phenomenon as spooky
action at a distance.
For decades, physicists
debated whether there were
hidden variables
pre-programmed into the
particles, like a pair
of matching shoes placed
into two identical boxes
before they were separated.
If you open one
box and find a
left shoe, you know
the other box holds
the right shoe,
without any mysterious
signal traveling between them.
However, experiments over
the past half-century have
shown that quantum particles
are not like pre-packed shoes.
No hidden instructions
packed inside the particles
can explain the results,
and the correlation between
the two particles is
stronger than any classical
system could ever produce.
Yet, despite this
instantaneous connection,
physicists discovered
something profound: quantum
entanglement cannot be
used to transmit any
useful information faster
than light.
This ironclad rule is
known in physics as
the No-Communication Theorem.
Imagine the astronaut near
Saturn looking at their
particle.
Because the outcomes of
quantum measurements are
fundamentally random, the
astronaut will simply see
an unpredictable sequence
of spin-up and spin-down
results.
They have no way
of knowing whether you
have performed a measurement
on Earth or not.
To decode any meaningful
message, the astronaut
must wait for you
to send a classical
message, via radio waves
or light signals,
telling them which
measurements you made.
That classical signal can
only travel at the
speed of light.

2.5 BILLION YEARS AGO
Around two and a
half billion years ago,
our planet transitioned
into the Proterozoic Eon,
bringing one of the
most dramatic
transformations in planetary
history.
As photosynthetic
cyanobacteria flourished
across the global ocean,
they released a steady
byproduct that had never
existed in large quantities
before: free oxygen.
Isn't it strange that
the very gas keeping
you awake tonight was
once a deadly toxin
to the planet's first
residents?
The newly generated oxygen
reacted with dissolved
iron in the seas,
creating massive deposits
of banded iron that
rusted and settled into
the ocean floor.
Once the iron reservoirs
were fully saturated,
free oxygen began leaking
directly into the
atmosphere, triggering
what geologists call the
Great Oxidation Event.
This sudden atmospheric
shift proved catastrophic
for the ancient anaerobic
organisms that could not
tolerate oxygen.
Furthermore, the oxygen
began reacting with
atmospheric methane, a
potent greenhouse gas,
stripping away the planet's
natural insulation.
Earth began to cool
at a terrifying speed,
plunging the entire globe
into a deep planetary freeze.
Scientists call this
possibility Snowball Earth,
when glaciers may have
crept from the poles
all the way to
the tropical equator.
How did life endure
beneath such thick ice?
Scientists think tiny
communities of single-celled
organisms clung to life
in pockets of liquid
water and around volcanic
hot spots.
For millions of years,
the world slept under
a shell of ice,
reflecting much of the
incoming sunlight back
into space.
Only the slow,
patient accumulation of
volcanic carbon dioxide
eventually broke the icy
grip, melting the glaciers
and releasing a flood
of nutrients into the
newly warmed seas.
Life had already learned
to build complex cells
with dedicated nuclei and
internal engines, and after
the thaws it blossomed
in new ways.
By the late Proterozoic,
in an enigmatic time
called the Ediacaran Period,
soft-bodied multicellular
creatures drifted through
the quiet seabed.
They looked like quilted
leaves, ribbed discs,
and feather-like fronds,
lacking eyes, shells,
or bones.
They rested upon the
microbial mats in serene
tranquility, living in
a world so gentle,
with few if any
hunters, that
paleontologists often call
it the Garden of Ediacara.
All of it began
in the quiet of
a young world's oceans.

100 MILLION YEARS
370 MILLION YEARS AGO
Did you know that
the trilobite empire was
nearly wiped out more
than a hundred million
years before its final
extinction?
Around three hundred and
seventy million years ago,
during the Late Devonian
crisis, a long ecological
disaster struck the global
oceans.
Scientists suspect that
widespread ocean anoxia,
a severe drop in
dissolved oxygen, spread
over the seabed and
helped collapse the shallow
reef ecosystems.
Over millions of years,
almost every famous branch
of the trilobite family
tree was erased.
The flamboyant giants,
the bizarre spade-nosed
scavengers, and the heavily
spined monsters vanished
forever.
Of the many orders
that had once dominated
the seas, only one
survived: the proetids.
These were not majestic
ocean rulers.
They were mostly small,
modest, oval-bodied animals,
generalists that quietly
clung to life in
the remaining shallow waters.
And yet they held on.
For well over a
hundred million years,
through the Carboniferous
and the Permian,
this single lineage kept
the trilobite story going
while sharks and bony
fish took over the
open sea around them.
Think about that.
Nearly everything that
had made trilobites so
spectacular was gone,
and the group still
survived for more than
a hundred million years.
That is a remarkable
record of persistence,
and it came from
small animals doing ordinary
things in shallow water,
year after year,
age after age.
But having a whole
family tree pruned down
to a single branch
may have become a trap.
No one knows for
sure, yet a group
with so little variety
left may have had
fewer options when the
greatest catastrophe of
all arrived at the
end of the Permian.
When that ultimate crisis
struck, the proetids were
the last trilobites on
Earth, and the line
ended with them.
A branch that once
seemed too small to
matter had carried the
entire legacy of its
kind across an age.
That story of narrowing
options is one of
the most important lessons
in the whole trilobite saga.

Fifteen years is a
long time to wait
for a ship.
Can you imagine watching
that boat prepare to launch?
The sailors left them
a few supplies,
some tools, and a promise.
On the way,
they said, they would
return with a ship.
On the twenty-seventh of
September, 1761, one hundred
and twenty-two French
sailors pushed off through
the surf.
The Malagasy survivors
stood on the beach,
watching the sail shrink
until it vanished over
the horizon.
The Providence reached
Madagascar, and from there
the crew made their
way to Isle de France.
Their first officer,
Barthélemy Castellan du
Vernet, begged the governor
to send a vessel
for the people left behind.
But the answer he
received was cold.
The Seven Years' War
was raging, and the
colony's resources were
stretched thin.
Worse, the governor,
Antoine Marie
Desforges-Boucher, was
reportedly furious that
the Utile's captain had
broken the rules on
trading in people.
The governor refused to
send a ship,
and the request was
turned down again and again.
In the years that
followed, the island slipped
from the minds of
the colonial authorities.
Rescue ships did try
in the 1770s,
but the surf turned
them back or the
reef wrecked them.
Meanwhile, back on the
island, time moved with
agonizing slowness.
The promised rescue never
came, and the people
left behind had to
rely on each other.
Imagine standing on that
beach after ten years
had passed.
Would you still believe
someone was coming for you?
The horizon was always
empty, a circle of
blue meeting blue.
An entire decade of
your life, on a
strip of sand you
could walk across in
less than half an hour.
Many people would have
lost hope.
Yet this community kept
living, and they kept
the fire alive.
They had every reason
to believe the world
had forgotten them,
because it largely had.

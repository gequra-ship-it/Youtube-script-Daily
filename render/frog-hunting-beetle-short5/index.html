For a long time,
bugs killing frogs sounded
like a myth.
Then someone tested it.
In 2011, Gil Wizen
and Avital Gasith,
at Tel Aviv University
in Israel, published a
laboratory study of two
beetles, Epomis
circumscriptus and Epomis
dejeani.
They used 420 larvae
and ran 382 encounters
with five kinds of
amphibians: a green toad,
a tree frog,
a water frog,
a banded newt,
and a fire salamander.
The result was clear.
In about seventy percent
of the encounters,
the larva lured the
amphibian before it
attacked, and the amphibian
was eaten.
In the rest,
the meeting was accidental,
but it still ended
badly for the amphibian.
Across the study,
the authors report a
success rate of close
to one hundred percent
for the larvae.
How could nature produce
something so strange?
Scientists suspect the
behavior started as a defense.
If a larva lives
where amphibians hunt,
the ones that learned
to dodge a tongue
and bite back would survive.
Over a long time,
defense could turn into
attack.
This is a hypothesis.
It is a reasonable
one, but it is
not a proven history.
There is also a
bigger idea behind this story.
Biologists sometimes talk
about the Red Queen
hypothesis.
It says that living
things keep changing just
to keep up with
each other, like runners
on a treadmill.
A frog's hunting reflex
works well against most
insects.
That makes the one
insect that can beat
it very unusual.
The frog's weakness is
also its strength.
A reflex that finds
food quickly cannot easily
stop to ask whether
the target is a
worm or a trap.
The same speed that
makes frogs good hunters
makes them easy to fool.
Epomis does not beat
the reflex with strength.
It beats it with
timing and a clever disguise.
One more point of honesty.
The amphibians in the
trials were young,
only two to three
weeks after metamorphosis,
and the work was
done in a laboratory
in Israel.
Other species in the
genus have been reported
in Africa and Asia,
but the wild lives
of most of them
are poorly known.
We often assume that
bigger animals always win,
and that a backbone
beats a bug.
Nature does not care
about our rankings.
Given enough time,
evolution can turn almost
any strength into a weakness.
Sometimes, the thing that
made you strong is
the very thing that
gets you killed.

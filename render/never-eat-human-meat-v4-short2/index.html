In the mountains of
Papua New Guinea,
a disease called kuru
made people shake,
lose their balance,
and sometimes burst into
uncontrolled laughter.
At its peak,
kuru was eight to
nine times more common
in women and children
than in men.
Roughly 2,700 people died.
The cause was a
funeral custom.
The Fore ate their
dead as an act of love.
Women and children ate
the brain, where the
infectious prions were
concentrated.
Then evolution answered.
In 2009, researchers found
a new version of
the prion protein gene
among the Fore, called G127V.
No kuru patients carried it.
It appeared where kuru
had raged, and nowhere
else in the world.
A protective mark,
left in just a
few generations.
Why nature may punish
eating your own kind
is the full story
on Miya's Lab.

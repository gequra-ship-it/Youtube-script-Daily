===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Take a look at the light switch on your wall or the smartphone resting in your hand. Flipping a switch feels effortless, yet it draws power from an invisible web stretching across entire continents. We split atoms, dam giant rivers, and beam data through the vacuum of space. How often do you think about where all that electricity truly comes from? From our vantage point on the ground, human technology looks almost miraculous. But how powerful are we when measured against the terrifying scale of the cosmos?

Before we climb the ladder, how much power does humanity actually use? Averaged over a whole year, all the electricity, gasoline, heating, and industry on Earth add up to roughly nineteen terawatts. That is nineteen trillion watts, running every second of every day. It sounds enormous. Now compare it with the Sun. The sunlight striking the Earth carries about one hundred seventy thousand terawatts. In a single hour, the planet receives about as much solar energy as humanity uses in an entire year. So where does our energy come from today? Mostly from burning coal, oil, and natural gas, fuels that formed from living things over hundreds of millions of years. We are spending an ancient savings account of sunlight. And the gap between what we use and what falls on us for free is the first hint of how Kardashev's ladder works. Energy is not scarce in the universe. It is only scarce for those who cannot yet reach it.

If an alien civilization looked at Earth tonight, what kind of creature would they see? In the nineteen sixties, Soviet astrophysicist Nikolai Kardashev asked this very question. Politics, culture, and architecture would be impossible to read across deep space. How do you even measure progress across billions of light-years? Kardashev proposed a beautifully simple metric: pure energy consumption. The more energy a civilization can capture and control, the more it can build, send, and change.

The man behind the ladder was a radio astronomer, not a futurist. In nineteen sixty-four, Nikolai Kardashev published a paper in a Soviet astronomy journal called Transmission of Information by Extraterrestrial Civilizations. His question was practical. If a civilization wanted to send a signal across the galaxy, how much power would it need, and how would we recognize it? To answer that, he had to guess how much energy a civilization might command. His original Type One was close to the technology of his own day, roughly four trillion watts. Humanity now uses several times more than that. Carl Sagan later redefined Type One at about ten quadrillion watts, and that is the version most people quote. By that measure, we still fall short. Kardashev even proposed that a mysterious radio source called CTA one hundred two might be a sign of an advanced civilization. In nineteen sixty-five, the idea made headlines. Further study showed that it was a quasar, a natural object.

Kardashev divided all potential civilizations into three massive cosmic tiers. In the popular version of his scale, a Type One civilization commands the entire energy output of its home planet. A Type Two civilization harnesses the total power radiated by its parent star. A Type Three civilization commands the staggering energy of an entire galaxy.

It sounds like fantasy, but the idea is simple: each step needs vastly more power than the last. Energy is the currency behind everything a civilization does, whether you are boiling water or traversing the stars.

Why would a civilization ever need so much power? Think about growth. If a civilization's energy use grew by only one percent a year, it would double about every seventy years. That sounds slow. But keep doing the arithmetic. At one percent a year, humanity's nineteen terawatts would reach the Type One level in roughly six hundred years. Reaching the total power of the Sun would take about three thousand years. And reaching the power of a galaxy would take roughly twenty-five hundred years more. This is arithmetic, not a forecast. Real growth could stall, or stop, for many reasons. But it shows why Kardashev's three steps are not three random numbers. They are what steady growth looks like on a cosmic calendar, where a few thousand years is a blink.

What would life inside a Type One civilization actually look like? It means capturing the sunlight falling on the world, along with wind, tidal, and geothermal power. Can you imagine waking up in a world where extreme weather is simply scheduled? A Type One society wouldn't just survive on its planet; it might manage it like a living organism. In theory, earthquakes could be eased before they strike, hurricanes could be dissipated, and global climates could be kept in balance.

What could a Type One civilization tap? Sunlight is the big one. Earth intercepts roughly one hundred seventy thousand terawatts, more than ten times the Type One benchmark. Wind is sunlight in motion, because the Sun's heat drives the atmosphere. Tides come from the Moon and the Sun tugging on the oceans, and tidal friction dissipates around three to four terawatts. Heat leaking from the planet's interior adds about forty-seven terawatts more. Next to sunlight, tides and the planet's own heat are small. A Type One civilization is mostly a sunlight civilization. There is one catch. Every watt a civilization uses ends up as heat. At the Type One level of ten quadrillion watts, that waste heat would equal roughly eight percent of the sunlight the Earth absorbs. Power on that scale would be a major planetary challenge, not only a triumph.

Where could far more power come from? Stars shine by nuclear fusion, joining light atoms into heavier ones. Humans have worked on fusion energy for decades. In December of twenty twenty-two, a laser facility in California, the National Ignition Facility, produced more fusion energy than the laser light delivered to the fuel. It was a landmark. But the lasers themselves drew far more energy from the grid than the fusion released, so it was not yet a power plant. Fusion is still a science experiment, not a working power source. Still, the experiment showed that humans can trigger the reaction that powers the Sun. Whether it can ever supply a Type One civilization is an open question.

So, where exactly do we stand on this monumental ladder? You might assume our glowing cities and orbital satellites place us comfortably near Type One, but the real number is humbling. Astronomer Carl Sagan estimated that humanity sits somewhere around Type zero point seven. Our power grids are still vulnerable. In March of nineteen eighty-nine, a solar storm blacked out the electric grid across Quebec. We still burn the remains of ancient life for fuel, and extreme weather can still bring our supply chains to their knees.

Nature still reminds us who is in charge. In eighteen fifteen, the eruption of Mount Tambora in Indonesia threw so much material into the sky that the following year became known as the year without a summer. In eighteen fifty-nine, the Carrington solar storm lit the skies with aurora and caused telegraph systems to fail across Europe and North America. A storm of that size today would hit a far more electrified world. Sixty-six million years ago, an asteroid about ten kilometers wide struck what is now Mexico, and the dinosaurs lost their world. But there is progress. In September of twenty twenty-two, NASA's DART spacecraft crashed into a small asteroid moon called Dimorphos and changed its orbit. It was a tiny test on a harmless target, but it was a first step toward the kind of control a Type One civilization would need.

If reaching Type One is difficult, stepping up to Type Two borders on the divine. A Type Two civilization commands billions of times more power than a Type One. What kind of engineering would it take to swallow the light of a star? In nineteen sixty, physicist Freeman Dyson suggested that an advanced civilization could build a vast swarm of solar collectors surrounding a sun. Such a megastructure, popularly known as a Dyson sphere, could capture most of a star's output.

Now picture the Sun. Every second, it releases roughly four hundred septillion watts. Earth intercepts less than one part in two billion of that. Almost all of the Sun's light streams away into the cold dark between the stars, and no one ever uses it. In one second, the Sun gives off far more energy than humanity has used in its entire history, more than ten thousand times more. That is the waste a Type Two civilization would refuse to accept. It would ask a simple question. Why let all that light escape?

Where does all that sunlight come from? In the Sun's core, hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium, and a tiny amount of mass turns into energy, following Einstein's famous equation. Every second, the Sun converts roughly four million metric tons of its mass into light. That sounds like a lot, but the Sun is so massive that it has burned like this for about four and a half billion years, and it will keep going for about five billion more. Then it will swell into a red giant. A Dyson swarm could capture the Sun's light, but it could not stop the Sun from aging. A Type Two civilization would eventually have to face the death of its star. Even the grandest energy source has a deadline.

That one-page paper by Dyson appeared in the journal Science. Dyson credited the idea to a science fiction novel, Olaf Stapledon's Star Maker, written in nineteen thirty-seven. He did not imagine a solid shell wrapped around a star. He considered a rigid shell impossible. He pictured a swarm of separate objects orbiting the star, a cloud of habitats and collectors that together would intercept its light. His second insight was about detection. Any energy a civilization uses ends up as heat. A swarm that absorbed starlight would warm up and radiate that energy away as infrared light. So astronomers might find such a civilization not by listening for radio messages, but by looking for stars that glow in infrared more than they should. Dyson also suggested that such a civilization might have no interest in communicating at all.

Has anyone looked? Yes. Astronomers have searched catalogs of stars for unexplained infrared glow, and so far they have found no confirmed Dyson swarm. The most famous false alarm came in twenty fifteen, when astronomers reported that a star known as Tabby's Star was dimming in strange, irregular ways. Some researchers discussed whether a giant structure could be passing in front of it. But later observations showed that the star dims more in blue light than in red. Solid objects block all colors equally, while dust blocks blue more, so dust is a better explanation. The mystery is not fully solved, but the alien megastructure idea lost its support. The lesson is simple. A silent sky is not proof of an empty sky. It only means we have not found anything yet.

Beyond a single solar system lies the incomprehensible expanse of the galactic spiral. A Type Three civilization would not stop at one sun; it would harness hundreds of billions of stars across a hundred thousand light-years. Some theoretical models suggest it might even extract rotational energy from supermassive black holes. Traveling between star systems might be as routine to such a society as taking a morning train is to you. To a civilization operating at this galactic scale, our entire recorded history might look like a momentary spark.

How big is a galaxy? Our Milky Way holds between one hundred and four hundred billion stars, spread across a disk about one hundred thousand light-years wide. Consider how slowly we cross even the tiniest part of it. Voyager 1 launched in nineteen seventy-seven and left the bubble of the Sun's wind in twenty twelve. At its speed, it would need roughly seventy-five thousand years to cover the distance to Proxima Centauri, the nearest star, which is just over four light-years away. A Type Three civilization would have to master distances a hundred thousand light-years wide. We do not know whether that is possible. We only know that the physics we understand does not forbid the energy, and that the engineering is far beyond us.

Why do some theorists think a black hole could be a power plant? A spinning black hole drags space around with it, and some of its energy is stored in that rotation. In nineteen sixty-nine, physicist Roger Penrose showed that, in principle, a particle could steal energy from this spin. Calculations suggest that up to about twenty-nine percent of a maximally spinning black hole's mass could be extracted as energy. Later work suggested that magnetic fields might tap the spin too, and this may help power the brilliant jets we see from the centers of some galaxies. In twenty nineteen, the Event Horizon Telescope gave us the first image of a black hole's shadow, in the galaxy Messier eighty-seven. Whether any civilization could harvest this energy is unknown, but it is a real prediction of physics.

Is energy really the right ruler for a civilization? Not everyone thinks so. Our own technology suggests a different path. Computers have become far more efficient, doing more work with less power, and a phone now holds more computing than room-sized machines once did. In nineteen ninety-eight, physicist John Barrow suggested that advanced civilizations might not grow bigger at all. They might instead grow smaller, mastering the very small, from molecules to atoms. If that is true, an advanced society could use modest energy and still be incredibly capable. It would also be harder for us to detect. We have no evidence for either view. But it reminds us that the Kardashev scale is a useful idea, not a law of nature. It measures one thing, energy, and ignores much of what makes a civilization wise.

This leads to one of the most famous questions in science. In nineteen fifty, the physicist Enrico Fermi is said to have asked his colleagues over lunch, where is everybody? If the galaxy is so old and so large, why have we met no one? In nineteen sixty, astronomer Frank Drake pointed a radio telescope at two nearby stars in the first modern search for alien signals, called Project Ozma. In nineteen sixty-one, he wrote an equation to organize what we would need to know: how many stars have planets, how often life begins, how often it becomes intelligent, and how long civilizations last. We still do not know several of those numbers. In nineteen seventy-seven, a radio telescope in Ohio recorded a strong, narrow burst that lasted seventy-two seconds. An astronomer circled it on the printout and wrote Wow. It has never been detected again, and no one knows what it was.

Yet this cosmic ladder leaves us with a chilling paradox. If energy growth were the natural path of intelligent life, why is the cosmos so silent? Some scientists suspect that the transition between Type zero and Type One is a lethal filter that few species survive, an idea known as the Great Filter. Are we clever enough to become masters of our planet before our own power consumes us? We are wielding planetary-scale technology while still learning how to cooperate. We may be living through one of humanity's greatest tests right now.

In nineteen seventy-five, astrophysicist Michael Hart argued that even slow expansion should fill a galaxy in a surprisingly short time. Some estimates put it at a few million to a few tens of millions of years, and the Milky Way is more than ten billion years old. That is why the silence feels so strange. In the nineteen nineties, economist Robin Hanson gave a name to one possible answer. The idea is simple. Between dead chemistry and a civilization that spreads across a galaxy lies a series of steps. If even one step is extremely unlikely, almost no one gets through. The filter could be behind us, such as the origin of life itself, which would mean we are lucky. Or it could be ahead of us, which would be a warning. Nobody knows. Other explanations are possible, including that life is rare, that civilizations do not last, or that we are simply not looking the right way.

What would passing such a test look like? Look at what we have already done. In nineteen thirty-eight, scientists discovered nuclear fission. Seven years later, the first atomic bombs existed. By nineteen eighty-six, the world's nuclear arsenals peaked at around seventy thousand warheads. Then, slowly, they shrank, to roughly twelve thousand today. There are hopeful stories too. In nineteen eighty-seven, nations signed the Montreal Protocol to phase out chemicals that were damaging the ozone layer, and the ozone layer is now recovering. Cooperation on a planetary scale is possible. The question is whether we can do it fast enough for the next problems, from climate change to powerful new technologies. Those are the skills a Type One civilization needs most.

Tonight, when you turn off the light and look up at the night sky, remember what you are looking at. That quiet darkness is not empty space; it is an open frontier waiting for civilizations that endure. We are still taking our very first steps into the cosmos, but we are looking upward with purpose. If you want to keep exploring the grand machinery of our universe, step inside this lab and subscribe to Miya's Lab. Every long journey through the stars begins with a single curious mind.

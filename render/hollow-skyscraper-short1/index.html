HALF A MILLION TONNES
Why does a tower
that is mostly empty
space not crush itself?
Burj Khalifa in Dubai
rises 828 meters,
higher than any other
building ever finished.
Yet most of it
is air: lobbies,
corridors, apartments and
elevator shafts.
A needle that tall
and that hollow should
fold under its own
weight, and the wind
should finish the job.
So what is really
holding it up?
The answer begins with
something you can crush
in one hand.
Take a flat sheet of paper.
Stand that sheet on
its edge and it
flops over at once.
Now roll the same
sheet into a tube.
Suddenly it can carry
a surprising load.
The paper did not change.
Only its shape did.
Nature found this trick
long ago.
A bird's bones are
hollow, braced by thin
struts inside, which keeps
them light and stiff.
Bamboo is a hollow
stalk, stiffened by solid
rings along its length.
Hollow shapes put material
exactly where it matters most.
Every skyscraper fights
a quiet war against
two invisible enemies.
The first is gravity.
It wants to pull
the whole tower straight
down, and the weight
is enormous.
Burj Khalifa is often
quoted at roughly half
a million tonnes,
even with nobody inside.
The second enemy pushes
sideways.
Engineers call it a
lateral load, and for
a tower this tall
the biggest one is wind.
In some parts of
the world, earthquakes
add another.
Burj Khalifa's chief
structural engineer, Bill
Baker of the firm
SOM, has described wind
as the most important
factor in designing tall
buildings.
So the puzzle is this.
How do you defeat
gravity and wind using
mostly empty air?
We will solve it
from the bottom up,
starting far below the
streets of Dubai.
A hollow shape is
strong because of where
its material sits.
That rule works at
every scale, from a
drinking straw to the
tallest tower ever built.
Defeating both enemies
starts underground, and
then it climbs,
one clever trick at a time.
The full story,
from the foundation to
the spire, is in
our long video.

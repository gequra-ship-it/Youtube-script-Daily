Could you survive fifty
degrees Celsius in a
scorching desert?
Surprisingly, yes, for
a while, as long
as the air is
dry and you have
plenty of water to drink.
In dry air,
your body cools itself
by sweating.
As sweat evaporates from
your skin, it carries
heat away into the air.
But what happens when
the humidity climbs toward
one hundred percent?
When the air is
already saturated with
moisture, sweat cannot
evaporate.
It simply pools on
your skin and drips
off uselessly, taking none
of the heat with it.
Scientists measure this
combination of heat and
humidity with a metric
called the wet-bulb
temperature.
To measure it,
you wrap a wet
cloth around the bulb
of a thermometer and
let the air blow across it.
The cloth cools as
the water evaporates,
so the reading tells
you how well evaporation
can work at that moment.
In dry air,
the wet-bulb reading is
far below the air temperature.
In saturated air,
the two readings become equal.
The theoretical limit for
human survival is a
wet-bulb temperature of
about thirty-five degrees
Celsius.
At that point,
the laws of physics
leave the body unable
to shed its own
heat to the surroundings.
Even a healthy person
resting in the shade
is expected to overheat,
because the body keeps
producing metabolic heat
with nowhere for it to go.
Your core temperature
climbs, and once it
passes forty degrees,
delicate proteins in your
cells begin to lose
their shape, which can
lead to organ failure.
Recent studies suspect
that the real danger
line may be lower
than thirty-five, especially
for older people and
those with health problems.
So the threat is
not only how hot
the air is.
It is how much
water the air already holds.
A mild-looking temperature
in a steamy climate
can be more dangerous
than a much hotter
day in a dry desert.

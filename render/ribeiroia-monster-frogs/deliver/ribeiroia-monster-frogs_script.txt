===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Imagine walking along the edge of a quiet pond on a warm summer afternoon. Dragonflies drift through the reeds, crickets call from the grass, and the water lies perfectly still. It looks like a picture of untouched nature. But beneath that calm surface, something strange can happen to the frogs. A creature too small to see without a microscope can change the way a tadpole's body is built, and the result can be a frog with legs where no legs should be.

Our story begins on August 8, 1995, in southern Minnesota. A group of students from the Minnesota New Country School were exploring a pond in the Ney Wildlife Preserve, near the small town of Henderson. They were catching northern leopard frogs, an ordinary sight in the Midwest. But the frogs they pulled from the water were not ordinary. Some had missing legs. Some had twisted legs. And some had extra legs. The students counted eleven deformed frogs and told their teacher.

Their teacher called the Minnesota Pollution Control Agency. A research scientist named Judy Helgen took the call. Within days, her team was wading into the same pond. What they found was worse than the students had described. Frogs with legs that did not work. Frogs with legs bent at impossible angles. Frogs missing an eye. This was not one unlucky animal. Something seemed to be going wrong for a large part of the whole population.

Here are the numbers from that pond. On August 18, 1995, 60 percent of the 53 frogs collected were deformed. A week later, the team collected 137 frogs, and 76 percent were deformed. And in September 1996, 47 percent of 70 frogs were deformed.

The story spread fast. Newspapers and television picked it up, and people across Minnesota began calling in their own sightings. By the end of September 1996, reports had come from 55 of the state's 87 counties, at 172 locations. Soon, deformed frogs were being reported in other states and in southern Canada too. And people began asking a frightening question. If something in the water was doing this to frogs, what might it be doing to the people who lived nearby?

There was a good reason to worry. Frogs are often called living alarm systems. Their skin is thin, and it takes in water and air directly from their surroundings. They also spend their early lives in water, as tadpoles, before moving onto land. So whatever is in a pond can reach a frog quickly. If frogs were suddenly growing extra legs, it seemed natural to suspect that something toxic had entered the water. What else could build a frog so wrong?

In September 1996, scientists met in Duluth to compare ideas. Some suspected pesticides. Others suspected stronger ultraviolet light, reaching the ground through a thinning ozone layer. Another researcher showed that a chemical called retinoic acid could trigger extra limbs in the lab, and he pointed out that a common insecticide, methoprene, was related to it. Each idea pointed toward the same uncomfortable conclusion: that modern human chemicals were changing the bodies of wild animals.

But one scientist at that meeting had a very different idea. Stan Sessions, a biologist from New York, argued that the culprit might not be a chemical at all. He suggested a parasite. If certain parasites infected tadpoles while their legs were first forming, he said, they could cause extra limbs. Sessions had first proposed this idea years earlier, in 1987. At the time, few people paid much attention. The chemical explanations simply seemed more likely.

Far away in California, a Stanford University student named Pieter Johnson had been following the story. In December 1996, he began a project of his own. Instead of starting with chemicals, he went out to ponds in Santa Clara County, south of San Francisco, where Pacific treefrogs were turning up with deformed legs. He looked closely at what lived in those ponds. And he noticed something interesting. The ponds with deformed frogs also had a particular kind of snail.

When Johnson and his colleagues examined the deformed frogs, they found tiny cysts packed around the base of the hind legs. Inside each cyst was a young flatworm, a parasite called Ribeiroia ondatrae. Its name has a strange history. The species was first described in 1931 from worms found in the livers of muskrats, and muskrats belong to the genus Ondatra. A parasite named after a muskrat was now the prime suspect in the mystery of the monster frogs.

But finding a parasite inside a deformed frog does not prove that the parasite caused the deformity. So Johnson ran an experiment. His team took Pacific treefrog eggs from ponds with no parasites and raised the tadpoles in tanks. Some tanks received Ribeiroia, at levels similar to the wild ponds. The others did not. The results were striking. In the parasite tanks, deformities appeared in 85 percent of the frogs. The tadpoles raised without the parasite developed normally.

The findings were published in the journal Science in April 1999, less than a year after Johnson graduated. In the same issue, Stan Sessions and his colleagues reported on frogs from twelve locations in California, Oregon, Arizona, and New York. They found many limbs that were mirror images of each other, a pattern typical of parasite infection. For the first time, there was strong evidence that a natural parasite could build these monster frogs.

So was the Minnesota mystery solved? Not exactly. The parasite clearly explained deformed frogs in many western ponds. But in Minnesota, the investigation never reached a firm answer. The state agency ended its study in 2001, citing budget limits. To this day, the cause of the Minnesota frogs is considered inconclusive, and many scientists think more than one cause may have been involved. No one knows for sure what happened in that pond in 1995.

To understand how a tiny worm can do this, we need to follow its life. Ribeiroia ondatrae cannot complete its life inside a single animal. It needs three different hosts. First, a snail. Second, a tadpole or a small fish. And finally, a bird or a mammal, such as a heron, a hawk, a duck, or even a badger. The journey begins at the bottom of the pond, inside a snail with a coiled, flat shell: the ram's horn snail.

It starts with a tiny swimming larva called a miracidium, which hatches from an egg in the water. When it finds a ram's horn snail, it gets inside. There, the parasite multiplies, making many copies of itself without mating. And as these young parasites feed, they eat the snail's reproductive tissue. The snail is castrated. It can no longer produce young of its own. Instead, its body becomes a factory, working for the parasite.

Inside the snail, the parasite produces a new stage called cercariae. These are free-swimming larvae with a tail, shaped a little like tiny tadpoles themselves. And there can be a lot of them. Researchers have reported that a single infected snail can release more than 1,000 cercariae in one night. Like the cercariae of other flukes, they do not feed. They have only a short time to find their next target: a growing tadpole.

Picture a tadpole swimming in the warm shallows. At this stage, its body is changing fast. Near the base of its tail, small clusters of cells called limb buds are starting to form. These buds will become its back legs. Each one holds the instructions to build a thigh, a knee, an ankle, a foot, and toes, all in the right order. And it is exactly here, around these young limb buds, that the cercariae tend to gather.

The cercariae burrow through the tadpole's thin skin and leave their swimming tails behind. Once inside, each one wraps itself in a protective wall and becomes a cyst, a resting stage called a metacercaria. In infected tadpoles, large numbers of these cysts build up near the base of the hind legs. And it does not take many. Researchers have reported that as few as 12 cercariae can kill or deform a single tadpole.

So how does a cyst make a leg grow twice? Scientists are still working that out. One idea is mechanical. The cysts may physically push apart the cells of the growing limb bud, splitting one set of instructions into several. Earlier lab experiments supported this idea. Researchers implanted tiny inert beads into the developing limb buds of salamanders and frogs, and extra limb structures grew. If plain beads can do it, a cyst sitting in the same place might do it too.

The other idea is chemical. Limbs are guided by signaling molecules, and one of the most important is retinoic acid, a substance made from vitamin A. In 2012, a team of researchers measured it in wood frog tadpoles. Limb buds with Ribeiroia inside had about 70 percent more retinoic acid than limb buds without the parasite. That does not prove the whole story. But it suggests the parasite may be scrambling the chemical instructions that tell a limb how to grow.

When those instructions break down, the results can be extraordinary. An infected limb bud can produce two or three legs instead of one, and each extra leg is often a mirror image of its neighbor. According to Johnson's lab, the parasite has been shown to cause up to six extra limbs, for a total of ten legs on a single frog. The extra legs usually grow from the hips, because that is where the cysts gather.

But extra legs are only part of the picture. The parasite can also cause the opposite. Some frogs end up with legs that are partly or completely missing. Others have skin webbing that binds a leg against the body, bony bridges that lock a joint in place, or extra or missing toes. Hind legs are hit most often. And researchers report that in some populations, these malformations can affect more than half of the young frogs leaving a pond.

Now imagine being one of those frogs. A healthy frog depends on strong, well-timed back legs. It uses them to leap away from danger, dive into the water, and snap up fast insects. A frog with extra legs dangling from its hips, or with legs bound by skin, cannot do these things well. Every jump becomes clumsy. Every escape becomes slower. But how much slower? In 2011, two researchers decided to measure it.

Brett Goodman and Pieter Johnson compared malformed frogs with normal ones. The malformed frogs jumped 41 percent shorter distances. They swam 37 percent slower. And they caught 55 percent fewer crickets. On every test that mattered for survival, the deformed frogs fell far behind.

Out in real ponds, the results were grim. In ordinary ponds, more than a fifth of the parasitized frogs died every two weeks, and almost no deformed frogs were left among the adults. Malformed frogs rarely live long enough to breed. And the parasite can kill outright, too. In lab studies, even low levels of infection caused 30 to 95 percent mortality in some frogs and toads. For a tadpole, Ribeiroia is far more than a strange curiosity.

This raises a puzzling question. Why would a parasite damage the animal it lives in? Many parasites do best when their hosts stay alive for a long time. A deformed frog that cannot hop seems like a terrible home. So what could the parasite possibly gain? To find a possible answer, we have to look at where its life cycle ends. Not in the pond. Not in the frog. But in the sky above the water.

Look up from the pond. A great blue heron stands in the shallows, perfectly still, watching. Herons, ducks, and hawks are among the final hosts of Ribeiroia. Only inside a bird or a mammal can the parasite grow into an adult and reproduce. But a cyst hidden in a tadpole's hip cannot fly. To reach the bird, the frog carrying it has to be eaten. And this is where the deformities may play a role.

Scientists have proposed that the deformities may help the parasite. A healthy frog can spot a hunting heron and leap into deep water. A frog with tangled extra legs is much easier to catch. If deformed frogs are eaten more often, more parasites reach the birds they need. Interestingly, Goodman and Johnson found that in a pond with no predators, extra legs had no significant effect on survival. That fits the idea: the danger of deformity comes mostly from being hunted.

When a bird swallows an infected frog, the cysts are freed in its gut. The young worms move along the intestine and attach to its lining. There, well fed, they grow into adults and begin producing eggs. Those eggs leave the bird in its droppings. And because birds travel, they can carry the parasite to new ponds and wetlands, sometimes far from where the infection began. In about two to three weeks, the eggs in the water are ready to hatch, and the cycle starts again.

Was this parasite something new in the 1990s? Not at all. Some reports of malformed amphibians go back more than two hundred years. In 2003, Pieter Johnson and his colleagues looked through records from 1946 to 1988. They found nine historical hot spots in seven states, and at most of them they found signs of the parasite in preserved specimens. Ribeiroia had been building extra legs on frogs long before the students in Minnesota ever dipped their nets.

But something had changed. When the team revisited three of the old sites, they still found the parasite, and severe malformations in 7 to 50 percent of the frogs. Before 1990, fewer than a dozen such sites were known. In the years that followed, more than 50 new hot spots were found. And at Jette Pond in Montana, malformations in Pacific treefrogs rose from about 20 percent in 1960 to about 46 percent in 2000. Why would an old parasite suddenly become so common?

The answer may lead back to us after all. Farms, lawns, and cattle pastures release nitrogen and phosphorus, the nutrients found in fertilizer and manure. When rain washes these nutrients into ponds, it changes the whole food web. Algae grow faster. And ram's horn snails, which graze on algae, have more to eat. More snails can mean more homes for Ribeiroia. In 2007, Johnson and his colleagues reported a test of whether this chain reaction really happens.

They built 36 outdoor ponds and added nutrients to some of them. In the enriched ponds, the total mass of snails rose by nearly 50 percent. Infected snails there released about twice as many cercariae. And tadpoles in the enriched ponds carried two to five times more infection.

Johnson described it as watching nutrient pollution move through the parasite's life cycle as it cascaded through the food web. It is a remarkable chain. Fertilizer feeds algae. Algae feed snails. Snails feed parasites. And parasites, in turn, reshape frogs. Other chemicals may add to the problem. The herbicide atrazine, for example, has been reported to weaken amphibian immune systems, which could make frogs more vulnerable to infection.

There is a hopeful twist, though. In 2013, Johnson's team published a huge survey: 345 wetlands in California, more than 24,000 amphibians examined, and more than 17,000 snails dissected. Ponds with more kinds of amphibians had far fewer deformed frogs, about 78.5 percent fewer than ponds with few species. The parasite did best in Pacific treefrogs. One explanation is that when other species share the pond, many parasites end up in hosts where they do less harm.

Could a richer pond really protect frogs? The team tested the idea in the lab. When the parasite was added to tanks holding tadpoles of several species, the animals carried about half as many parasites as in tanks with a single species. In a related study, toad tadpoles raised alongside gray treefrogs had 37 percent less infection and fewer malformations. In other words, a pond full of different neighbors can work a little like a shield.

All of this matters because amphibians are already in trouble. A global assessment of more than 6,000 amphibian species found that 32 percent were threatened and 43 percent were declining. Habitat loss and a deadly fungal disease called chytrid have hit many species hard. Parasite deformities are only one part of that picture. But in ponds where they are common, they remove young frogs before they can grow up and breed, adding one more pressure to animals already under strain.

The story of the monster frogs teaches a powerful lesson. At first, it looked like a simple case of poison in the water. In the end, it involved farmers and fertilizer, algae and snails, flatworms and tadpoles, herons and hawks. Even the number of frog species in a pond played a part. When we change one strand of nature's web, the effect can travel through every other strand, in ways that are very hard to predict.

It is easy to see Ribeiroia as a horror story, and in a way it is. But it is also a remarkable piece of evolution. Without eyes and without a brain, this tiny flatworm moves through snails, tadpoles, and birds, and along the way it can reshape the body of a vertebrate. Nature does not judge whether that is cruel or clever. It only keeps what works. And for this parasite, the strategy has worked for a very long time.

So the next time you stand beside a quiet pond, look a little closer. Under that still surface, snails graze, tadpoles grow, and invisible parasites search for their next host. Most frogs you see will be perfectly normal. But each one has survived a world we are only beginning to understand.

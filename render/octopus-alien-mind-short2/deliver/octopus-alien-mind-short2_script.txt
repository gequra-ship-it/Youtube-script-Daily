===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

In a fraction of a second, an octopus can change its skin color, its pattern, and even its texture. It can look like swaying seaweed, a mossy rock, or a lump of coral so well that a diver can swim right past. How does it do that?

This relies on three kinds of cells in the skin. On top are chromatophores: small, elastic sacs of black, brown, red, orange, or yellow pigment, each ringed by tiny muscles. Beneath them lie iridophores, which work like living mirrors and reflect shimmering blues, greens, and golds. Deeper still are leucophores, which scatter light and add bright white patches.

Many animals change color slowly. Cephalopod camouflage is wired directly into the nervous system. When the brain decides to change color, nerve signals travel to the muscles around each chromatophore. Thousands of pigment sacs expand and contract together, a bit like the pixels on a screen.

Color alone rarely fools a sharp-eyed predator, so the octopus also changes the shape of its skin. Using muscles that raise small bumps called papillae, it can push the skin up into spikes or ridges in a fraction of a second, matching the rough texture of rocks or algae.

Here is the puzzle. Most octopuses appear to be colorblind. Their eyes have only one type of light-sensitive pigment, which in most animals means no color vision. Yet an octopus matches the colors around it very well. Scientists have debated this for a long time.

One idea, published in 2016, is a hypothesis based on computer models, and it has not been confirmed by experiments. It suggests that the odd U-shaped pupils of cuttlefish and squid may take advantage of chromatic aberration. Different colors of light focus at slightly different depths, so a shifting focus might reveal color.

Researchers also found something surprising. In 2015, a team reported that the skin of the California two-spot octopus contains opsins, the light-sensitive proteins found in eyes. Patches of skin responded to light in lab tests. The skin may sense light on its own, although scientists are still working out how important this is.

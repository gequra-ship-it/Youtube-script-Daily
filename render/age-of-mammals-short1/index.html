23 MILLION YEARS AGO
Did you know that
humble grass helped reshape
the teeth of ancient animals?
It sounds soft.
But for a hungry
herbivore, a mouthful of
grass can feel like
chewing sandpaper.
To see why,
we have to go
back to the Miocene
epoch, starting about
twenty-three million years
ago.
Over millions of years,
the world grew cooler
and drier.
Dense forests broke apart
into open woodlands.
In many places,
a new kind of
landscape spread across
the continents: grassland.
Wide, sunny, and open,
it looked like a
feast for any grazing animal.
But grass hides a defense.
Its tissues contain
microscopic particles of
silica, which is essentially
glass.
Every blade is full
of tiny, hard grains.
Chewing grass is like
grinding your teeth against
fine sandpaper.
And you cannot simply
destroy it.
Grass grows from the
base, right near the soil.
If an animal bites
off the top,
the grass simply grows back.
So grasslands are remarkably
resilient, and the grazers
keep chewing.
Animals with soft,
short teeth faced a problem.
Their enamel could grind
down quickly, and a
creature that cannot chew
cannot eat.
Natural selection favored
animals whose teeth could
last.
Horses and antelopes evolved
remarkably tall,
high-crowned teeth.
Hard enamel and softer
cementum wear down at
different speeds.
That uneven wear creates
self-sharpening ridges,
perfect for grinding grass.
Many scientists link these
tall teeth to the
gritty diet, though dust
and soil on the
leaves probably mattered too.
Open grassland changed
more than teeth.
A predator can spot
a grazing herd from
miles away across the
flat horizon.
So survival became a
high-speed arms race.
Herbivores lengthened their
lower leg bones to
stretch their running stride.
They rose onto the
tips of their toes,
and those toes became hooves.
Their side toes shrank
or fused into strong,
springy levers.
The earliest horses were
about the size of
a small dog,
with several toes on
each foot.
Predators answered with
longer legs, flexible
spines, and stealthy hunting
tactics.
Cats and dogs diversified,
refining the art of
the sudden, explosive chase.
Herds thundering across
open savannahs began to
look strikingly like the
modern world.
So the next time
you see a horse
grazing in a field,
look at its teeth
and its hooves.
Both are souvenirs from
the age of grass.

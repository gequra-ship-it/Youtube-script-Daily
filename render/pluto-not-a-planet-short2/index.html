Have you ever looked
up at the Moon
and realized it completely
outclasses Pluto?
When Pluto was found
in 1930, astronomers guessed
it might be a giant.
But as our technology
improved, the numbers told
a very different story.
Pluto measures roughly
2,376 kilometers across.
Earth's Moon measures about
3,474 kilometers.
That means our Moon
is much bigger than
Pluto, and Pluto's mass
is only about one
sixth of the Moon's.
In fact, there are
seven moons in our
solar system larger than
Pluto, including Jupiter's
Ganymede, Callisto, Io,
and Europa, Saturn's Titan,
and Neptune's Triton.
In 1931, scientists thought
Pluto might be as
massive as Earth.
By 1948, they thought
it was about as
massive as Mars.
Pluto is also small
compared to our own
country sizes.
If you laid it
across the continental
United States, it would
stretch only about halfway
from coast to coast.
Its whole surface has
about the same area as Russia.
Then, in 1978,
astronomer James Christy
noticed a strange bulge
on Pluto's images.
It was a moon,
later named Charon.
Charon is about half
as wide as Pluto,
and it circles Pluto
in just over six days.
The two worlds even
circle a point in
space that sits between them.
Scientists could finally
use that orbit to
calculate Pluto's true mass.
The answer was shocking.
Pluto has only about
two tenths of one
percent of the mass of Earth.
It had virtually no
gravitational influence
on the giant planets.
Gravity on Pluto's surface
is only about six
percent of Earth's,
so a person who
weighs seventy kilograms
here would weigh about
four kilograms there.
Sunlight takes about five
and a half hours
to reach it,
so Pluto looks frustratingly
faint from Earth.
How did a cosmic
lightweight stay on the
planet list for seventy-six
years?

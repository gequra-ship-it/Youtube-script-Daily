===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Thirty thousand years ago, people in Ice Age Europe pressed their hands against cave walls and blew pigment around them. Researchers studied more than five hundred of these stencils from twenty-six caves in France and Spain. About three quarters were left hands. What does that tell us about the artists?

It fits a simple picture. Most artists held the pigment in their right hand and pressed the free left hand against the rock. Even in the Ice Age, the right hand was usually the busy one. And the evidence goes back much further than art.

In a cave pit in northern Spain called the Sima de los Huesos, researchers studied the front teeth of early relatives of the Neanderthals, who lived more than four hundred thousand years ago. These people seem to have gripped meat between their teeth and sliced it with a stone tool. The angle of the tiny scratches shows which hand held the tool. In all fifteen individuals they studied, it was the right.

Neanderthal teeth tell the same story, and so do their arm bones, which grew thicker on the side that worked harder. Together, these clues suggest that roughly nine out of ten Neanderthals were right-handed, about the same share as people today. The ratio seems to be older than our own species.

Language kept a record of how people felt about the two hands. In Latin, the word for left was sinister, and in English it came to mean threatening or evil. The Latin word for right, dexter, gave us dexterity, meaning skill. In French, gauche means left, and it also means clumsy.

For a long time, that preference was enforced. Well into the twentieth century, many schools in Europe, North America and East Asia made left-handed children write with their right hand. Some teachers tied a child's left hand behind the chair, or rapped their knuckles with a ruler. Many grew up as switched right-handers.

Then, as the pressure eased, the numbers changed. Surveys of Americans born around 1900 found only about three percent who called themselves left-handed. Among people born in the second half of the century, the share climbed to roughly eleven or twelve percent. It wasn't a fashion spreading. Children were simply no longer being forced to hide it.

So the next time you see someone writing with their left hand, think of the long story behind it, from Ice Age cave walls to tied-down classroom chairs. The full story, and why the share stays near ten percent, is on Miya's Lab.

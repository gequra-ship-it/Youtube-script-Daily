Could you simply cook
the meat to make it safe?
Here is the most
unsettling part of the
whole story.
With most dangerous germs,
cooking works.
Salmonella and E.
coli are destroyed when
heat unravels their proteins.
A good kitchen is
usually enough.
So you might expect
the same for the
infection behind kuru.
But prions are different.
Normal cooking does not
reliably inactivate them.
They can survive the
chemicals used to preserve
tissue, such as formaldehyde.
And a standard hospital
sterilizing cycle is not
enough, which is why
hospitals use harsher
protocols after surgery
on a patient with
a prion disease.
How tough are they?
In a laboratory study
published in the year
2000, a team led
by Paul Brown heated
infected hamster brain
to six hundred degrees
Celsius.
A small amount of
infectivity still survived.
It did not survive
at one thousand degrees.
That was one experiment
on one strain,
but it shows how
stubborn prions can be.
Now think about your
immune system.
It usually spots invaders
by the foreign proteins
on their surface.
But a prion is
built from a protein
your own body makes.
So there is no useful defense.
No antibody response.
No fever.
No early warning.
The infection can travel
along nerves toward the
brain while you feel
completely well.
And it can wait.
Kuru has one of
the longest incubation
periods ever recorded.
A study of the
last patients found that
some fell ill about
forty years after exposure,
and the researchers
concluded that the delay
could exceed fifty years.
There is a related
story from Britain.
In the 1980s and
1990s, cattle were fed
meat-and-bone meal made
from the remains of
other animals, and a
prion disease spread through
the herds.
It was called mad cow disease.
In 1996, a human
form linked to it
was reported.
The lesson was clear:
feeding animals to their
own species is dangerous.
To be accurate,
eating a person is
not automatically a prion
infection.
The risk exists when
that person had a
prion disease.
But the danger is
invisible, and the delay
is so long that
no one could tell in time.
Nature's message was clear:
do not recycle your
own species through the
food chain.

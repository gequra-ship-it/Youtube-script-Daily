To unravel these puzzles,
NASA is building an
extraordinary rotorcraft
called Dragonfly, designed
to fly across Titan's
dunes and craters.
Because Titan has low
gravity and remarkably
thick air, flying there
takes far less effort
than flying on Earth.
Can you imagine the
first aerial footage sent
back from an alien moon?
The plan is for
the drone to travel
in flights across the
landscape, covering roughly
a hundred kilometers over
its mission, analyzing
the organic chemistry of
different terrains and
searching for chemical
signs of life.
It is expected to
reach Titan in the
mid twenty thirties.
Every sample could add
a new piece to the puzzle.
Is organic chemistry
something that tends to
turn into life whenever
the conditions allow?
Or is life a
one-time miracle that
happened here, and only here?
Titan will not answer
that question tomorrow,
but each careful measurement
narrows the possibilities.
That is how science
moves: by shrinking the
unknown one observation
at a time.
It is also a
story about patience.
Missions to the outer
solar system take years
to build and years
to travel, and the
reward is a view
of a place no
human has ever seen.
On Earth, a rotorcraft
must fight gravity and
thin air.
On Titan, the thick
air and low gravity
do much of the work.
That makes it a
rare place where a
flying robot can cover
a lot of ground,
visiting different kinds
of terrain and sampling
the chemistry of each.
The next time you
look up at Saturn,
remember the golden moon
beside it.
Perhaps life does not
require a warm,
sunny cradle with liquid
oceans like ours to take root.
Or perhaps it does,
and Titan will show us why.
Either answer would teach
us something profound about
what it means to be alive.

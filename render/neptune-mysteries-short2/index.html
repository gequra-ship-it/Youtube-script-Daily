Neptune receives almost
no heat from the Sun.
So who is driving the wind?
If you were to
descend into Neptune’s
sky, you would quickly
realize that its serene
beauty is an illusion.
Neptune is home to
the most ferocious and
terrifying winds recorded
anywhere in our entire
solar system.
Near the equator,
colossal jet streams blast
westward at speeds
approaching two thousand
kilometers per hour.
That is faster than
the speed of sound
here on Earth,
outstripping even the most
catastrophic hurricanes
our planet has ever seen.
What driving engine could
possibly fuel such
catastrophic windstorms
in the dead of space?
On Earth, atmospheric winds
are driven by solar
energy heating the equator
more than the poles,
creating swirling
circulation cells.
But at Neptune’s vast
distance, the incoming
energy from the Sun
is barely a whisper.
How can a planet
receiving less than one
percent of Earth’s solar
warmth generate winds
several times faster than
those on Jupiter?
At Neptune, the weakest
sunlight in the solar
system drives some of
its strongest winds.
To understand this paradox,
planetary scientists look
toward the strange physics
of frictionless fluid motion.
Neptune has no solid
surface to create drag
against its roaring
atmospheric tides.
Some researchers suspect
that because Neptune
receives so little solar
heat, its weather systems
encounter almost no
turbulence or friction.
Once a planetary jet
stream gets moving in
that super-cold environment,
nothing exists to slow
it down.
When Voyager 2 flew
past the planet,
it captured another
astonishing sight: a
colossal dark vortex known
as the Great Dark Spot.
This gigantic storm was
about as wide as
the entire planet Earth,
rotating counterclockwise
across the southern
hemisphere.
Surrounding this dark abyss
were companion clouds of
bright white methane ice
crystals, floating like
wisps of silk.
It looked like a
storm that could endure
for centuries, much like
Jupiter’s famous Great
Red Spot.
But when the Hubble
Space Telescope looked
toward Neptune in 1994,
the storm was gone.

===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Your banana is slightly radioactive. It sounds like an urban legend, but it is real science. Should you worry? Let us look at the numbers.

Bananas are rich in potassium, a mineral your cells, nerves, and heart need. About zero point zero one two percent of natural potassium is potassium-forty, an unstable isotope that slowly decays by itself. That decay is the source of the banana's small bit of radioactivity.

When potassium-forty decays, it gives off beta particles and some gamma rays. A banana therefore gives off a tiny, measurable amount of radiation. Large shipments of bananas can even set off radiation detectors at ports and borders, and officials then confirm it is just fruit.

People even use an informal measure called the banana equivalent dose to explain tiny amounts of radiation. One banana gives you roughly a tenth of a microsievert, by the usual estimate.

A lethal dose would take an absurd number of bananas, on the order of tens of millions at once, which is simply impossible. And because your body keeps potassium at a steady level, extra potassium is removed by your kidneys instead of piling up. For most healthy people, a banana is perfectly safe.

Bananas have another identity twist: botanically, a banana is a berry, but a strawberry is not. A true berry develops from a single ovary of one flower, with a skin, a fleshy middle layer, and an inner layer around the seeds. Strawberries and raspberries develop from flowers with many separate parts.

Why do bananas curve? The little fruits start out pointing down, then bend upward. Plant scientists explain this as negative gravitropism, a growth response that turns the fruit against gravity.

And a ripe banana in a paper bag releases ethylene, a plant hormone that can speed up a hard avocado or peach next to it. Brown speckles on the peel, often called sugar spots, mean the banana is very ripe and sweet. Watch the full deep dive on Miya's Lab for the secrets of the world's most familiar fruit.

Here is a question
that sounds simple.
How much data can
a black hole store?
Physicists examined the
mathematical boundary of
a black hole and
found something utterly
baffling about black hole
entropy and information
storage.
Think of entropy as
a measure of how
much information a black
hole can hold.
Now compare that with
everyday life.
Usually, the capacity of
an object to store
data depends directly on
its physical volume.
Think of a computer
hard drive.
More volume typically allows
more data storage.
A bigger drive,
with more room inside,
can hold more files.
If you want to
store more, you build
something larger.
That feels completely natural.
Yet theoretical
calculations, starting
with Jacob Bekenstein and
Stephen Hawking in the
nineteen-seventies, showed
that black hole information
scales strictly with surface
area.
Not volume.
Surface area.
Think of it this way.
If a house could
hold only as much
furniture as its outer
walls could cover,
no matter how big
the rooms were,
you would be very confused.
That is the kind
of surprise physicists
felt when they looked
at the numbers.
Imagine a drive whose
capacity did not depend
on how much room
it had inside,
but only on the
size of its outer skin.
That would be a
very strange drive.
And that is exactly
what the calculations said
about black holes.
All the data describing
everything inside appears
mathematically imprinted
onto the two-dimensional
event horizon.
The boundary holds every
single piece of information
about the three-dimensional
interior.
The inside, with all
its depth, seems to
be described by a
flat surface around it.
This startling realization
prompted physicists to
ask an even bolder question.
If a black hole
can store three dimensions
on a flat surface,
why not the whole universe?
Theoretical physicists
began exploring whether
our entire cosmos functions
as a cosmic projection.
Like the credit card
sticker, a flat surface
might carry a full
three-dimensional world.
That is a surprising
twist: the place that
holds the most information
may be defined by
its edge, not by its insides.
And then, in the
late nineteen-nineties,
the idea got a
powerful mathematical boost.

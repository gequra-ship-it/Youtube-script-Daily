Did you know that
Pluto does not even
circle the Sun on
the same level as
the other planets?
The eight major planets
glide in nearly flat
circles, like toy cars
rolling around on a
smooth floor.
Pluto completely shatters
this symmetry.
Its orbit is tilted
at an astonishing
seventeen-degree angle.
Even stranger, its path
is stretched into an
extreme oval.
At its closest,
Pluto is about four
and a half billion
kilometers from the Sun.
At its farthest,
it drifts out to
about seven and a
half billion.
It takes 248 years
to complete a single lap.
In fact, since its
discovery in 1930,
Pluto has not even
finished one full orbit.
It will do that
in the year 2178.
Pluto even spins on
its side, tilted about
120 degrees, so it
rotates backward compared
with most planets.
Pluto came closest to
the Sun in 1989.
Neptune's path is almost
a perfect circle,
but Pluto's oval path
cuts inside it.
Most planets keep to
their own lanes,
never crossing a neighbor's
path.
For twenty years,
between 1979 and 1999,
Pluto was closer to
the Sun than Neptune was.
It crossed its neighbor's
path like a cosmic rebel.
Does that mean the
two worlds could collide?
No.
Pluto and Neptune are
locked in a pattern
called a resonance.
Pluto circles the Sun
twice while Neptune circles
three times, so whenever
Pluto crosses Neptune's
path, the two are far apart.
They never get closer
than about two and
a half billion kilometers.
For decades, astronomers
called Pluto an eccentric
exception.
But was it really
a planet at all,
or the first clue
of a hidden realm?

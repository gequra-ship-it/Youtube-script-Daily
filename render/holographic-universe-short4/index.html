In the late
nineteen-nineties,
researchers discovered
a remarkable mathematical
duality linking two
different worlds.
Picture two descriptions
of reality that look
nothing alike.
The first world has
gravity in it,
with all its curving
and pulling.
The second is a
boundary where gravity
does not appear at all.
One has depth.
The other has one
dimension less.
And yet, detailed
calculations showed that
a universe with gravity
can be mathematically
equivalent to a boundary
without gravity.
What does equivalent mean
here?
Every gravitational event
happening in the interior
maps directly to quantum
interactions on the outer
edge.
When something falls,
bends, or collides in
the interior, there is
a matching quantum event
on the edge.
Imagine reading the same
novel in English and
then in French.
The plot, the characters,
and the ending are
all the same.
A reader who knows
both languages can pick
whichever one makes a
question easier to answer.
Both descriptions are
mathematically identical,
just written in completely
different physical languages.
The words are different,
the grammar is different,
but the story is the same.
Physicists can read the
story in the language
of gravity, or in
the language of quantum
interactions on a boundary,
and they get the same answers.
It is worth pausing
on how strange this
equivalence is.
If two descriptions are
mathematically identical,
then neither one is
the truth while the
other is only a trick.
They are two ways
of saying the same thing.
Does this mean our
universe is exactly such
a hologram?
No.
The universe in that
mathematical example has
a special saddle-shaped
geometry, known as anti-de
Sitter space, and our
own universe is not
of that kind.
Whether the idea describes
our own universe is
still an open question.
But think about what
it suggests.
If two such different
pictures can describe the
same reality, then maybe
the depth and gravity
we experience is only
one way of reading
a deeper description.
Sometimes, to understand
a world, you need
to learn a second language.

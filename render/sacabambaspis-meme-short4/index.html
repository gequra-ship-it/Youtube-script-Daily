450 MILLION YEARS AGO
Around thirty fish lie
packed into one small
patch of Bolivian rock.
Something killed them fast.
Those fish are
Sacabambaspis, a jawless
fish about twenty-five
centimeters long that lived
in Ordovician seas roughly
450 million years ago.
Their fossils come from
the Anzaldo Formation near
Cochabamba.
Most fossil fish are
found alone, as scattered
plates.
Here, many of the
specimens are articulated,
which means their bones
are still lying together,
as they did in life.
Living fish do not
die in neat piles.
Something unusual happened.
A study published in
2007, led by Davies,
looked at the rocks
and proposed an explanation.
The fish, and a
group of shelled animals
called brachiopods, were
probably killed by floods
of fresh water and
surges of mud,
which buried them quickly
in shallow water near
the coast.
Think about what that means.
Sea animals live in
salt water.
If a flood of
fresh water pours into
a shallow bay,
the water around them
changes fast.
At the same time,
mud can bury animals alive.
Sudden burial is the key.
It protected the bodies
from scavengers and decay,
and that is how
a fragile fish became
a fossil.
Some writers suggest a
big storm was the trigger.
That part is likely,
but it is still
an interpretation, not
a proven fact.
We cannot go back and watch.
Here is where I
need to be careful.
You may read that
all the fish point
in the same direction
because of ocean currents.
I could not find
a source for that,
so I am not
going to tell you
it is true.
What we can say
is that the rocks
show a rapid burial
of animals that lived
near the shore.
Look at what a
fossil like this gives us.
Most animals die and
disappear.
A few are buried
before anything can touch
them.
For Sacabambaspis, one
terrible day for the
fish became a gift
for scientists, and a
snapshot of an ocean
floor from around 450
million years ago.
One storm, one moment,
and a whole world
was frozen in rock.

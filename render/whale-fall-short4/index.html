When the big scavengers
leave, you might think
the story is over.
In fact, the strangest
chapter has not even started.
But the real prize
remains locked inside the
skeleton.
Whale bones are porous
and spongelike, and inside
them lies a huge
reservoir of fats.
By some estimates,
these lipids can make
up as much as
about six percent of
the whale's original body
weight.
The fats are sealed
inside the bone,
out of reach of
ordinary scavengers.
Unlocking this hidden vault
takes a very different
kind of life.
This brings us to
the third and most
chemically miraculous stage,
the sulfophilic, or
sulfur-loving stage.
It can last for
decades, with estimates
reaching eighty years or
more for the largest
whales, which makes it
the longest chapter of
a whale fall.
Deep inside the bone,
bacteria that live without
oxygen break down the
fats using sulfate dissolved
in seawater.
Their waste product is
hydrogen sulfide, a toxic gas.
For most animals,
hydrogen sulfide is a poison.
But on the abyssal
plain, it is fuel.
Chemosynthetic bacteria
coat the skeleton in
thick, pale mats,
using the sulfide to
make energy without a
single drop of sunlight.
This turns a dead
whale into an ecosystem
similar to deep-sea
hydrothermal vents and
cold seeps, a tiny
chemical oasis in the
middle of a barren desert.
On the surface of
our planet, almost all
life runs on sunlight.
Plants and algae capture
it, and animals eat them.
Chemosynthesis flips that
story.
Instead of sunlight,
these bacteria use the
energy stored in chemicals,
like hydrogen sulfide.
Animals that cannot make
their own food simply
partner with the bacteria,
giving them shelter in
exchange for a steady
supply of energy.
Some biologists think whale
falls play another role:
stepping stones.
Hydrothermal vents can
be separated by hundreds
or even thousands of
kilometers of barren
seafloor, and many vent
animals struggle to cross
such distances as larvae.
One hypothesis is that
whale falls, scattered
across the deep sea,
offer resting spots along
the way, helping vent
animals spread across ocean
basins over millions of years.
The idea is still debated.
Death can be a bridge.
In this case,
it may even be
a bridge across an ocean.

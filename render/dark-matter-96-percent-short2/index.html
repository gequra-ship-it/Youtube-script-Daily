Dark matter
How did we realize
that most of reality
was missing?
The answer came from
astronomers who looked
closely at how spiral
galaxies rotate.
A spiral galaxy is
a great wheel of
stars, turning slowly around
its center.
According to the established
laws of physics,
stars near the outer
rim of a galaxy
should move much slower
than stars near the
crowded center.
It works just like
our solar system,
where distant planets drift
lazily while inner worlds
race around the Sun.
The farther you are
from the center of
the mass, the weaker
the pull, and the
slower you need to
move to stay in orbit.
But when scientists measured
the actual speed of
the outer stars,
they encountered a profound
shock.
The outer stars were
moving astonishingly fast.
In fact, these stars
were moving so rapidly
that the visible gravity
of the galaxy could
not possibly hold them.
Think of a ball
whirled on a string.
If you spin it
too fast, the string
cannot hold, and the
ball flies away.
By all ordinary logic,
these swirling galaxies
should have torn themselves
apart and flung their
stars into the dark
void long ago.
Yet, they remain beautifully
intact.
Something is holding them
together.
Scientists suspect that
an immense, invisible halo
of mysterious mass surrounds
each galaxy, providing
the extra gravitational
glue needed to hold
everything together.
This is a hypothesis
built to explain what
we observe, not a
substance anyone has held
in their hands.
Because this substance
emits no light,
absorbs no light,
and reflects no light,
we call it dark matter.
Notice the careful words
in all of this.
A name is not an explanation.
Imagine a detective who
finds footprints in the
snow but never sees
the person who made them.
Dark matter is like
those footprints.
We read its effect
on the stars,
and we reason about
what could have left
such a mark.
No one needed a
dramatic moment to find it.
It was just a
star turning a little
too fast, and a
notebook full of numbers
that refused to agree
with expectations.

MORE THAN 30
460 MILLION YEARS AGO
One Bolivian fossil site
holds a remarkable crowd.
More than thirty of
these armored fish were
found packed into a
very small area,
together with the shells
of small animals called
lingulid brachiopods.
This looks like a
mass death, probably caused
by a powerful storm.
It gives us a
rare snapshot of a
single bad day,
about four hundred and
sixty million years ago.
Picture that day,
as the researchers do.
A storm probably built
over the coast,
and rain poured into
the rivers.
A flood of muddy
fresh water rushed out
into the bay,
and the fish,
adapted to the sea,
could not cope.
Then sediment settled over
them and buried them
quickly, which is exactly
what a fossil needs.
Fossils are not photographs.
Rock presses on them
for hundreds of millions
of years, and shells
and plates get flattened
or broken.
A palaeontologist has to
work out the original
shape from the pieces,
and they do it
by comparing many specimens.
A reconstruction is the
best guess from the
evidence, and a different
artist could make a
different guess.
Sacabambaspis was not alone.
In central Australia,
in a layer called
the Stairway Sandstone
near Alice Springs,
scientists found Arandaspis,
a close relative.
It had the same
kind of head shield
and the same missing jaw.
Fossils from Bolivia,
Australia, Argentina, and
Oman suggest that these
fish lived along the
shallow margins of the
same ancient supercontinent.
Then came one of
the biggest changes in
vertebrate history.
Most researchers think
the jaw grew out
of the first of
the supporting arches that
held up the gills.
Over time, those arches
turned into hinged jaws
that could grasp,
bite, and crush.
Today almost every backboned
animal you can name has them.
It is important to
be careful here.
Sacabambaspis is not our
grandparent.
It belongs to a
side branch of the
family tree, a cousin
that left no descendants.
What we share with
it is a deep
ancestry among early
vertebrates.
The mineral that makes
your bones and tooth
enamel, calcium phosphate,
was already being used
in armor like this.
Sacabambaspis also helps
correct a common picture.
Evolution is not a
ladder with fish at
the bottom and humans
at the top.
It is more like
a bush, with many
branches, side paths,
and endings.
Being on a branch
that ends does not
make a species a failure.

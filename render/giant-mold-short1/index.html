What if I told
you that before trees
existed, the tallest living
things on dry land
were giant spires that
looked like mold?
Over four hundred million
years ago, during the
Silurian and Early Devonian
periods, our planet looked
completely alien.
If you could step
back into that silent
world, you would not
see a single tall
tree, leafy bush,
or blooming flower.
The earliest plants were
tiny, creeping stems that
barely reached your ankles.
There were no animals
with backbones on land
yet, and nothing bigger
than a small creeping
creature moved across the
ground.
In a world this
empty, anything that stood
tall would have stopped
you in your tracks.
But towering over these
miniature green carpets
were stone-colored pillars
rising eight meters straight
into the sky.
Their trunks were up
to a meter wide
at the base,
standing erect against
the wind.
If you stood next
to one, you would
have to look up
at something more than
four times your height.
Picture two double-decker
buses stacked on top
of each other,
made of pale, stony tissue.
In the nineteenth century,
scientists thought these
fossils were the wood
of ancient conifers.
But under the microscope,
something staggering appeared.
There were no wood
cells and no growth rings.
Instead, the inside was
made of densely woven
microscopic tubes, much
like the body of a fungus.
So it was not
a tree, and it
was not a normal plant.
It was something that
did not match anything
we know growing today.
This ancient titan is
known as Prototaxites,
and for roughly forty
million years it was
the tallest thing on land.
While modern mushrooms
hide quietly on the
damp forest floor,
this monster stood like
a tower over the continents.
But how did a
fungus grow taller than
a house, without leaves
or sunlight?

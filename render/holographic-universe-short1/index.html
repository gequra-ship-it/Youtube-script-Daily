Hologram
Event horizon
Planck units
Look at the room around you.
Every wall, every shadow,
every inch of empty
space feels deep and solid.
But some physicists ask
a startling question.
What if all of
that depth is described
by information written
on a flat surface?
You already own a
small version of this trick.
Many credit cards carry
a hologram.
A flat piece of
plastic holds what looks
like a three-dimensional
image, and as you
tilt it, you see
the object from different
angles.
Dennis Gabor invented
holography in nineteen
forty-seven, and won the
Nobel Prize in Physics
for it in nineteen
seventy-one.
Now picture a black hole.
Its edge is called
the event horizon,
the point of no return.
In the early nineteen
seventies, Jacob Bekenstein
proposed that a black
hole carries entropy,
a measure of hidden
detail, proportional to
the area of that horizon.
In nineteen seventy-four,
Stephen Hawking fixed the
exact value: the area
divided by four,
in tiny Planck units.
Here is what makes
that strange.
A hard drive holds
more data when it
has more volume.
But for a black
hole, the count of
hidden detail seems to
depend on the area
of its skin,
not the space inside.
It is as if
the contents are described
by a flat recording
on the horizon.
In nineteen ninety-three,
the Dutch physicist Gerard
't Hooft suggested that
gravity in a region
might need only as
many pieces of information
as fit on its boundary.
In nineteen ninety-five,
Leonard Susskind gave the
idea its name,
the holographic principle,
in a paper called
The World as a Hologram.
There is a neat
reason it might be true.
Pack more and more
information into a sphere
of space, and the
energy needed eventually
makes it collapse into
a black hole.
So no region can
hold more entropy than
a black hole of
the same size.
Now the honest part.
This does not mean
you are a picture.
It is a statement
about how much information
describes a region of space.
It has not been
proven for our universe,
and nobody has shown
that the room around
you is a projection.
Still, the idea has
already taught physicists
something real: space and
information may be far
more closely connected
than anyone imagined.

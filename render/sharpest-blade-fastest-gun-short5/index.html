Yet, for physicists probing
the deepest foundations
of the cosmos,
even eight kilometers per
second is practically
standing still.
If you truly want
to build the ultimate
gun, you cannot settle
for launching chunks of
metal or plastic pellets.
You must strip matter
down to its most
fundamental ingredients
and shoot them with
the help of the
fundamental forces of the
universe.
This brings us to
the grandest scientific
instrument ever constructed:
the Large Hadron Collider.
Buried a hundred meters
beneath the serene French
and Swiss countryside,
curving in a giant
circular ring twenty-seven
kilometers around, lies
the crown jewel of
particle physics.
It is easy to
think of the Large
Hadron Collider at CERN
as an observatory or
a giant microscope.
But if you strip
away the technical jargon,
what is it fundamentally
doing?
It takes subatomic
particles, loads them into
a barrel, accelerates them
with electromagnetic fields,
and fires them directly
at a target.
Inside this subterranean
vacuum ring, twin pipes
are chilled by liquid
helium down to minus
two hundred and seventy-one
degrees Celsius, a
temperature colder than
the empty space between
the galaxies.
Thousands of massive
superconducting
electromagnets steer and
squeeze beams of protons,
the atomic cores of hydrogen.
Radiofrequency cavities
act like relentless cosmic
kicks, pushing these protons
faster and faster with
every single lap around
the twenty-seven-kilometer
track.
By the time the
proton beam reaches its
full operational energy,
those subatomic bullets
are traveling at ninety-nine
point nine, nine,
nine, nine, nine,
nine, one percent of
the speed of light.
At this unimaginable
velocity, a single proton
can circle the entire
twenty-seven-kilometer
ring more than eleven
thousand times in a
single second.
It comes within a
hair of the cosmic
speed limit dictated by
Albert Einstein's special
theory of relativity.
At the exact point
of impact, the energy
density becomes so
concentrated that it briefly
recreates conditions like
those of the early
universe, mere fractions
of a second after
the Big Bang.
In that tiny,
incandescent speck of
spacetime, ordinary matter
dissolves.
Protons shatter, and the
raw kinetic energy of
the collision crystallizes
back into new,
exotic particles that rarely
exist in nature today,
such as the Higgs boson.

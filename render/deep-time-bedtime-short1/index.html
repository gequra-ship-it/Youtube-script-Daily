4 BILLION YEARS AGO
Imagine a voyage back
to the Hadean Eon,
more than four billion
years ago.
The young Earth was
not the tranquil blue
marble we know today,
but an incandescent sphere
of glowing magma.
Meteorites hammered the
molten surface in continuous
cascades of violent fire,
keeping the crust in
a churning liquid state.
The sky above was
a choking shroud of
carbon dioxide, sulfur,
and dense water vapor,
completely devoid of
breathable oxygen.
According to leading
scientific models, a
wandering celestial body
roughly the size of
Mars collided with our
proto-Earth, hurling
colossal plumes of debris
into orbit that gradually
coalesced into our Moon.
What would it feel
like to stand on
the edge of that
newborn crust?
Over tens of millions
of years, the relentless
bombardment began to slow,
and the surface cooled
into dark basalt rock.
As the volcanic skies
thickened with condensed
steam, the clouds finally
broke open.
Scientists suspect it began
to rain, not as
a gentle spring shower,
but as a torrential,
planet-wide deluge that
lasted for centuries.
Water poured from the
dark heavens, pooling into
the low basins between
steaming volcanic islands,
giving birth to Earth’s
very first oceans.
As we cross the
boundary into the Archean
Eon, about four billion
years ago, the boiling
seas began to cool.
Scientists suspect they
turned an iron-rich green
beneath a hazy orange sky.
Could anything survive
in such an alien furnace?
Deep beneath the surface,
along the midnight cracks
of the ocean floor,
hydrothermal vents spewed
superheated mineral soup
into the dark water.
One leading hypothesis
says that in tiny
pores, shielded from lethal
solar radiation, simple
organic molecules began
to fold, copy themselves,
and stir with the
quiet spark of life.
These first living
inhabitants were microscopic
single-celled organisms,
drifting through the warm,
sulfurous currents.
Eventually, in the sunlit
shallows, some of these
tiny pioneers learned to
harness the light of
the young sun through
early photosynthesis.
They clustered together
in colonial mats,
trapping sediment and
building mounded stone
cushions known as
stromatolites.
If you were to
walk along the primitive
coastlines of the Archean
world, you would hear
only the gentle lap
of the waves against
these quiet stony domes.
For billions of years,
these humble microbes worked
in total obscurity,
completely unaware that
their quiet work was
setting the stage for
everything to come.

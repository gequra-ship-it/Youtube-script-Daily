Ice grows over a
giant continent, and the
shallow sea around a
small armored fish drains
away.
That was the world
at the end of
the Ordovician Period,
about 444 million years ago.
Sacabambaspis, a jawless
fish about twenty-five
centimeters long, lived
shortly before it,
roughly 450 million years ago.
We do not know
exactly when the last
Sacabambaspis died.
But we know what
happened to the world
it lived in.
Glaciers grew over the
huge southern landmass
called Gondwana.
Sea levels dropped,
and the shallow seas
where many animals lived
shrank away.
Then, as the ice
later melted, parts of
the ocean ran low on oxygen.
Together, these changes
helped cause one of
the biggest mass extinctions
in Earth's history.
Roughly eighty-five percent
of marine species disappeared.
Did any jawless fish
make it to the present day?
Yes, but not armored ones.
Lampreys and hagfish are
the only living jawless fish.
They have slimy,
soft, eel-like bodies,
and no bony armor.
Some scientists think their
ancestors lost armor along
the way, while others
think they never had it.
They are not descendants
of Sacabambaspis.
They are distant cousins
that show what a
body without jaws looks
like today.
A lamprey has a
round, toothed suction
mouth that latches onto
its prey.
Meanwhile, another group
had found a new trick.
Scientists think that parts
of the gill supports,
the bones that hold
up the gills,
were reshaped into hinged
jaws.
That change gave rise
to the jawed vertebrates,
which include sharks,
bony fish, and us.
By the Devonian Period,
more than sixty million
years after Sacabambaspis,
armored jawed fish called
placoderms ruled the seas.
The most famous,
Dunkleosteus, had bony
plates in its mouth
that worked like blades.
For years, people said
it grew to nine
meters or more.
A 2023 study suggests
a typical adult was
only about three and
a half meters long.
Still a very dangerous animal.
Sacabambaspis was not our
great-grandparent.
It was a member
of a side branch
of the family tree
of vertebrates.
But the traits that
animals like it show
us, including bone and
a head with sense
organs, were built piece
by piece over tens
of millions of years.
Even the spine you
touch today has an old story.

Have you ever heard
of terminal burrowing?
It is one of
the most unsettling
behaviors documented in
forensic medicine.
When a person reaches
the final stages of
severe hypothermia,
something strange can happen
to their behavior.
As clear thinking shuts
down, some victims seem
to crawl into tiny,
enclosed spaces.
Investigators have found
them wedged beneath beds
or tucked behind wardrobes
and furniture.
It has also been
called the hide-and-die
syndrome.
In one forensic study
of fatal hypothermia cases,
roughly one in five
victims showed signs of
this behavior.
It looks very much
like the instinct of
a hibernating animal seeking
shelter from the winter cold.
Some researchers suspect
that primitive reflexes
deep in the brain
take control when the
thinking parts of the
brain stop working.
In other words,
it may be an
ancient survival program
that switches on when
everything else fails.
It is only a
hypothesis, because no
one can interview the
victims afterward.
It may also help
explain why searchers
sometimes fail to find
people who vanished in
cold weather, since the
person may be hiding
in a tight space
instead of lying in the open.
This behavior is often
found together with
paradoxical undressing,
the false sense of
burning heat that makes
freezing people remove
their clothes.
Together, they show how
a brain that is
losing its battle against
the cold can behave
in ways that look irrational.
Strange as it sounds,
the same instinct protects
many animals.
Mice, squirrels, and bears
all seek out burrows
and dens before winter,
and some animals lower
their body temperature
on purpose to save energy.
Humans cannot do that,
but we seem to
carry traces of the
same urge to hide
from the cold.
Even after memory and
identity have faded,
the body seems to
make one last,
frantic attempt to find
shelter.

Because it emits no
light, absorbs no light,
and reflects no light,
we simply call it dark matter.
The word dark does
not mean a dark
color, like black paint.
It means invisible to
every kind of light,
so no telescope can see it.
We can only notice
what it does.
What could dark matter
actually be?
It accounts for roughly
27 percent of the
entire universe, dwarfing
everything we know by
more than five times.
For every bit of
ordinary matter in stars
and planets and people,
there is more than
five times as much
of this mysterious substance.
Evidence strongly suggests
it is not made
of protons, neutrons,
or electrons.
Those are the ingredients
of everything we know.
If a cloud of
dark matter passed through
your living room right
now, you would not
see it, hear it,
or feel it touch your skin.
It interacts with our
world almost entirely
through gravity.
That is the only
handle we have on it.
Dark matter tugs on
stars and galaxies,
and we notice the tugging.
Leading hypotheses suggest
it may consist of
undiscovered subatomic
particles that barely
interact with normal matter
at all, but no
one knows for sure.
Honest science says it
plainly: we know where
it seems to be,
and we do not
know what it is.
Picture an invisible ocean
in which the whole
galaxy floats.
You cannot see the water.
But you can watch
how the ship moves,
and you know something
is there.
Remember what kind of
knowledge this is.
Dark matter is not
something captured in a
laboratory.
It is a gap
between careful measurements
of how galaxies spin
and the visible matter
we can count.
The name is given
to the difference.
That is the honest summary.
We know roughly how
much seems to be there.
We know where it
seems to gather,
around galaxies, pulling
inward like glue.
And we do not
know what it is made of.

the first piece of
the missing universe.

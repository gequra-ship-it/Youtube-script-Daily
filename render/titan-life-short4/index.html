Titan has another secret
waiting deep beneath its
icy crust.
Gravity measurements and
the way the moon
flexes under Saturn's pull
strongly suggest that a
global ocean of liquid
water lies buried underground.
While the surface is
locked in a methane-drenched
deep freeze, this hidden
ocean may be kept
from freezing by heat
from the moon's interior
and by the constant
squeezing and stretching
of tides.
In other words,
Titan may offer two
completely different
potential habitats for
life on a single world.
An exotic hydrocarbon
environment might exist
on its surface,
while familiar, water-based
microbial life could,
in principle, exist in
the dark ocean below.
Where else in the
solar system can you
find such a combination?
Notice how this changes
the way we think
about the moon.
A single world with
two possible hiding places
for life.
On top, an exotic
frozen landscape of liquid
fuel.
Beneath, a dark ocean
of liquid water.
Why can an ocean
stay liquid in a
place so cold?
Saturn's gravity keeps
squeezing and stretching
the moon, and that
flexing generates heat.
A world does not
need a bright sun
to be warm inside.
Energy can come from
the pull of a giant neighbor.
The evidence for that
ocean comes from measuring
how the moon flexes
and how its gravity
behaves, and the word
scientists use is suggest.
That is an honest word.
It means the measurements
point strongly toward an
ocean, even though no
one has dipped a
probe into it.
Now picture the surface
above it: an eerie,
orange marble wrapped in
a thick golden haze,
with air pressure about
one and a half
times Earth's, and a
temperature near minus
one hundred and eighty
degrees Celsius.
Beneath all that cold,
a hidden sea.
A moon with a
hidden ocean and a
surface of liquid fuel
is a rare combination.
It gives scientists two
very different places to
look, and it reminds
us that the unfamiliar
is rarely as simple
as it first appears.

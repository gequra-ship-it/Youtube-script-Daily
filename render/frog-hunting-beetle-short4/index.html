You might think the
bug grows up and
moves on to an easier diet.
It does not.
The adult is a
hunter of amphibians too.
After the larva has
fed, molted, and pupated
in the soil,
an adult Epomis beetle
comes out.
It has a hard
shell and long legs,
and it runs across
the ground at night.
Adults and amphibians often
share the same damp
shelters by day,
and the beetles act
when it gets dark.
We should be careful
about one thing.
The 2011 study describes
adults as generalist
predators that eat many
kinds of food,
including amphibians.
So we cannot say
that adult Epomis eat
only frogs.
What we can say
is that they do attack them.
In the laboratory,
the researchers saw an
adult jump onto the
back of an amphibian.
The beetle used its
jaws to make a
cut in the back.
The victim became paralyzed
within a few minutes.
Within hours, the adult
had eaten most of
its prey, working from
the legs up,
until only a head
and limbs were left.
These details come from
reports on the study,
and the authors describe
adult hunting as early-stage
research.
How the bite works
is not fully explained.
In the wild,
Wizen and Gasith found
three adult beetles that
were eating toads from behind.
That fits what happened
in the lab.
But three beetles is
a small sample.
Nobody can yet say
how often adults do
this outside the laboratory.
Why from behind?
A frog's best weapon
is in front of it.
The tongue, the jump,
and the sharp eyes
all work best on
things that appear ahead.
An attacker on its
back is in a
place that is harder
to deal with.
That is a reasonable
idea, but it is
only an idea,
not a proven reason.
And what about the
poison in toads?
Many toads carry defensive
chemicals in their skin.
It would be tempting
to say that Epomis is immune.
But the original paper
does not report on
toxin tests.
So the honest answer
is that we do
not know how these
beetles deal with toad poison.
The frog's best defense
is its legs.
The adult beetle seems
to know exactly where to bite.

When we turn our
telescopes toward the
farthest reaches of the
night sky, we encounter
the most staggering
realization of all.
The universe is filled
with billions of galaxies,
and almost all of
them are moving away from us.
In the early twentieth
century, astronomers
realized that the farther
away a galaxy is,
the faster it appears
to be receding.
For the most distant
galaxies visible in our
deep-space observatories,
their apparent recession
speed is not just
half the speed of
light, or ninety percent
of the speed of light.
They are moving away
from us faster than
light itself.
At first, this sounds
like an undeniable violation
of everything special
relativity stands for.
If nothing can move
faster than light,
how can an entire
galaxy containing hundreds
of billions of stars
be traveling faster than
299,792 kilometers per second?
The secret lies in
a subtle but crucial
distinction made by Einstein
in his General Theory
of Relativity.
The speed limit applies
to objects moving through
space, but it does
not apply to the
expansion of space itself.
Imagine baking a loaf
of raisin bread in
your kitchen oven.
As the dough warms
and rises, it expands
uniformly in every direction.
If you observe two
raisins that were initially
close together, they move
apart slowly as the
dough stretches between them.
But if you observe
two raisins located on
opposite ends of the
loaf, the amount of
dough expanding between
them is far greater.
From the perspective of
either raisin, the other
seems to be flying
away at a tremendous
speed, even though neither
raisin is actually crawling
through the bread.
In our cosmos,
the galaxies are like
those raisins, and spacetime
itself is the expanding dough.
Galaxies are not rocketing
through the void at
impossible speeds, propelled
by some unfathomable engine.
Instead, the space between
them keeps stretching.
Because the expansion of
space adds up across
vast cosmic distances,
the cumulative rate of
expansion across billions
of light-years easily
outpaces the speed of light.
The light emitted by
those distant galaxies
is stretched into longer,
redder wavelengths,
eventually fading into
the deep infrared.

The last chapter of
a whale fall can
last for a very
long time, and it
begins long after the
chemical party ends.
Decades pass.
Eventually, the fats and
sulfur compounds in the
skeleton run out.
The bacteria and worms,
starved of fuel,
slowly die off.
Yet the whale fall
still shapes the deep
sea, through what scientists
call the reef stage.
Even after the last
nourishment has been used
up, the bare mineral
skeleton remains on the
muddy seafloor.
On the abyssal plains,
hard surfaces are rare.
The ground is mostly
soft mud and silt.
Animals that need something
solid to hold on
to, such as corals,
sponges, anemones, and
crinoids, have very few
places to settle.
A whale skull or
rib cage can become
one of them,
a small natural reef
where filter feeders attach
and lift themselves above
the mud.
Whale falls may also
matter for the climate.
A whale collects a
great deal of carbon
in its body over
a life that can
last many decades.
When it sinks to
the deep seafloor,
that carbon is carried
far from the surface
and can stay buried
in the deep for
a very long time.
Scientists call this natural
conveyor the biological
carbon pump, and whales
are one small part of it.
But this ancient system
was disrupted.
Industrial whaling in the
twentieth century killed
an estimated 2.9 million
great whales, according
to a 2014 study
by Rocha and colleagues.
Whales processed on ships
or on land never
sank to the seafloor,
so the deep lost its feasts.
Some scientists suspect
that whaling removed a
large share of natural
whale falls, though the
exact number is uncertain.
For animals that depend
on whale falls,
this may have been
devastating.
There is a hopeful
note, however.
Commercial whaling was
put on hold by
a global moratorium that
began in 1986,
and some whale populations
have been slowly recovering
since.
As their numbers grow,
more whales will die
naturally and sink,
and the deep seafloor
may slowly see more
of these ancient feasts
return.
One life gives up
its form, so that
many unseen, wondrous
creatures can thrive.

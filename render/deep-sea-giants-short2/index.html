For centuries, sailors
whispered about the kraken,
a sea monster that
could drag wooden ships
beneath the waves.
The legends were exaggerated.
But the real animals
behind them are hardly
less astonishing.
Meet the colossal squid.
It lives in the
freezing waters around
Antarctica, and it has
some of the largest
eyes in the entire
animal kingdom.
A single eye has
been measured at about
twenty-seven centimeters
across.
That is roughly the
size of a large dinner plate.
Why would any animal
need an eye that big?
Think about where it lives.
Below one thousand meters,
virtually every trace of
sunlight is gone.
This is the midnight
zone, a place of
perpetual shadow and biting
cold.
The only light is
the faint flicker of
bioluminescence, living
creatures making their
own glow.
In a world like
that, an eye is
a bucket for catching
almost nothing.
The bigger the eye,
the more of those
faint flickers it can collect.
Researchers explain that
huge eyes like these
help detect the faintest
disturbances in the water.
And one of the
things it may need
to spot is a threat.
Studies of the giant
squid suggest that big
eyes may help spot
something like the distant
shadow of a hunting
sperm whale, moving through
the dark.
The colossal squid is
not alone in being enormous.
Its relative, the giant
squid, also glides through
the deep, and both
can reach lengths that
rival a city bus.
The giant isopod grows
to about forty centimeters.
The Japanese spider crab
stretches its legs nearly
four meters from tip to tip.
Scientists call this pattern
deep-sea gigantism, the
tendency of certain marine
species to grow far
larger than their
shallow-water relatives.
On land, huge animals
like elephants depend on
endless plains of grass.
Down in the eternal
night, the rules of
life seem turned upside down.
Why does it happen?
Researchers point to several
things that may work together.
Cold water slows the
body's chemistry.
Large bodies use energy
more efficiently.
Cold, oxygen-rich water
may remove a ceiling on size.
And there may be
a defensive bonus,
because past a certain
size you no longer
fit inside an enemy's mouth.
These are hypotheses,
not final proof,
because studying animals
that live thousands of
meters down is
extraordinarily difficult.
Still, the next time
you imagine a sea
monster, remember that
the real thing is
quieter, stranger and may
be watching you with
an eye the size
of a dinner plate.

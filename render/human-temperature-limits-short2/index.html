Why do rescuers sometimes
find freezing victims partly
undressed in the snow?
When temperatures drop
to dangerous levels,
severe hypothermia can
cause a bizarre and
tragic behavior known as
paradoxical undressing.
In one forensic study
of fatal hypothermia cases,
about one in four
victims was found with
clothing removed.
For decades, this baffling
scene puzzled investigators.
How could someone freezing
to death take off
their clothes in a
sub-zero blizzard?
One widely accepted
hypothesis points to a
failure of the brain's
temperature control.
In the cold,
your body narrows the
blood vessels in your
arms and legs to
keep warm blood around
your vital organs.
That is why your
hands and feet go
pale and numb first.
But as the core
temperature drops very
low, the muscles in
those vessel walls tire
and may suddenly relax.
A wave of warm
blood from the core
rushes outward to the skin.
To a confused,
exhausted person, that
rush can create a
powerful false sensation
of burning heat.
Believing they are
overheating, they tear
off their clothes.
It is a cruel
trick of biology,
because every layer they
remove makes the cold
even more lethal.
Investigators have also
noted that people in
this state are often
confused and unable to
think clearly, so the
scene can look like
a struggle or even
a crime when it is not.
Understanding the mechanism
has helped doctors and
rescuers interpret these
scenes correctly.
Think about how strange
this is.
Hypothermia does not always
feel like freezing.
As the brain slows
down, the victim may
feel warm, sleepy,
and calm instead of
afraid, and the usual
alarm signals of pain
and shivering fade away.
That is why the
danger can be so
easy to miss,
even for the person
living through it.
This behavior is a
hypothesis, not a fully
proven fact, and researchers
are still studying exactly
how it happens.
But it shows how
deeply the body's thermostat
shapes what we feel,
even when what we
feel is wrong.

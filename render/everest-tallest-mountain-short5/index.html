Which is the tallest
known mountain in the
Solar System?
To find it,
we must leave Earth
and turn our telescopes
toward the red deserts
of Mars.
Olympus Mons is a
planetary wonder that defies
easy comprehension.
It towers approximately
twenty-one to twenty-two
kilometers above the
surrounding Martian plains.
That is about two
and a half times
the height of Mount
Everest above sea level,
and more than twice
the base-to-peak height
of Mauna Kea.
If you placed Olympus
Mons on North America,
its circular base,
roughly six hundred
kilometers wide, would
cover a region about
the size of Arizona.
Its summit caldera alone
spans dozens of kilometers
across.
Like Mauna Kea,
it is a shield
volcano built by layer
after layer of lava,
only on a far larger scale.
Scientists think that low
gravity and a crust
that stays fixed over
a hotspot let it
grow so tall.
What would it feel
like to stand on
the slopes of Olympus Mons?
You might expect a
terrifying, jagged cliff
like the Himalayan peaks,
but the reality is
wonderfully strange.
Because Olympus Mons is
a huge shield volcano,
its average slope is
only a few degrees.
The mountain is so
broad that you would
hardly realize you were
climbing; the ground would
simply tilt gently upward
into a dusty sky.
Even beyond Mars,
our Solar System holds
other bizarre geological
spires.
On the asteroid Vesta,
a giant impact carved
a crater called Rheasilvia.
NASA's Dawn spacecraft
found its central peak
rising roughly twenty to
twenty-five kilometers
above the crater floor.
On a small body
with a tiny fraction
of Earth's gravity,
rock can stand far
taller than on our
dense, heavy planet.
So which mountain is
truly the highest?
From sea level,
Mount Everest wins.
From the ocean floor,
Mauna Kea wins.
From the center of
the Earth, Chimborazo wins.
Change the ruler,
and the champion changes.
And beyond our world,
the champions are far
taller than any of them.

===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Look at these two sea creatures drifting through a prehistoric ocean. At first glance, they look like twins. Both carry a coiled, spiral shell, and both move by squirting water through a tube called a funnel. Yet sixty-six million years ago, a catastrophe from space shattered their world. When the dust settled, one of them had vanished from Earth forever, while the other survived all the way to the present day. Why? Let's find out.

The event is called the Cretaceous-Paleogene mass extinction, and it was triggered by an asteroid that struck at Chicxulub, in what is now Mexico. We all know the fate of Tyrannosaurus rex and Triceratops on land. But the devastation beneath the waves was just as thorough. Food chains unraveled across the oceans, and in the middle of this destruction, the story of two shelled cephalopods became one of paleontology's classic puzzles.

On one side, we have the ammonites, one of the most successful and diverse groups in the history of the ocean. They lived for more than three hundred million years, and they survived several earlier mass extinctions. On the other side sits the nautilus, a quiet relative that looks almost the same. How could a dominant, widespread group disappear completely, while its lookalike survived? Researchers have found several clues, and together they paint a surprisingly clear picture.

Here is the timeline. Ammonoids first appeared around four hundred million years ago. The asteroid struck sixty-six million years ago. That is a reign of more than three hundred million years, a long dominance of the seas.

Ammonites were not fragile. Their group had already survived some of the biggest crises in Earth's history. At the end of the Permian Period, about two hundred fifty-two million years ago, the worst mass extinction of all wiped out most marine species, and ammonoids were reduced to only a few lineages. From those survivors, they rebounded and diversified again. They were hit hard once more at the end of the Triassic, and recovered again. So when ammonites vanished at the end of the Cretaceous, it was not because they were weak. Something about this particular disaster was different.

To solve this, we first have to drop an assumption: similar shells do not mean close relatives. The nautilus belongs to the nautiloids, a branch of cephalopods that split off very early. Ammonites were different. Scientists place them closer to modern squid, cuttlefish, and octopuses than to the nautilus. Their matching spirals are a classic case of convergent evolution, where separate lineages end up with a similar design.

Cut either shell in half, and the differences begin to show. Both are divided into chambers, linked by a thin tube called the siphuncle. By moving liquid and gas in and out of the chambers, the animal can adjust its buoyancy, a bit like the ballast tank of a submarine. But the tube is not in the same place. In the nautilus, it runs through the middle of each chamber wall.

In ammonites, the siphuncle usually ran along the outer edge of the shell, close to its rim. The chamber walls, called septa, also differed. A nautilus builds simple, smooth, dome-shaped walls. Ammonites built walls with elaborate, fractal-like folds along their edges.

Where those folded walls meet the shell, they leave wavy lines called sutures, which look like frozen leaves or snowflakes in stone. Why did ammonites evolve such complicated patterns? Scientists are still debating it. One idea is that the folds helped strengthen thin shells against water pressure. Another is that they helped with the movement of liquid inside the chambers. No one knows for sure, and the answer may be a mix of both.

It is worth pausing here for some honesty. Soft bodies rarely fossilize, so we know much less about the animal inside an ammonite shell than about its shell. Scientists compare the shells with living cephalopods and use rare, exceptionally preserved fossils to fill the gaps. That means some of what we are about to discuss is an informed inference, not a direct observation, and we will say so when it is.

Now, let's look at where they lived. Many ammonites are thought to have lived in the sunlit upper layers of the sea, often shallower than about one hundred meters, although the depth varied by species and over time. There, warm, bright water supported swarms of plankton and small animals, and ammonites moved among them as active swimmers. Their coiled shells came in many shapes, from slim, streamlined discs to bulky, rounded ones, and that variety suggests that different ammonites had different swimming styles.

The nautilus lives very differently. Today, it is found mostly along the steep slopes of coral reefs, in the dim twilight zone, roughly two hundred to seven hundred meters down. Its thick shell helps it withstand the pressure there, and it generally avoids warm, shallow water. That difference in depth turns out to matter a great deal.

Here is the contrast. Many ammonites lived in the upper ocean, at depths of zero to one hundred meters. The nautilus lives deeper, around two hundred to seven hundred meters. One group lived in bright water, the other in the twilight. Two very different worlds.

Why does the nautilus live down there? Its relatives, the nautiloids, were once far more common in shallow seas during the Paleozoic Era. Over time, faster competitors appeared, such as ammonoids, squid-like cephalopods, and fishes, and the nautiloids dwindled. One hypothesis is that the survivors found refuge in deeper, food-poor water where competition was lower. If so, the nautilus was practicing life on scraps long before the asteroid arrived.

Next, food. Using powerful X-ray scans, researchers studied exceptionally well-preserved fossils of an ammonite called Baculites and examined its mouthparts in fine detail. Its radula, a ribbon of tiny teeth, was small and delicate. Inside the fossil's digestive tract, the team found tiny crustaceans and the larvae of snails. That suggests that at least some ammonites fed on small plankton in the upper ocean. This is one of the clearest direct clues we have about what ammonites actually ate.

The nautilus eats in a completely different way. It has strong, beak-like jaws, and its pinhole eyes have no lens, so it sees only a blurry picture. It is not a sight-based chaser. Instead, it uses up to about ninety tentacles to sense chemicals in the water and find food, such as dead animals, shed crustacean shells, and other scraps drifting down from above.

Both animals moved by jet propulsion. They draw water into the body, then squeeze it out through the funnel, which pushes the animal the other way. The nautilus does this gently, drifting more than racing. Its gas-filled chambers keep it almost weightless, so it can hover at a chosen depth with little effort. Ammonites are believed to have used the same basic system, although fossils cannot tell us exactly how fast they swam.

Diet is tied to metabolism, the rate at which an animal burns energy. Ammonites probably lived faster lives in bright, food-rich water, which would have meant a steady need for fresh food. The nautilus is the opposite. It has a very low metabolic rate, and it can go a long time without eating, which suits the cold, dim, food-poor deep.

But the biggest difference may be how they raised their young. In a crisis, the way an animal reproduces can decide whether a whole lineage survives. The tiny first shell of an ammonite, called the ammonitella, is preserved in many fossils. Compared with the nautilus, it reveals two very different strategies: many tiny offspring, or a few large, well-provisioned ones.

Ammonites hatched from small eggs. Their hatchlings were only about one millimeter across, with a tiny shell. Nobody has counted ammonite eggs, but because the babies were so small, scientists think that females laid very many of them. The hatchlings were far too small to swim against ocean currents, and the evidence suggests that they drifted near the surface as plankton. This strategy is common among animals that live in open water: when the young are cheap to make, a few lucky survivors are enough to keep a species going.

Drifting at the surface paid off in normal times. Warm water and plenty of phytoplankton let the larvae grow quickly. But there was a hidden weakness. The next generation depended on a steady supply of tiny food floating in the sunlight. If that supply failed, a whole generation could starve.

The nautilus took the opposite approach. A female lays only a small number of large eggs, each about three centimeters long. In aquariums, she attaches them one at a time to hard surfaces, and wild nautiluses are thought to do something similar on rocky reef slopes. Inside each egg is a rich supply of yolk, so the embryo carries its own food. The nautilus invests heavily in each egg, instead of making many cheap ones.

Here are the sizes. An ammonite hatchling was only about one millimeter across. A nautilus egg is about three centimeters long. And in the cold, it takes almost a year to hatch. It is a choice between quantity and quality.

Cold water slows development, so a nautilus embryo takes close to a year to hatch. When it finally emerges, it is not a tiny larva. It is a miniature version of the adult, about three centimeters across, with a hard shell, gas chambers for buoyancy, tentacles, and the ability to swim and search for food on its own.

Young nautiluses are thought to stay near the sea floor from the start, searching for scraps like their parents. They do not drift for weeks in surface plankton. That means the youngest nautiluses lived far from the sunlit layers where, as we are about to see, the disaster struck first.

Sixty-six million years ago, an asteroid about ten kilometers wide struck the shallow sea off the Yucatán Peninsula. The impact carved a crater roughly one hundred eighty kilometers across and released energy equal to billions of atomic bombs. Models suggest that it triggered giant tsunamis, strong earthquakes, and wildfires. But the deadliest effects came afterward. Within hours, debris thrown into the air was falling back across the planet, and heat from the falling material may have set fire to forests thousands of kilometers away.

The impact blasted dust, soot, and sulfur-rich particles high into the atmosphere. Scientists think this veil blocked much of the sunlight for months, perhaps years, and made the planet colder. For an ocean built on sunlight, that was devastating.

Without sunlight, photosynthesis dropped sharply. Phytoplankton, the tiny drifting algae at the base of the ocean food web, suffered a mass die-off. The fossil record shows that many plankton species with calcium carbonate shells disappeared. Small animals that fed on them lost their food, and then the predators above them did too. The collapse climbed up the food chain.

Some studies suggest that the impact also caused acid rain and ocean acidification, as sulfur and carbon dioxide entered the air. Acidic water can dissolve the calcium carbonate that shells are made of, and newly hatched ammonites, with thin shells, would have been especially vulnerable. How strong this effect was is still debated, but the surface ocean had become a very poor place to live. The surface layer is where many ammonites are thought to have lived and where their larvae drifted.

Adult ammonites living near the surface faced a hard problem. Their food was disappearing, and if their energy needs were as high as their lifestyle suggests, starvation would have come quickly. Their light shells were probably not built for the crushing pressure of deep water, so the deep refuge may have been out of reach.

But the final blow was probably to the young. Even if a few adults lived through the famine, their tiny hatchlings entered an ocean with very little plankton to eat. If most larvae starved, no new generation could replace the old one. Many researchers think this may be why every ammonite lineage vanished.

Now, go deeper, hundreds of meters below the chaos. In the twilight zone, the effects of the disaster were probably slower and weaker. Deep water buffers changes in temperature, and acidic water was diluted in a huge volume of ocean. The nautilus lived there, far from the sunlit layers where the food web had collapsed. That does not mean life there was easy. It only means that the deep sea may have kept something that the surface lost: time.

At first, the disaster may even have brought food. Dead plankton, fish, and other animals sank from the upper ocean as marine snow, and scavengers on the sea floor could eat them. A strong-jawed scavenger like the nautilus was well placed to use that brief feast.

Of course, that rain of food did not last. But the nautilus had a major advantage: its very slow metabolism. When food is scarce, it saves energy, and it can survive long periods with little to eat, far longer than an animal that burns energy quickly. Waiting out hard times was exactly what the nautilus was built to do.

Meanwhile, nautilus eggs had thick protective capsules and plenty of yolk. Their embryos did not need surface plankton, because their food was already inside the egg. We cannot observe those ancient eggs, so this is an inference from living nautiluses. But it fits the picture: a food crash above did not directly starve the next generation below.

When young nautiluses did hatch, they were large and developed enough to look for bits of dead animals and other scraps on the sea floor. Scraps that could not feed a one-millimeter larva could feed a three-centimeter hatchling. The nautilus was built for a world with very little food.

Let's put the evidence side by side. First, metabolism. Ammonites likely burned energy quickly, while the nautilus has a low metabolism. Second, food. Ammonites relied on plankton, but the nautilus eats scraps. Third, the young. Ammonite hatchlings were tiny, while nautilus hatchlings are about three centimeters across. In short, the nautilus was built for lean times.

Geologists can see this in the rocks. Below a thin, iridium-rich layer that marks the asteroid impact, rocks around the world can be packed with ammonite fossils. At or just above that layer, ammonites are gone. A group that had existed for more than three hundred million years was erased, in geological terms, in a blink. No ammonite is known to have survived into the Cenozoic Era.

Ammonites are also a gift to geologists. They evolved quickly, spread widely, and their shells fossilize well, so different species mark different slices of time. Scientists use them as index fossils to date rock layers around the world. In a way, the very group that vanished became one of our best clocks for reading the Mesozoic Era.

The extinction left empty spaces in the ocean. Their soft-bodied relatives, the ancestors of modern squid, cuttlefish, and octopuses, did survive. These animals have lost their heavy outer shells, which probably gave them more agility. Over time, they spread into many of the hunting roles in the sea, and today they are the most familiar cephalopods.

Even the nautilus came through narrowly. Many other shelled cephalopod lineages had already disappeared before the asteroid, and today only a handful of nautilus species remain, all in one small family. Survival through the extinction did not make the nautilus a winner. It simply left the nautilus as one of the few shelled cephalopods still alive.

The nautilus kept living in the Indo-Pacific, along the outer slopes of coral reefs. Its body plan looks much like that of its ancient relatives, so it is often called a living fossil, although that is a loose term, and the species alive today are not identical to those of the past. Still, looking at a nautilus is a glimpse of what a cephalopod with an outer shell was like.

Today, this survivor faces a new danger: us. In recent decades, nautilus numbers have dropped sharply in parts of the Philippines, Indonesia, and the South Pacific. They are caught mostly for their shells, which are sold as decorations, jewelry, and collector items in the international trade.

The traits that helped the nautilus survive now make it vulnerable. It grows slowly, and it takes many years to reach maturity, more than a decade by some estimates. A female lays only a small number of eggs, so populations recover slowly. When traps empty out a local reef, recovery may take decades, and some isolated groups could disappear.

Climate change adds another worry. As carbon dioxide dissolves in seawater, the ocean becomes more acidic, and that reduces the carbonate that shell-builders need. Researchers estimate that today's rate of acidification is faster than anything seen in at least the past three hundred million years. How much it will affect nautilus shells in the wild is still being studied.

There is some good news. In 2016, countries agreed to list all nautiluses under Appendix II of CITES, the international wildlife trade convention, and the rule took effect in 2017. Now, exporting their shells needs permits. Scientists are also surveying wild populations with baited camera systems, to learn how many are left.

Here is the timeline. In 2016, countries voted at a CITES meeting to list nautiluses. The rule took effect in 2017. They are now under Appendix II, which means exports need permits. Trade is now regulated.

The ammonite and the nautilus teach an old lesson about evolution. A trait that makes an animal excel in one world can become a trap in another. Ammonites were agile, diverse, and well suited to bright, productive seas. When those seas were suddenly wrecked, their strengths became weaknesses. The nautilus, shaped by hard times, was better prepared for the lean world that followed.

The nautilus was never the star of the prehistoric ocean. It lived on the margins, ate scraps, and grew slowly. It probably survived not because it was the strongest or fastest, but because it was good at getting by with little. When the sunlight failed, animals that depended on it were lost, while an animal built for the dark carried on.

Imagine holding an ammonite fossil in one hand and a nautilus shell in the other. One is a gravestone for a group that ruled the seas for more than three hundred million years. The other is a living link to that ancient world. Their story shows that Earth does not care who is winning at the moment, and that survival often depends on traits that once seemed unimportant.

Studying deep time is also a way to understand how fragile life's support systems can be. The end-Cretaceous crisis shows that a collapse at the base of the food web can spread upward, harming even animals that seemed secure. Earth has gone through five major mass extinctions, and each one teaches us how ecosystems fail.

That a nautilus is still here, moving through the dark water of a reef slope, is remarkable. Its lineage lived through an asteroid impact and countless changes in the oceans. Now its future depends on one mammal species that appeared only a geological moment ago: us. Protecting it means protecting a very old branch of the tree of life. It is a reminder that survival through one catastrophe does not guarantee survival through the next.

Next time you see an ammonite fossil with its delicate, fractal sutures, remember that it was not a failed design. It was a successful animal that ran out of luck when the rules changed overnight. And when you think of the nautilus, remember how well life can do on the margins. Both shells are spirals, and together they tell a story of innovation, catastrophe, extinction, and survival.

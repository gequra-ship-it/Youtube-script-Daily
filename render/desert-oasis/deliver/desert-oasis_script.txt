===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Imagine standing in the middle of an ocean of sand. The sun is overhead, the ground shimmers with heat, and the horizon is empty in every direction. Rain may not fall here for years. Everything about this place says that water is impossible. And yet, somewhere out in this emptiness, there are pools of clear water ringed by green palm trees. How can that be?

For desert travelers, a glimpse of green on the horizon could be a cruel trick. A mirage happens when light bends as it passes through layers of hot air near the ground, so the sky itself seems to shimmer like a lake. But sometimes the water is real. It rises out of the dry ground, day after day, year after year. We call these places oases.

An oasis is an island of plants and water in a sea of dry land. Birds, insects and mammals gather here. Date palms shade the springs, and people have farmed around some oases for thousands of years. But this raises a simple question. If it almost never rains in the desert, where does all this water come from? Let's follow it, step by step.

First, why are deserts so dry? Many of the world's great deserts, including the Sahara and the Arabian Desert, sit near thirty degrees north or south of the equator. There, air that rose over the tropics sinks back toward the ground. Sinking air warms and dries out, so clouds struggle to form. Rain can fail for years, the ground can bake far hotter than the air above it, and any water left on the surface quickly evaporates.

If the sky is dry, the answer is not in the sky. The secret of most oases is underground. A desert is not just a pile of sand. Beneath the dunes lie layers of rock laid down over millions of years: sandstone, limestone, clay and shale. Some of those layers can hold enormous amounts of water, and move it slowly over great distances.

Rocks like sandstone are full of tiny spaces between their grains, and limestone is often cracked and hollowed out. Water can soak into these spaces and slowly move through them. A layer of rock that holds and passes water like this is called an aquifer. Think of it as a sponge made of stone. Some aquifers are vast. The Nubian Sandstone Aquifer System lies under parts of Egypt, Libya, Sudan and Chad.

How does water get into an aquifer in the first place? Often, it starts far away. Rain and melting snow fall on mountains or higher ground at the edge of a desert. Some of that water seeps into cracks and into rock layers that come up to the surface there. Then gravity takes over, and the water begins a very slow journey underground, sometimes for hundreds of kilometers. Along the way, it can take thousands of years to reach the place where it finally comes back to the surface.

Not every rock lets water through. Clay and shale are so tight that water barely moves through them. Geologists call layers like these aquitards. When a water-bearing layer is sandwiched between two tight layers, the water is trapped. If the place where it entered is higher than the land further along, the trapped water is under pressure. This is called a confined, or artesian, aquifer.

Think of a garden hose with one end held high. Water pushes down from above, and the lower end is under pressure. In an artesian aquifer, the weight of the water upslope does the pushing. If a crack or a fault breaks through the rock above it, water can rise to the surface on its own, with no pump at all. Many springs in desert oases are fed this way.

One famous example is Siwa, in Egypt's Western Desert, near the Libyan border. Siwa lies in a depression below sea level, and by some counts it has more than a thousand springs. Its water comes from the Nubian Sandstone Aquifer System. Studies suggest the water feeding Siwa's springs has spent tens of thousands of years underground. Around it grow date palms and olive trees, and beside them lie salty lakes.

But where did so much underground water come from, in a place this dry? Part of the answer lies in the past. Roughly eleven thousand to six thousand years ago, the Sahara was not a desert. It was a green savanna with lakes and rivers. Rock art in the Sahara shows elephants, giraffes and hippos. A stronger African monsoon reached far north, bringing regular summer rain.

Earth's orbit slowly wobbles, and that wobble changes how much summer sunshine falls on North Africa. As the sunshine weakened, the monsoon retreated south, and the Sahara dried out. But water that had soaked into the ground during this and earlier wet periods stayed there. Deep aquifers that receive almost no new rain today still hold it. This ancient water is called fossil water.

How old is this water? Scientists can tell by measuring rare radioactive atoms dissolved in it. Water feeding the springs of Siwa is thought to be around thirty thousand to fifty thousand years old. In 2004, a team used krypton-81 to date water from the Nubian aquifer elsewhere in Egypt's Western Desert, and found ages of two hundred thousand to one million years. It moves north only about one to two meters a year.

Pressure is not the only way to make an oasis. Wind can do it too. In open desert, strong winds blow loose sand and dust away, again and again, for thousands of years. Where the rock is weak, especially where salt crystals break it apart, the ground can be lowered bit by bit. Geologists call this removal of loose material by wind deflation.

Below every desert there is a level called the water table. Beneath it, the spaces in the rock are full of water. If wind lowers the ground far enough, the bottom of the hollow can reach that level. Then water seeps out at the surface, and marshes, salt flats or small lakes appear. The wind has dug down to the water.

The most dramatic example is the Qattara Depression in northwestern Egypt. Its lowest point lies about one hundred thirty-three meters below sea level, one of the lowest places in Africa. Scientists think wind, together with salt weathering, removed the rock that once filled it. Its floor is covered by salt marshes and salt flats, kept damp where groundwater comes close to the surface.

Faults can also create oases. A fault is a break in the Earth's crust where rock has moved. Along some faults, ground-up rock and clay form a barrier, like an underground dam. Groundwater flowing toward the fault is blocked and pushed upward. Where it reaches the surface, a line of springs can appear, so oases sometimes sit in a neat row along the fault.

You can see this in California's Coachella Valley. There, desert fan palms, the only palm native to the western United States, grow in groves right along the San Andreas Fault. Crushed rock in the fault zone blocks groundwater and brings it near the surface. Thousand Palms Oasis is one of these groves. One of the most famous earthquake faults on Earth is also what keeps these palms alive.

Even the driest deserts get rain now and then, often as short, violent storms. Hard, dry ground cannot soak it up fast enough, so the water rushes downhill as a flash flood. These floods pour into dry valleys called wadis. They can be deadly. But they also feed the ground beneath the valley floor.

As a flood races down a wadi, part of the water sinks into the sand and gravel of the valley floor. Long after the surface dries, water can remain below, shaded from the sun, slowly moving downstream. Where this hidden flow comes close to the surface, wells can reach it, and palms and gardens can grow. Many oases sit in wadis for exactly this reason.

Some deserts are crossed by rivers that start somewhere much wetter. The Nile is the great example. Most of the water that reaches Egypt comes from rain in the Ethiopian Highlands, carried by the Blue Nile. As the river runs north through the desert, it creates a narrow green strip of farmland. In a way, the Nile valley is one very long oasis, watered by rain that fell far away.

Once water appears, how do plants get there? Some seeds are carried by wind. Others arrive with animals, especially birds, which can spread seeds in their droppings. Birds crossing the desert are drawn to water and green plants, and they stop to drink and rest. Over time, a bare spring can become a green island.

The most important plant of many oases is the date palm, Phoenix dactylifera. It has a deep, spreading root system that can reach groundwater, and it tolerates salty soil better than most crops. It also gives fruit that stores well, along with leaves and wood for building. For thousands of years, date palms have been the backbone of oasis farming across North Africa and the Middle East.

Date palms also change the climate beneath them. Their crowns shade the ground, which keeps it cooler and slows evaporation. Oasis farmers used this to grow food in layers. Tall palms stand on top. Beneath them grow fruit trees such as figs, pomegranates and citrus. And on the ground grow vegetables, grains and fodder. A single oasis garden can feed far more people this way.

How big can an oasis get? In eastern Saudi Arabia, the Al-Ahsa Oasis holds about two and a half million date palms. UNESCO, which listed it as a World Heritage Site in 2018, describes it as the largest oasis in the world. For centuries it was watered by natural springs and shallow wells, and its canals, gardens and old towns show how people organized life around that water.

People also learned to make oases themselves. In ancient Persia, engineers developed the qanat, and settlements depending on them existed by about three thousand years ago. Builders dug a well at the foot of a hill to reach groundwater. Then they dug a tunnel with a gentle downhill slope from that well to the fields below, using a line of vertical shafts for air and for hauling out soil. Gravity did the rest.

Why underground? Because in the desert, open water evaporates quickly. A canal on the surface would lose much of its water to the sun and wind. In a tunnel, the water stays cool and much more of it arrives. Qanats could carry water for many kilometers, and some are still in use today. Iran's qanats are listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The idea spread widely. In the Sahara of Algeria, these tunnels are called foggaras. In Oman and the United Arab Emirates, similar channels are called falaj. In the Turpan Basin of western China, they are known as karez. And in Peru, the Nazca region has its own underground channels, called puquios. Across very different cultures, people found the same solution to the same problem.

Oases also shaped trade. The Silk Road was not a single road but a network of routes linked by oasis towns. Caravans carrying silk, spices and other goods moved from one water source to the next. Cities such as Dunhuang in China, Samarkand in Central Asia and Palmyra in Syria grew rich as stops along these routes. Without their water, the long-distance trade of the ancient world would have looked very different.

Life in an oasis is a constant struggle with the desert around it. Crescent-shaped dunes called barchans can creep forward with the wind, burying fields, houses and wells. For centuries, oasis people have fought back with fences of palm fronds and rows of plants that slow the sand. Keeping an oasis alive takes work every single year. When the work stops, sand can bury fields and choke wells surprisingly fast.

There is a quieter danger, too. All natural water carries some dissolved salt. When irrigation water evaporates under the desert sun, the water leaves but the salt stays behind. Year after year, salt builds up in the soil until even hardy crops struggle. This is called salinization. That is why good drainage, to carry salty water away, matters as much in an oasis as bringing water in.

In the twentieth century, powerful pumps changed everything. Deep wells could now lift fossil water far faster than any natural spring. In Saudi Arabia, from the 1980s, center-pivot sprinklers turned patches of desert into green circles of wheat, some about a kilometer across, watered by ancient groundwater. Seen from space, the desert filled with green dots.

But fossil water is like money in a bank account that no longer receives deposits. Every liter pumped from a non-renewable aquifer is a liter that will not come back in our lifetimes. In parts of North Africa and the Arabian Peninsula, water levels have dropped as pumping increased, and some traditional springs and qanats have weakened or dried up.

The most ambitious project of all is Libya's Great Man-Made River, begun in 1983. It pumps fossil water from wells deep in the Sahara and carries it north to coastal cities through buried concrete pipes four meters wide. The planned network is nearly four thousand kilometers long, designed to deliver close to six million cubic meters of water a day. But that water comes from aquifers that are barely being refilled.

Some desert countries now turn to the sea. In Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates and elsewhere around the Gulf, large plants remove salt from seawater, increasingly using a method called reverse osmosis, which pushes water through very fine membranes. Desalination takes a lot of energy, and it leaves behind very salty brine. But for many coastal desert cities, it has become a main source of drinking water.

In Chile's Atacama Desert, one of the driest places on Earth, rain almost never falls. But along the coast, thick fog drifts in from the Pacific. Some communities hang large mesh nets on hillsides. Tiny fog droplets collect on the mesh, merge into drops, and trickle into gutters and pipes. It is a small source of water, but it comes straight out of the air.

Other projects work with the land itself. Farmers and designers dig shallow ditches along the contour of a slope, called swales, to catch the rare rain before it runs off. They cover the soil with mulch to keep it cool and moist, and plant hardy trees. In places like Jordan, small demonstration sites have shown that patches of dry land can be made greener this way, though it takes years of care.

Oases are also laboratories of evolution. Just as remote islands produce species found nowhere else, so do isolated springs. In Mexico's Chihuahuan Desert, the Cuatro Ciénegas basin holds hundreds of spring-fed pools. More than one hundred fifty species of plants and animals there are found nowhere else, including fish, snails and the only box turtle that lives in water. Some pools even contain stromatolites, layered mats built by microbes.

For wildlife, an oasis can mean the difference between life and death. Gazelles, foxes and many other animals depend on scattered sources of water in the desert. Huge numbers of migrating birds cross the Sahara twice a year, and oases offer them food, shade and water along the way. Lose those green dots on the map, and the journey becomes far harder.

Some water is still being discovered. In November 1981, the Space Shuttle Columbia carried an imaging radar over the eastern Sahara. In very dry sand, the radar could see down several meters, and it revealed ancient river valleys buried beneath the sand, some ten to fifteen kilometers wide. Scientists nicknamed them radar rivers. Such buried valleys help show where water once flowed, and where groundwater may still be found.

Pumping too much water has another cost. In some aquifers, especially those with layers of clay, the water helps hold the ground up. When it is pumped out, those layers can compact, and the land surface slowly sinks. This is called land subsidence. Part of that compaction is permanent: even if water returns, the squeezed clay may never store as much again.

Traditional oasis communities understood scarcity well. Water was usually shared by strict rules, often by time. In some North African oases, each family's share was measured by simple water clocks. In thirteenth-century Tozeur, in what is now Tunisia, the scholar Ibn Chabbat is credited with writing detailed rules for sharing the oasis water. In the desert, fairness was a matter of survival.

Today, oases face new pressure. Hotter summers mean more evaporation from pools, soils and leaves. Growing towns and farms pump more groundwater. In some regions, the surrounding land is becoming drier and more degraded. Scientists watch oases closely, because they show early how fragile life in dry places can be when water runs short.

So how is an oasis made? Not by a single lucky accident. It can be rain that fell on distant mountains, or in a green Sahara thousands of years ago. It can be stored in sandstone and held under pressure by clay. It can rise through a fault, appear where wind has dug down to the water table, or hide under the gravel of a wadi. Many slow processes, working together, bring water to the surface.

The next time you see a picture of palm trees around a blue pool in the desert, remember what lies beneath it. Water that may have traveled for centuries underground.

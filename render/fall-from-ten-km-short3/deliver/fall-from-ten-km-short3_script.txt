===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Can the way you position your arms and legs actually change how you fall from the sky? Skydivers say yes. And the worst thing you can do is curl into a tight ball.

When you fall, gravity pulls you down and the air pushes back. That push is aerodynamic drag, and it grows as you speed up. Soon the two forces balance, your acceleration stops, and you reach terminal velocity.

Curling up shrinks your surface area. With less body in the way of the rushing air, you slice through it, and your falling speed rises. If your goal is to slow down, the ball is a poor choice.

Skydivers use the belly-to-earth spread-eagle. Arms and legs go wide, your back arches slightly, and your chest faces the ground. Now your body catches as many air molecules as possible, and your terminal velocity falls toward its lowest value, around two hundred kilometers per hour.

Spreading out also keeps you stable. An uneven object can fall into a violent flat spin, and a fast spin can cause dizziness and even blackouts. Outstretched limbs act like the four fins of a rocket and damp the rotation. You can even steer a little by tilting your hands and shoulders.

Then, in the final moments, the usual advice is a feet-first landing with slightly bent knees. The long bones of the legs, the ankles, and the pelvis are among the sturdiest in the body. They take the first hit and may crumple, absorbing energy before the shock reaches the spine and skull.

Military parachutists add one more trick, the parachute landing fall. Boots touch down, then the body collapses to the side and rolls across the calf, thigh, hip, and back. A hard vertical stop becomes rotation along the ground.

None of this makes a fall from ten kilometers safe. A roll cannot cancel that much speed, and luck plays the dominant role. But the ideas are the same ones again and again: spread the impact over more body and more time.

===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Look up at the night sky, and you are immediately consumed by an overwhelming sense of infinite wonder. For thousands of years, humanity looked at the stars and believed that our destiny was to explore every single corner of this vast expanse. We built telescopes, launched rockets into the void, and dreamed of venturing toward the ultimate edge of existence. But modern astrophysics has uncovered a terrifying truth that shatters that dream. There is a cosmic barrier, an invisible wall written into the laws of reality, that guarantees we will never escape our cosmic backyard.

To understand our imprisonment, we must first look at the greatest physical achievement of human exploration: Voyager 1. Launched in 1977, this small metal probe has been hurtling through the icy vacuum at over 38,000 miles per hour for nearly half a century. It has traveled farther than any object ever built by human hands. Yet, on the cosmic scale, it has barely stepped off our front porch. It has not even left the outer gravitational grip of our Sun, wandering through an endless desert of nothingness.

At its current speed, Voyager 1 would need more than 73,000 years just to cover the distance to our nearest neighboring star, Proxima Centauri. Think about that timeframe for a moment. Entire human civilizations have risen, built colossal empires, and fallen to dust in a small fraction of that time. And Proxima Centauri is merely our next-door neighbor, sitting about 4.2 light-years away, in a galaxy that spans more than 100,000 light-years. If getting to our closest neighbor takes longer than all of recorded history, how could we ever hope to reach the edge of the universe itself?

You might argue that our current propulsion technology is simply too primitive. Perhaps in the distant future, we will build engines that approach the speed of light. But here lies the first brutal law of physics, described by Albert Einstein: the speed of light is not just a speed. It is the universal speed limit of cause and effect. Traveling at about 186,000 miles per second, light is the fastest that any information or matter can move through space. Nothing with mass can ever exceed it, or even reach it.

As an object with mass accelerates closer to the speed of light, an inescapable nightmare appears. Every extra bit of speed costs more energy than the last, and the total climbs toward infinity. To reach the speed of light itself, you would need an infinite amount of energy. There is not enough energy in all the stars, galaxies, and black holes of the known universe combined to push a single starship to the exact speed of light. Physics itself slams the door shut, locking us behind the iron bars of sub-light speed forever.

Even if we could somehow bend reality and travel at 99.9 percent of the speed of light, the sheer scale of the cosmos mocks our ambitions.

Our home galaxy, the Milky Way, is an archipelago of more than 100 billion stars. Beyond our galaxy lies the Local Group, roughly 10 million light-years across. Beyond that lies the Laniakea Supercluster, containing about 100,000 galaxies stretched across some 520 million light-years.

When you zoom out to this level, our entire existence is less than a speck of dust floating in an ocean of silence.

For a long time, humanity took comfort in the idea that even if the universe was immense, it was at least static. In a stationary universe, given infinite time and immortal patience, you could eventually cross any distance and reach the edge. That comforting picture began to fall apart in 1929, thanks to an astronomer named Edwin Hubble. Using the giant 100-inch Hooker telescope on Mount Wilson, Hubble found a pattern that shook the foundations of science: the universe is not standing still. It is expanding.

Hubble studied the light coming from distant galaxies and saw an undeniable pattern: their light was shifted toward the red end of the spectrum. At first, this redshift looked like the Doppler effect, the same stretch you hear when a siren races away from you. And the farther away a galaxy was, the faster it seemed to be racing off. This was not random movement of objects drifting through empty space. Distant galaxies were receding from each other everywhere, like dots painted on the surface of an inflating balloon.

Here is where human intuition fails and the true horror begins. The galaxies are not really flying through space like bullets through the air. Instead, the fabric of space itself is stretching between them. Think of space not as an empty stage where actors dance, but as a living fabric. Between you and a distant galaxy, new cubic light-years of empty space keep appearing, steadily pushing everything farther apart, every single moment.

This is described by Hubble's Law, which says that the speed at which a galaxy recedes is proportional to its distance.

For every megaparsec, about 3.26 million light-years, space adds roughly 70 kilometers per second to the speed of recession. A galaxy two megaparsecs away recedes at about 140 kilometers per second. A galaxy ten megaparsecs away recedes at about 700 kilometers per second.

The farther away you look, the faster the gap between us grows.

Now, connect the dots. If the speed of recession keeps rising with distance, there must come a point where it exceeds the speed of light itself. And that point is real. At a distance of about 14 billion light-years from Earth, the space between us and those distant regions is growing faster than 186,000 miles per second. Space itself is carrying them away from us faster than light can travel.

You might immediately ask: does this break Einstein's rule that nothing can move faster than light? The mind-bending answer is no. Special relativity says that no matter or signal can move through space faster than light. But it says nothing about how fast space itself can stretch. The most distant galaxies we can see are being carried away from us at several times the speed of light today, and no law of physics is broken.

Imagine you are running on a giant treadmill. Your top running speed is 10 miles per hour. But the belt beneath your feet is moving backward at 20 miles per hour, then 50, then 100 miles per hour. No matter how fiercely you pump your legs, no matter how much fuel you burn, you will not only fail to move forward, you will be dragged backward into the darkness. For a beam of light or a starship trying to reach the most distant galaxies, the cosmic treadmill moves faster than its top speed.

Strangely, light that sets out from just beyond that 14-billion-light-year line can still reach us one day, because the line itself slowly moves outward. The true point of no return lies a little farther away, at roughly 16 billion light-years. It is called the Cosmic Event Horizon. Much like the event horizon of a black hole, it is a boundary of cause and effect. Any galaxy beyond it is cut off from us for good. Even if you fired a beam of light toward those regions right now, it would travel forever and never arrive, because the space in between grows too fast.

If you think this situation is already grim, prepare yourself, because in 1998, astronomers uncovered an even stranger truth. Until then, scientists believed that the gravity of all the matter in the universe was acting like a brake, slowing the expansion over time. Some thought the universe might one day stop expanding, or even collapse. But when two independent teams measured distant Type Ia supernovae to check how much the expansion was slowing, they found the exact opposite. The expansion is not slowing down at all. It is accelerating.

Something invisible, mysterious, and dominant is pushing the fabric of the cosmos apart at an ever-increasing pace. Scientists named it dark energy. In the simplest model, it does not thin out as space expands. Its density stays the same, so as more space is created, there is more dark energy to push it apart. Today, dark energy makes up roughly 68 percent of the total energy content of the universe, and it is winning the cosmic tug-of-war against gravity.

Because of dark energy, distant galaxies keep slipping across the cosmic event horizon. Right now, as you listen to this sentence, it is happening. By one estimate, about 20,000 stars every second pass from reachable to forever unreachable. They are not dying or exploding. They are simply being carried away faster than we could ever follow, cut off from our corner of reality forever.

We must make an essential distinction between the universe and the observable universe. The observable universe is a sphere centered on us, about 93 billion light-years in diameter. It holds everything whose light has had time to reach us since the Big Bang, 13.8 billion years ago. Beyond this bubble lies the unobservable universe, a realm that may even be infinite in size.

Many people believe that we could eventually travel anywhere inside the observable universe if we just moved at nearly the speed of light. This is a tragic misunderstanding. We can see light that left distant galaxies billions of years ago, but we can never visit most of them. The light we see today is ancient history, a ghost from the past. By one widely cited estimate, if we launched a ship at the speed of light today, it could never reach about 94 percent of the galaxies in the observable universe. They have already slipped past the cosmic event horizon.

Let that sink in for a second. Even if humanity survives for a billion years, builds megastructures, and masters near-light-speed travel, nearly the entire universe is off-limits. We are looking at a celestial gallery of unreachable destinations. We can look, we can photograph, we can study their past, but we can never touch them. They are sealed off from us by the laws of spacetime.

So, what is left for us? What part of this immense reality can we actually reach? The answer is our gravitationally bound neighborhood: the Local Group. This cosmic island holds more than 80 known galaxies. It is dominated by the huge Andromeda Galaxy, our own Milky Way, and the smaller Triangulum Galaxy, along with dozens of dwarf galaxies. Within this bubble, gravity is strong enough to hold the galaxies together against the push of dark energy.

In fact, rather than moving away from us, the Andromeda Galaxy is heading toward the Milky Way at about 250,000 miles per hour. For years, astronomers predicted a collision in about 4.5 billion years, followed by a merger into a single giant galaxy nicknamed Milkdromeda. A 2025 study found the odds are less certain, closer to a coin flip within the next 10 billion years. But either way, the galaxies of our Local Group are expected to end up together, and that will be the permanent home for everything we can ever reach.

This brings us to what the edge of the universe truly means. The edge is not a brick wall floating in space, where you can reach out your hand and touch the void. The edge is a horizon of cause and effect, closing in around our group of galaxies. Everything outside the Local Group will eventually be swept beyond that horizon, carried off by the unrelenting expansion of space. The true edge of the universe is a horizon made of ever-growing distance.

Let us look into the far future, more than a trillion years from now. As dark energy continues its work, every galaxy beyond our own merged galaxy will be carried past the horizon. Their light will be stretched until no instrument could ever detect it. The night sky of Milkdromeda will become intensely, terrifyingly dark. Anyone looking through a telescope in that distant era will see nothing beyond the borders of their single home galaxy.

Physicists Lawrence Krauss and Robert Scherrer described the tragic fate of astronomers born in that era. They would find no sign of other galaxies racing away. The cosmic microwave background, the afterglow of the Big Bang, would be stretched beyond detection. Looking out at one lonely galaxy surrounded by darkness, they might conclude that the universe is static and eternal. The true history of the cosmos would be erased.

But wait, what about loopholes? What about wormholes, the Einstein-Rosen bridges that, in theory, could connect distant regions of spacetime? Physicists have explored whether a traveler could cheat cosmic distance by taking such a shortcut. Yet the physics of wormholes presents enormous barriers. To hold a wormhole open long enough for anything to pass, you would need exotic matter with negative energy, in amounts no one knows how to make, and that may not exist at all.

And even if such exotic matter could be found, quantum physics may add another hurdle. Stephen Hawking proposed that quantum fluctuations could build up inside such a tunnel and destroy it before it could ever be used for time travel. He called this the chronology protection conjecture. No one knows for sure, because we still lack a complete theory of quantum gravity. But nature seems to guard its speed limit fiercely.

What about warp drives? In 1994, physicist Miguel Alcubierre showed mathematically that a spacecraft could, in theory, travel faster than light by contracting space in front of it and expanding space behind it. Inside the warp bubble, the ship itself is at rest, so it never breaks the local speed limit. It sounds like the perfect escape hatch, but the physical reality of the Alcubierre drive is just as daunting as the wormhole.

Early calculations said a warp bubble would need at least the mass-energy of the planet Jupiter, and it would have to be negative energy. Later redesigns lowered that number, but no one knows how to make negative energy in useful amounts. Worse still, a 2012 study found that a moving warp bubble could sweep up particles along the way, like a cosmic snowplow. When the ship stopped, that trapped energy could burst out ahead of it, blasting anything waiting at the destination.

Every avenue of theoretical physics that promises an escape toward the edge of the universe runs into a stone wall of physical impossibility. Whether it is the infinite energy requirement of Einstein's relativity, the accelerating expansion driven by dark energy, or the fatal problems of exotic shortcuts, the universe speaks with one voice. The laws of nature are not designed for interstellar nomads. We are trapped inside our local cosmic cell.

But let us push the question even further. What if you could magically teleport to the edge of our observable universe right now? What would you actually see? A wall of darkness? A boundary where space ends and nothingness begins? Modern cosmology gives a mind-bending answer. If you teleported 46 billion light-years away, you would simply find yourself at the center of your own observable bubble, looking much like ours. As far as we can tell, space would continue in every direction.

Think of an ant walking on the surface of a giant beach ball. The ant can walk forward forever in any direction and never meet an edge. It will never fall off its world, because the surface is finite, yet has no boundary. Some cosmologists have considered that our universe might be curved in a similar way. If it were, traveling in a straight line for an unimaginable distance would eventually bring you back to where you started, like sailing around a planet.

On the other hand, measurements from the Planck space telescope show that the geometry of our universe is remarkably flat. If space is flat and has no unusual shape on the largest scales, the simplest conclusion is that it goes on forever, infinite in volume, with an endless number of galaxies. In an infinite universe, the idea of a physical edge does not even exist.

Consider the irony. On one hand, if the universe is infinite, there is no edge to reach. On the other hand, even if there were an edge, the accelerating expansion of space ensures we could never travel fast enough to catch up to it. We are chasing a phantom edge that retreats from us faster than light itself. Every step we take forward is dwarfed by the new space opening up between us and our destination.

Look at the vast voids that already exist within our universe, such as the colossal Boötes Void. Spanning roughly 250 to 330 million light-years, this immense pocket of space contains only about 60 known galaxies. It is a desert of emptiness. Astronomer Greg Aldering noted that if the Milky Way sat at the center of the Boötes Void, we would not have known that other galaxies existed until the 1960s. As dark energy keeps working, the sky beyond our home will become emptier than any void we know today.

Picture it as a cosmic shoreline. We live on the shore of an endless ocean, watching distant islands drift over the horizon one by one. The tide of expansion is rising, washing away the bridges of light that connect us to the rest of the universe. The shoreline is receding, cutting our island off from every other outpost of reality, and no technology can stop it.

Even if space were not expanding, a second executioner waits for any traveler trying to cross the cosmos: entropy. The second law of thermodynamics says that the usable energy in the universe keeps running down over time. Stars have finite lifespans. The gas needed to form new stars will run out, and star formation is expected to end in about 100 trillion years. Crossing cosmic distances takes immense energy, and the clock is always ticking against the energy reserves of reality itself.

In the deep future, long before a ship could reach anywhere significant, the brilliant era of starlight will come to a close. Stars like our Sun will burn out, leaving cold red dwarfs that slowly fade over trillions of years. Eventually, the cosmos enters what physicists call the Degenerate Era, filled only with white dwarfs, neutron stars, and black holes. Traveling through space will mean voyaging through an endless graveyard of frozen stellar remains in total darkness.

After ten to the power of forty years, even protons, the building blocks of atomic nuclei, may decay into radiation, if protons decay at all. No experiment has ever seen it happen. If they do, solid matter ceases to exist. Spaceships, planets, bones, and steel will dissolve away. The only large structures left will be black holes. But even these titans are doomed. Through the incredibly slow leak of Hawking radiation, over something like ten to the power of a hundred years, even the largest black holes will evaporate into faint photons, leaving behind almost nothing.

This final state is known as the heat death, or the Big Freeze, one leading scenario for the far future. The universe becomes a thin, even soup of extremely low-energy particles spread across unimaginable space. Temperatures drop to a tiny fraction of a degree above absolute zero. Time itself loses practical meaning, because nothing changes, nothing moves, and no useful work can ever be done. In this scenario, the cosmic story ends not with a violent crash, but with a frozen, silent whisper that lasts forever.

When you confront the combined weight of these cosmic truths, it delivers a profound shock. We are not the masters of an infinite cosmic playground. We are prisoners of physics, living on a tiny grain of sand within a single, isolated island of galaxies. The universe does not care about our curiosity, our dreams of conquest, or our longing to see what lies beyond the veil. It is governed by laws that pull the parts of reality apart faster than light can ever bridge them.

Yet, paradoxical as it may sound, this crushing reality reveals an incredible privilege that belongs to our era. We happen to exist during a golden age of cosmology. We live in a narrow window when the universe is old enough to have formed complex elements, rocky planets, and conscious minds, yet young enough that distant galaxies and the cosmic microwave background are still visible to our telescopes.

If humanity had appeared a hundred billion years later, the night sky would have been almost empty of other galaxies. Our scientists might have lived and died never knowing about the expanding universe or the Big Bang, trapped in an illusion of eternal, lonely darkness. But we live now. We have the rare privilege of looking through the cosmic window before dark energy closes the shutters. We are the witnesses of a disappearing masterpiece.

This places a great responsibility on human minds. We are among the rare observers in cosmic history who can record the true architecture of reality before it fades from view. Every galaxy we map, every spectrum we measure, and every light curve we calculate is an act of defiance against cosmic forgetting. We are writing down the story of an expanding universe that future civilizations may never have the chance to witness.

We can never reach the edge of the universe, and we must make peace with that truth. The edge is not a place awaiting our flags and footprints. It is an unreachable horizon, enforced by the speed of light and dark energy. But accepting our cosmic confinement does not diminish the human spirit. It elevates it. It reminds us that our local realm, this small gravitational oasis containing hundreds of billions of suns, is unimaginably precious.

Within the Local Group alone, there are hundreds of billions of star systems, countless planets, alien moons, hidden oceans, and uncharted worlds awaiting exploration. Even without leaving our local cell, humanity has enough cosmic territory to explore for millions of generations. We do not need to reach the edge of reality to find purpose, beauty, and discovery. The garden in which we are planted is vast beyond our wildest imaginations.

The real mystery of the cosmos is not that there are regions forever locked away from us. The real mystery is that we, tiny, fragile collections of stardust, walking on the surface of a pale blue dot, can sit beneath the night sky, look out into the void, and actually understand the very laws that keep us imprisoned. Through mathematics, physics, and reason, the universe has developed a mind that can contemplate its own boundaries.

The calcium in your bones, the iron in your blood, and the oxygen you breathe were forged inside stars that lived and died billions of years ago. You are not separate from the cosmos, looking in from the outside. You are the cosmos experiencing itself from within. When you realize that you are part of the universe waking up to witness its own magnificence, the walls of our cosmic prison suddenly lose their sting.

So the next time you step outside on a clear night and gaze up into the cold, silent depths of space, look at the stars with a new sense of humility and awe. Do not mourn the fact that we can never touch the edge of the universe. Instead, marvel at the breathtaking reality that in this brief, precious cosmic second, we are here, we are alive, and we can see the light of a vanishing world before it fades into the dark forever.

The edge of the universe will forever remain an untouchable phantom, sealed behind the barrier of expanding space. But the true frontier was never about how far we could fly into the void. The true frontier is our unquenchable thirst to understand the reality we live in. If this journey sparked your curiosity, subscribing to Miya's Lab keeps one more window to the stars open. As long as we look up with curiosity and courage, our journey across the cosmos has only just begun. Thank you for watching, and keep looking up.

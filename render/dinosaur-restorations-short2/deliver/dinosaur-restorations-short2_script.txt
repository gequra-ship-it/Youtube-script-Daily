===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

What do you do when the only fossils of a giant predator are destroyed by bombs? Spinosaurus was found in the Egyptian desert, then lost in a German museum in 1944. How do you draw an animal nobody can study anymore?

No animal shows how pictures can change better than Spinosaurus. In 1912, fossils of a huge predator were found in the Egyptian desert, and the German paleontologist Ernst Stromer described them in 1915. The animal had long jaws and spines on its back at least one point six five meters long, which seemed to hold up a large sail.

Stromer's fossils were kept in a museum in Munich. Stromer was a critic of the Nazi regime, and tension with the museum's Nazi director meant the fossils were not moved to safety. On the night of the twenty-fourth to the twenty-fifth of April, 1944, Allied bombs hit the museum, and the Spinosaurus fossils were destroyed. Only Stromer's drawings and descriptions remained.

For decades, Spinosaurus was known mostly from those drawings and a few scraps. Artists often gave it the body of a typical large two-legged predator, added a sail on its back and a long, crocodile-like snout.

Then new fossils from Morocco changed the picture. In 2014, a team led by Nizar Ibrahim described a partial skeleton with surprisingly short hind legs and dense bones. They proposed that Spinosaurus spent much of its life in rivers, catching fish. Some other scientists disputed parts of this reconstruction, and the argument was only beginning.

In 2020, the same team described its tail. It was tall and flat, with long spines forming something like a fin, a bit like the tail of a newt. They argued it could push the animal through water.

In just over a century, Spinosaurus has been redrawn again and again. In 1915, Stromer described a giant predator with a sail. In 1944, the original fossils were lost in the war. In 2014, new fossils gave it short hind legs and a life in the water. And in 2020, a paddle-like tail made it look more like a swimmer than ever.

Scientists are still arguing about how Spinosaurus really lived, and that is exactly the point: the picture is still changing. The full story is on Miya's Lab.

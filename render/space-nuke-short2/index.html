Why does every nuclear
explosion on Earth look
like a giant mushroom?
When a warhead detonates
in our atmosphere,
it creates a bubble
of air heated to
tens of millions of degrees.
Because this superheated
gas is far lighter
than the cold air
around it, it surges
upward like a giant balloon.
As it rises,
it drags dirt and
smoke up behind it
in a column.
When that column reaches
the stable upper atmosphere,
it spreads out and
flattens into that iconic
mushroom cap.
The whole shape depends
on air, gravity, and buoyancy.
But what happens if
you set off that
same warhead in the
vacuum of space?
In orbit, there is
no surrounding atmosphere,
no air pressure,
and no buoyancy.
Because there is no
cooler air to force
a fireball upward,
a mushroom cloud cannot form.
Instead, the vaporized
remains of the weapon
expand in every direction
at once.
To your eyes,
it would not look
like a pillar of smoke.
It would look like
an expanding, translucent
bubble of superheated plasma.
For an instant,
this glowing sphere would
outshine the midday sun,
a miniature, temporary
star in the blackness.
Then, because there is
no air to trap
its heat, the plasma
thins out.
Within seconds, the sphere
would stretch into a
faint, ghostly shell and
fade into darkness.
What remains is not
visible at all.
Remember, in space nothing
slows anything down.
With no air to
resist it, the cloud
of fast-moving debris keeps
spreading outward for as
long as it can,
growing thinner and thinner
until it is simply gone.
A wave of X-rays
and gamma rays races
outward at the speed
of light, and charged
particles stream away into
the dark.
Those particles do not
stay invisible forever,
though.
When they meet a
planet with a magnetic
field, strange things happen.
Our own planet's magnetic
shield once caught the
debris from a real
high-altitude test in 1962.

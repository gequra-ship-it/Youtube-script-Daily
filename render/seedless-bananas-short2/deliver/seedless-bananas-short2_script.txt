===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

The banana you eat today is not the banana your grandparents ate. The world has already lived through one banana crisis, and another may be starting right now.

For much of the first half of the twentieth century, the banana trade was dominated by a variety called the Gros Michel, nicknamed Big Mike. It had a thick skin and grew in heavy bunches that could be shipped whole, on the stem, without bruising easily. Many people described its flavor as richer and sweeter than the Cavendish.

Like every commercial banana, the Gros Michel was grown from cuttings, so whole plantations were clones. In a varied crop, some plants carry natural differences that help them survive. In a field of clones, if a disease can kill one plant, it can kill them all.

Starting in the late 1800s, a soil fungus called Fusarium oxysporum, in a form known as Tropical Race 1, spread through banana plantations in Central America. It entered through the roots and clogged the plant's water-carrying tissues. The disease became known as Panama disease. Its spores can survive in the soil for decades, and no fungicide could clear the infected fields.

By the middle of the twentieth century, large areas of infected land were abandoned. The rescue was a variety called the Cavendish, named for William Cavendish, the sixth Duke of Devonshire, whose Chatsworth greenhouse grew early plants that had come by way of Mauritius. The Cavendish could resist Tropical Race 1.

Many described the Cavendish as less flavorful and more delicate, but it resisted the disease and gave good harvests, so the world switched to it. Today, nearly all the bananas exported to wealthy countries are Cavendish, with estimates ranging from about ninety-five to ninety-nine percent.

Then a newer strain of the fungus, called Tropical Race 4, appeared. It can attack the Cavendish. It was confirmed in Colombia in 2019, in Peru in 2021, and in Venezuela in 2023. Once it is in a field, its spores can stay in the soil for decades.

Researchers are fighting back with a genetically modified Cavendish called QCAV-4, approved in Australia in 2024, as well as gene editing and breeding. Many experts also say we need more variety. Watch the full deep dive on Miya's Lab to see the whole story of the banana.

What about the world
hidden beneath the waves,
where the planet drops
into darkness?
To understand the vertical
scale of our world,
we must look at
its deepest places.
If mountains are monuments
of tectonic collision,
ocean trenches are chasms
of planetary recycling.
In the western Pacific,
near the Mariana Islands,
the seabed plunges into
the Mariana Trench.
At its lowest known
point, the Challenger Deep,
the ocean floor lies
nearly eleven thousand
meters below the surface.
The trench is a
crescent-shaped scar more
than two thousand five
hundred kilometers long,
formed where the Pacific
plate dives beneath a
neighboring plate and sinks
into the mantle.
Everest stands 8,848 meters
tall, and the Challenger
Deep reaches deeper than
that by more than
two kilometers.
At the bottom,
the water pressure is
more than a thousand
times the pressure at
the surface.
To grasp that depth,
imagine an inverted mountain
of stone.
Suppose you could carve
Mount Everest from its
roots and drop it
into the Challenger Deep.
The entire mountain would
slip beneath the dark water.
Its jagged summit,
which pierces the sky
on our continents,
would still sit more
than two kilometers under
the surface.
The deepest abyss on
our planet is deeper
than the highest mountain
is tall.
Why can ocean trenches
plunge eleven kilometers
down, while our tallest
mountains rise less than nine?
Part of the answer
is the strength of
rock and the pull of gravity.
A mountain is an
immense load pressing down
on the Earth's crust
and the slowly flowing
mantle beneath it.
Geologists suspect that
a mountain much taller
than ten kilometers would
crush the rock beneath
it, forcing that rock
to deform and flow
like soft plastic.
Trenches are different.
They form where one
tectonic plate dives beneath
another, so they are
not piled up against
gravity at all.
This raises a thrilling
question: what happens
when we leave Earth entirely?
Mars is a smaller
world, with only about
thirty-eight percent of
our planet's surface
gravity, and it has
no active plate tectonics.

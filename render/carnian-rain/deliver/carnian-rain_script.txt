===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Imagine waking up to the sound of rain against your window. Now imagine that it does not stop. Not tomorrow, not next year, not in your lifetime, and not in the lifetime of anyone you will ever know. Around 234 million years ago, something like that happened to our planet. For roughly one to two million years, Earth went through a long, wet chapter that scientists call the Carnian Pluvial Episode. And when the clouds finally cleared, the world belonged to a new kind of animal.

The story starts with rocks. In England, there is a thick series of reddish layers called the Mercia Mudstone Group. They formed in a hot, dry world, layer after layer of desert dust and salty mud. But in the middle of those red layers, geologists found something that did not fit: bands rich in clay and plant debris, the kind of sediment carried by rivers in a much wetter climate. Why would a desert suddenly fill with rivers?

On November 10, 1987, two British geologists, Michael Simms and Alastair Ruffell, got talking at Birmingham University. Simms had been studying fossils and Ruffell had been studying sediments. They realized they were seeing the same thing from two different directions. In 1989, they published their idea in the journal Geology. Somewhere in the Carnian age of the Late Triassic, they argued, the climate had turned much wetter, and it had done so over a very large area.

At first, many researchers were not convinced. A wet interval inside a famously dry period seemed strange. But over the following decades, the evidence kept piling up. Geologists found the same change in the Dolomites of Italy, in river sandstones in Germany, in lake sediments in China, and in rocks from many other regions. What began as an odd stripe in English mud turned out to be a signal written into rocks around the world.

To understand why this was such a shock, we need to picture the planet at the time. Around 234 million years ago, almost all the land on Earth was joined into one supercontinent, Pangaea. It was surrounded by a single enormous ocean called Panthalassa. Pangaea was so huge that moist air from the sea struggled to reach its heart. Large parts of the interior were hot and dry, with deserts and seasonal rivers.

This world was also still healing. About 20 million years earlier, at the end of the Permian period, Earth had suffered the worst mass extinction we know of. Around 95 percent of marine species disappeared. By the Carnian, life had recovered and rebuilt new ecosystems on land and in the sea. Then, just as things had settled, the climate was about to be shaken again. How many shocks can a living world take?

And here is a surprise. Dinosaurs already existed, but they were not in charge. They had appeared millions of years earlier, yet they stayed small and rare. The land belonged to other animals. There were heavy plant-eaters such as rhynchosaurs, with beak-like snouts, and dicynodonts, distant relatives of mammals. And there were pseudosuchians, the ancient relatives of crocodiles, including large predators and armored plant-eaters. Dinosaurs lived in their shadow.

So what broke the dry world? Most scientists point to a massive volcanic event. Far out in the ocean, in a region now attached to the coast of western Canada and Alaska, lava poured out over a long period. Geologists call it the Wrangellia Large Igneous Province. Its remains still exist today, including a layer of volcanic rock estimated to be about six kilometers thick beneath most of Vancouver Island.

The scale is hard to imagine. According to one estimate, Wrangellia produced more than a million cubic kilometers of basalt, and that may be an underestimate, because some of the rock has since disappeared into the Earth. But the real danger was not the lava itself. It was what came with it. Volcanoes release gases, and the most important one here was carbon dioxide.

How do we know the carbon cycle was disrupted? Carbon atoms come in different forms called isotopes, and their balance is recorded in rocks and fossil wood. At the start of the Carnian Pluvial Episode, that balance shifts sharply, in rocks from Europe and Asia. In some places, scientists can even count separate pulses, three or possibly four of them. In China, researchers found four spikes in carbon and mercury signals spread over about 1.65 million years.

How can scientists measure the temperature of a sea that vanished over 200 million years ago? One clever method uses conodonts. These were small, eel-like animals that left behind tiny tooth-like elements made of a mineral called apatite. The oxygen atoms locked inside those teeth carry a record of the water temperature they grew in. By comparing teeth from before and during the episode, researchers can estimate how much the ocean warmed.

And how quickly did the change begin? That question is still open. Some researchers have proposed that the onset may have been very rapid in geological terms, perhaps unfolding over roughly 15,800 years, as feedbacks in the carbon cycle amplified the first push from the volcanoes. Others have suggested that methane, released from frozen deposits on the sea floor, may have added to the warming. Fifteen thousand years sounds long to us. To a planet, it is a sudden jolt.

Here is the scale of the change. The episode began around 234.5 million years ago and lasted roughly one to two million years. Fossil teeth of tiny eel-like animals called conodonts suggest warming of at least 3 to 4 degrees Celsius. And in the oceans, around one third of all species were lost.

Warming and rain are closely linked. A warmer ocean evaporates more water, and warmer air can hold more moisture. When that moist air rises and cools, it falls as rain. So as the planet heated up, the water cycle sped up too. More water was lifted from the vast ocean, and more of it fell onto the land. In a world built around one giant continent, that extra moisture changed everything.

Pangaea already had a powerful seasonal climate. In summer, the huge landmass heated up and pulled in moist air from the ocean. Many scientists describe this pattern as a mega-monsoon. During the Carnian, warming may have pushed this system even harder. Some researchers have also proposed that newly rising mountains helped trap rain in certain regions. Either way, places that had been dry for a very long time began to receive far more water.

So did it really rain nonstop for two million years? Not quite. The episode was not one single storm that never paused. It came in several pulses, wetter and drier phases, spread over roughly one to two million years. There were still seasons, droughts, and ordinary days. But the overall climate was much more humid than before. Over such a long time, that difference was enough to rebuild entire landscapes.

One of the strongest clues is the type of clay. A mineral called kaolinite tends to form when rocks are weathered under warm, wet conditions, with plenty of rain soaking through the soil. In England, the Carnian layers contain kaolinite-rich clays together with plant debris. Some limestone areas even developed karst, a landscape of caves and hollows carved by water. Could a dry world really leave such wet fingerprints? The rocks say yes.

And the landscapes did change. In Germany, a famous layer called the Schilfsandstein, which means reed sandstone, records large river systems carrying sand and plant material. In England, the red mudstones gave way to clay and plant debris. Lakes expanded in China. Rocks that had formed in dry basins now recorded running water, wetlands, and soils shaped by rain.

One of the most beautiful clues is amber, fossilized tree resin. In the Dolomites, scientists found Carnian amber in ancient soils. It is the oldest known amber that preserves arthropods and microorganisms. In 2012, researchers described two tiny mites and part of a fly trapped inside drops of this amber, about 230 million years old. Scientists suspect the humid climate helped trees produce and preserve so much resin.

And here is a strange detail. After this time, amber nearly vanishes from the rock record again. It does not show up in comparable amounts until much later, in the Early Cretaceous. In other words, the Carnian left behind a little window of resin, a burst of sticky treasure that would not be matched for over a hundred million years. Why the forests of that time produced so much of it is still being studied.

The oceans were hit hard. Ammonoids, the coiled-shell relatives of squid, suffered heavy losses. So did conodonts, crinoids, which are sea lilies, and tiny colony animals called bryozoans. One of the carbon pulses lines up with major extinctions of ammonoids and conodonts. When ocean chemistry changes quickly, animals that build shells and skeletons from carbonate often struggle most.

In some places, the seas lost oxygen. In South China, dark layers called black shales formed during the episode. These rocks form when the bottom water has so little oxygen that dead material does not fully decay. Some of them preserve remarkable fossils of sea lilies and ichthyosaurs. Black shales are less widespread in this event than in some other extinctions, but they show that parts of the ocean became deadly for life on the sea floor.

But even in the ocean, the crisis opened a door. After the episode, a group called scleractinian corals diversified rapidly. These are the stony corals that build our reefs today. Scientists link their success to a partnership with tiny algae living inside their tissues, the same partnership that powers modern reefs. The Carnian also marks the start of modern-style coral reefs and of many modern plankton groups.

Among those plankton were two groups that still shape the oceans now. Coccolithophores, tiny algae covered in plates of chalk, and dinoflagellates, single cells that spin through the water with whip-like tails, both appear early in the record around this time. Today, coccolithophores help build chalk deposits and cycle carbon through the sea. It is remarkable to think that part of the modern ocean took shape during this long wet age.

On land, the plants changed too. Conifers, ferns, and a group of extinct plants called bennettitaleans diversified. Fossil pollen and spores show more plants that loved moisture, and lots of fern spores and freshwater algae. For the first time since the great extinction at the end of the Permian, about 15 million years earlier, coal swamps returned. The land was becoming greener and wetter.

How can we see this so clearly? Through scientists called palynologists, who study microscopic pollen and spores trapped in rock. Pollen grains are tough, and they are produced in huge numbers. By counting which types appear in each layer, researchers can reconstruct which plants grew nearby. Across the Carnian layers, the pollen record shifts toward plants that needed water, a microscopic diary of a changing world.

For the old rulers, these changes were bad news. Rhynchosaurs had been common plant-eaters in the Middle Triassic. During the Carnian, most of their lineages died out, leaving only one group, the hyperodapedontines, which disappeared later, early in the next age. Scientists have proposed that changes in vegetation played a part. Animals built to eat certain plants can suffer when those plants decline.

And then came the dinosaurs. In 2018, Massimo Bernardi, Mike Benton, and their colleagues studied dinosaur footprints in the Dolomites, where the rock layers are well dated. The pattern was striking. As Benton put it, first there were no dinosaur tracks, and then there were many. The change lines up with the end of the Carnian Pluvial Episode, around 232 million years ago.

Look at the timeline. Dinosaurs appeared about 20 million years before the rainy episode, yet they stayed rare. The wet episode began around 234.5 million years ago. And the earliest dinosaur-bearing layers of the Ischigualasto Formation in Argentina date to around 231 million years ago.

Ischigualasto, sometimes called the Valley of the Moon, is one of the most important places in the world for early dinosaurs. Its rocks have produced skeletons of some of the oldest dinosaurs known, such as the small Eoraptor and the larger predator Herrerasaurus. At the same time, in Argentina and Brazil, the first extensive dinosaur skeletons appear. Something had changed in the dinosaurs' favor.

Eoraptor, one of those early dinosaurs, was only about a meter long, light enough to move quickly on two legs. Looking at it, you would hardly guess that its relatives would one day include the giant long-necked sauropods and fearsome predators like Tyrannosaurus. Back then, there was nothing obviously special about these animals. Was their future written in their bones, or did they simply get lucky when the world changed?

Herrerasaurus was one of the early predators of this new world. It walked on two legs, had sharp, curved teeth, and could reach several meters in length. By later standards, it was not a giant. But in its time, it showed that dinosaurs were becoming more than small, rare animals at the edge of the ecosystem. They were starting to take up important roles as hunters and as plant-eaters.

So why did dinosaurs win? This is where the story gets more interesting than a simple headline. Mike Benton has suggested that it was the sudden return of drier conditions after the humid episode that gave dinosaurs their chance. In other words, it may not have been the rain itself, but the upheaval, the loss of old plant-eaters and the reshaping of the plants, that opened space for newcomers.

Dinosaurs did have some useful features. Their legs were held straight under the body, supported by an open hip socket, rather than sprawled out to the side. This upright posture is an efficient way to walk and run. Many early dinosaurs were light and moved on two legs. Scientists have proposed that traits like these helped dinosaurs spread quickly once the old ecosystems were disrupted.

And here is a twist you may not expect. We often picture dinosaurs laying hard, chalky eggs like birds. But a study published in 2020 suggested that the very first dinosaurs probably laid soft-shelled eggs, more like many turtles and lizards. Hard eggshells seem to have evolved later, more than once, in different dinosaur groups. So the earliest dinosaurs succeeded without the armored eggs we often imagine.

It is also worth knowing that not every scientist sees the rain as the main cause of the dinosaurs' rise. Some studies find that the diversity of dinosaurs and their close relatives grew faster in the ages just before and just after the Carnian. In that view, the Carnian Pluvial Episode was one important step, but not the only driver. The rise of the dinosaurs may have taken many millions of years and more than one push.

Even so, the list of newcomers from around this time is remarkable. The researchers behind the 2020 review linked the Carnian to the appearance or early spread of many modern groups on land: dinosaurs, and some of the first turtles, crocodiles, lizards, and mammals. Conifers spread. New insect groups appeared. In the oceans, modern-style reefs and plankton began. That is why they called it the dawn of the modern world.

Among the animals living in these changing forests were small cynodonts, relatives of mammals. Scientists trace the ancestry of mammaliamorphs, a group that includes mammals and their closest relatives, back to around this time. They were small, probably active at night, and easy to overlook. But their descendants would one day include whales, elephants, bats, and us.

For decades, scientists talked about the five great mass extinctions of the past 500 million years, such as the one at the end of the Permian, or the asteroid impact that ended the age of dinosaurs. The Carnian Pluvial Episode was not on that list. But after reviewing the evidence, Jacopo Dal Corso put it simply: we have identified another great extinction event. It was a crisis that destroyed, but also created.

There is another reason we can study this time at all. Fossils form best when remains are buried quickly, before they rot or are scattered. Rivers, floods, and lakes are good at doing that. Wetter landscapes with more moving water and sediment created more chances for plants and animals to be buried in mud and sand. Some of what we know about this period exists because the water covered it up.

Scientists who study the Carnian also see a lesson for today. The episode shows how strongly the climate can respond when large amounts of carbon dioxide enter the air, and how warming can speed up the water cycle and reshape life. The situations are not the same: the Carnian unfolded over thousands of years and longer, in a very different world. But the basic physics of warming and water is the same physics we live with now.

We usually think of rain as gentle, something that feeds rivers and makes things grow. The Carnian shows the other side of water. Over a long enough time, extra rain can move mountains of sediment, change which plants can survive, and push old ecosystems past their limits. And yet the same water also made room for coral reefs, conifer forests, and a new group of animals to rise.

Next time you stand beneath a dinosaur skeleton in a museum, think about where that story began. Not with a giant, but with small, rare animals living in the shadow of others. Their world was shaken by volcanic eruptions, warming, and a long wet age. When the dust settled and the rivers retreated, the dinosaurs were ready, and they went on to rule the land for more than 150 million years.

So, the next time it rains for days on end and you start to feel tired of it, remember that it once stayed wet for something like a million years or more. Out of that long, strange chapter came reefs, forests, and the age of dinosaurs.

Have you ever tried
to mentally picture a
fourth spatial direction,
only to feel your
brain slam against a
total brick wall?
You can easily imagine
moving left and right,
forward and backward,
up and down.
Every corner of your
room features those three
familiar ninety-degree angles.
But the moment you
try to visualize a
fourth axis that is
perpendicular to all three
of them at the
exact same time,
your imagination
short-circuits.
Why is our mind
so utterly blind to
something mathematics can
calculate with ease?
The answer is not
a failure of intelligence;
it is most likely
an outcome of biological
evolution.
Hundreds of thousands of
years ago, our ancestors
survived on the prehistoric
savannah by solving
immediate physical challenges.
They needed to judge
the sudden leap of
an approaching predator,
calculate the arc of
a thrown hunting spear,
and spot ripe fruit
across a valley.
Every single survival
problem our ancestors faced
took place strictly within
three spatial dimensions.
Natural selection probably
had no reason to
spend precious metabolic
energy building neural
hardware for
four-dimensional navigation.
Imagine an early human
who spent their days
trying to visualize
hypercubes instead of
watching out for snakes
in the tall grass.
They may well not
have survived to pass
on their genes.
Our sensory organs and
cognitive wiring were shaped
for a three-dimensional
world because that was
the boundary of our
physical niche.
Yet here is the
triumph of human
consciousness.
Even though our biological
wetware cannot project
a mental picture of
the fourth dimension,
our mathematics can navigate
it with flawless precision.
Through algebra and
geometry, we can explore
ten, twenty, or a
hundred dimensions without
ever getting lost.
We may be biological
creatures living in a
three-dimensional world,
but our intellect has
already escaped into the
higher realms of the cosmos.

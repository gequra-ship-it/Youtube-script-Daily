What decides whether a
baby favors the left
hand or the right?
Geneticists searched for
a single left-handed gene
for decades.
For decades, geneticists
searched fervently for
a singular, mendelian
"left-handed gene" that
could cleanly explain the
trait's inheritance.
If your mother and
father are both left-handed,
you might expect their
offspring to be guaranteed
lefties, right?
Even if both of
your parents are
left-handed, you still
have only about a
one-in-four chance of being
left-handed yourself.
The strongest evidence
challenging a simple genetic
switch comes from studies
examining identical twins.
Identical twins share one
hundred percent of their
nuclear genetic code,
making them perfect natural
laboratories for nature
versus nurture inquiries.
If handedness were purely
determined by standard
dominant and recessive
alleles, identical twin
pairs should always display
identical handedness without
exception.
However, clinical studies
show that roughly one
in five identical twin
pairs are discordant:
one is right-handed,
and the other is left-handed.
If identical DNA does
not dictate hand preference,
what else could possibly
be pulling the strings
behind the scenes?
In 2019, researchers at
the University of Oxford
conducted the largest
genome-wide association
study on handedness to
date, analyzing DNA from
over four hundred thousand
people in the UK Biobank.
Their findings argued
strongly against a
single-gene explanation.
Handedness is not controlled
by one solitary master
switch; it is a
polygenic trait influenced
by many small genetic
variants working in concert.
Handedness is not a
hardwired command written
into your genome,
but rather an intricate
symphony of subtle genetic
whispers.
Intriguingly, the genetic
variants linked to
left-handedness heavily
overlap with genes
responsible for building
the cytoskeleton,
specifically structures
called microtubules.
This same molecular
machinery establishes
left-right asymmetry in
our internal organs,
determining why human hearts
consistently settle on
the left side while
livers grow on the right.
Left-handedness appears
intimately tied to this
foundational biological
blueprint that tells an
embryo how to distinguish
its left side from
its right side.
There is no switch.
There are many small dials.

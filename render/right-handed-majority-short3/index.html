Long before a baby
ever holds a rattle,
handedness may already
be decided.
Ultrasound imaging reveals
that by the tenth
week of gestation,
human fetuses tend to
move one arm more
frequently than the other.
By the fifteenth week,
an overwhelming ninety
percent of fetuses can
be observed sucking their
right thumb rather than
their left thumb.
Longitudinal studies
tracking these unborn babies
into adolescence found
that prenatal thumb
preference predicted their
later hand dominance.
Handedness appears to take
shape months before our
first breath.
For over a century,
neuroscientists assumed
that handedness originated
exclusively in the cortex
of the brain.
But in 2017,
a study from Ruhr
University Bochum challenged
that view.
At eight to twelve
weeks of gestation,
the motor cortex of
the brain is not
yet connected to the
spinal cord.
Yet, asymmetric movement
in fetal arms is
already observable at this
stage.
Researchers discovered
asymmetric gene expression
occurring directly inside
the cervical spinal cord,
long before the brain
takes command.
Hand preference may actually
start not as a
cerebral decision, but
as a local spinal reflex.
To comprehend why the
species leans so
aggressively to the right,
we must examine the
asymmetrical architecture
of the human brain.
The nervous system is
wired contralaterally:
the left cerebral hemisphere
controls the muscles on
the right side of
the body, and vice versa.
In about ninety-five percent
of right-handed people,
essential language
processing—housing Broca's
area for speech production
and Wernicke's area for
comprehension—is localized
strictly in the left
hemisphere.
Evolutionary linguists
hypothesize that human
language did not begin
with vocal grunts,
but rather with intricate
manual gestures.
As our ancestors learned
to hunt cooperatively and
craft complex tools,
communicative hand signals
became vital survival tools.
Because the left hemisphere
was already fine-tuning
fine motor precision for
right-hand tool
manipulation, it naturally
adopted gestural
communication as well.
Over millions of years,
as gestural signing
gradually transformed into
spoken syntax, speech
processing nestled into
the very same
left-hemisphere machinery.
Right-handedness and spoken
language effectively evolved
hand-in-hand, reinforcing
one another through
evolutionary synergy.
Your hands began to
be chosen in the
dark, long before you
could choose.

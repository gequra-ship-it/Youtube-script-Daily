Open your mouth and say hello.
Did you know that
the jaw you just
used was invented by a fish?
Your backbone, your jaw,
and the bones of
your arm were all
first tried out by
strange animals hundreds
of millions of years ago.
So where did your
body's toolkit come from?
Start with the backbone.
Pikaia was a small,
flattened swimmer, only
a few centimeters long,
found in the Burgess Shale.
It had a notochord,
a flexible rod along
its back, and blocks
of muscle like those
of a fish.
That makes Pikaia an
early chordate, part of
the group that includes
all animals with backbones.
Now the jaw.
The leading idea is
that jaws came from
the bony arches that
supported the gills.
Every jaw today,
including yours, traces
back to them.
Next, the arm.
Our own ancestors were
working on fleshy, lobed fins.
Inside their fins was
a chain of bones
that matches the upper
arm, the forearm,
and the wrist in
your own arm.
In 2004, on Ellesmere
Island in the Canadian
Arctic, a team searching
rocks of just the
right age found Tiktaalik.
It lived about 375
million years ago.
It had scales,
gills, and fins like
a fish, but also
a flat head like
a crocodile, a neck
that could move,
and fin bones arranged
like a simple wrist.
It was a fish,
but a fish partway to walking.
After Tiktaalik came early
four-limbed animals like
Acanthostega and Ichthyostega.
Acanthostega had eight
digits on each front
limb, but it still
had internal gills and
a tail fin,
and its limbs were
weak for walking.
That suggests something
surprising: feet may have
first evolved for moving
through water and weeds,
not for walking on land.
One more invention helped
animals leave the water.
Then, sometime in the
Carboniferous, a group
of animals evolved the
amniotic egg.
So the backbone traces
back to small swimmers,
the jaw to gill
arches, and the bones
of your arm to fleshy fins.
Many of the tools
your body uses were
first tried out in
these ancient seas,
swamps, and deserts.

story, from the first

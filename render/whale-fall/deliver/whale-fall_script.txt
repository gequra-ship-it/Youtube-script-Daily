===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

The blue whale is the largest animal known to have ever lived. It can grow longer than a basketball court and weigh close to 200 tonnes. Whales spend decades crossing entire oceans, feeding, singing, and raising their young. Some bowhead whales are thought to live for more than two centuries. But every giant eventually takes its last breath. So what happens to a body that size when it dies? The answer leads to one of the strangest and most beautiful hidden worlds on our planet.

You might imagine that a dead whale simply disappears. It does not. A large whale's body holds tens of thousands of kilograms of flesh, fat, and bone. In the deep sea, that is an almost unimaginable amount of food. And what happens next can feed a whole community of strange animals for decades, possibly for up to a century. To understand how, we need to follow the body down, step by step, from the surface to the bottom of the ocean.

First, a question. Does a dead whale float or sink? Most great whales are actually slightly denser than seawater. They only float when their lungs hold air. So when a whale dies with little air in its lungs, it can begin to sink right away. But if decomposition starts first, bacteria inside the body produce gases. Those gases can inflate the carcass and keep it at the surface for days, drifting with the wind and the currents like a giant balloon.

Sometimes that gas has nowhere to go. In January 2004, in Tainan City, Taiwan, a dead sperm whale was being transported through the streets when the gas inside its decomposing body made it burst. And in 1970, in Florence, Oregon, officials tried to get rid of a rotting sperm whale on a beach by blowing it up with dynamite. Chunks of blubber rained down on the onlookers. Exploding whales are rare, but they are real. Still, the more remarkable story happens somewhere else.

When a carcass sinks in the open ocean, scientists call it a whale fall. Once the lungs deflate, the body can sink quickly and stay mostly intact, because there are few scavengers waiting in the open water on the way down. The deeper it goes, the colder the water gets, which slows decay. And the rising pressure squeezes any remaining gas into solution, so there is less to lift the body back up. Slowly, the giant leaves the light behind.

On its way down, the whale passes through layers of the ocean. Near the surface is the sunlit zone, where there is enough light for plants and algae to grow. Below about 200 meters begins the twilight zone, where light fades to a dim blue. And below about 1,000 meters lies the midnight zone, where no sunlight reaches at all. Whale falls are typically found deeper than 1,000 meters. The body is now entering a world that has never seen the Sun.

To understand why a whale fall matters, you need to picture the deep seafloor. It is cold, dark, and under crushing pressure. And above all, it is hungry. Most animals down here survive on marine snow, a slow drift of tiny particles falling from the waters above: bits of dead plankton, waste, and dust. Much of the abyssal seafloor is, in effect, a food desert. Then, one day, an entire whale arrives. For the animals living here, it is like a feast falling from the sky.

The body settles onto the soft mud, sending up a slow cloud of sediment. In a place where the floor can be flat for kilometers, a mountain of food has suddenly appeared. Scientists describe what follows as a series of stages. The first three are named after the animals that dominate them: the mobile scavengers, the enrichment opportunists, and the sulfur-loving, or sulfophilic, community. Some scientists add a fourth, the reef stage. Let's follow them in order.

But first, how do we even know any of this? In 1977, US Navy pilots in a deep-diving vessel called Trieste II found a whale skeleton on the deep seafloor. It had been stripped completely of tissue. Then, in 1987, a team led by the oceanographer Craig Smith, diving in the research submersible Alvin, found a whale skeleton at about 1,240 meters in the Catalina Basin off California. This time, it was covered in life. That discovery opened a new field of study.

Back to our whale. Even in total darkness, news of a feast spreads. As the body begins to break down, chemicals seep into the water and drift away with the currents. Deep-sea scavengers are very good at following these chemical trails. One by one, and then in crowds, they start to arrive. For animals that may go a very long time between meals, this is not a chance to miss. The first and most dramatic stage has begun: the mobile scavengers.

Some of the first large visitors are sleeper sharks. These are slow, heavy-bodied sharks of the cold deep sea. They move with very little effort, saving energy in a place where food is rare. At a whale fall, they bite into the blubber and twist their bodies to tear pieces away. Bony fish join them, along with crabs and other scavengers. A whale carcass can become one of the busiest places on the seafloor, all happening in complete darkness.

Then come the hagfish, often in large numbers. Hagfish are eel-shaped animals with no jaws. Instead, they use tooth-like plates made of keratin, the same material as your fingernails, to rasp away at flesh. They can burrow right into a carcass and feed from the inside. And they have a famous defense. When attacked, a hagfish can release a huge amount of slime in a fraction of a second, enough to clog the gills of a predator. Fossil hagfish date back around 300 million years.

The crowd also includes crustaceans: amphipods, tanner crabs, and galatheid crabs, sometimes called squat lobsters. In some deep waters, there are giant isopods too, distant relatives of the pill bugs you may find under a garden stone. The giant isopod Bathynomus giganteus usually grows to between 19 and 36 centimeters, and the largest confirmed one was about 50 centimeters long. These armored animals feed on the remains of dead creatures that sink to the ocean floor.

Together, these scavengers work astonishingly fast. Researchers estimate that they can remove 40 to 60 kilograms of soft tissue every day. That is roughly the weight of a person, eaten every single day. This mobile scavenger stage lasts from a few months up to about a year and a half. By the end, the flesh, the blubber, and the organs are gone. What is left is a skeleton, lying on the mud. But for the deep sea, the story is only beginning.

Here is the timeline. The mobile scavenger stage lasts from months up to about a year and a half. The enrichment opportunist stage lasts from months up to about four and a half years. And the sulfophilic stage can last from 50 to possibly 100 years.

Now the second stage begins: the enrichment opportunists. During the feeding frenzy, scraps of tissue and fat were scattered across the mud around the skeleton. The sediment there is now rich in food. Small animals that can move in and multiply fast, such as bristle worms, snails, and tiny crustaceans, take over the bones and the nearby mud. They reach densities far higher than on the ordinary seafloor around them. The whale is now feeding a crowd of tiny animals.

How long can this last? In one study, scientists followed a 30-tonne whale fall in the northeast Pacific for years. The enriched sediment around it stayed rich for about six to seven years. The researchers counted 100 species of small animals living in the mud around the whale. Ten of the most abundant ones were found only at whale falls. In other words, some animals seem to depend on dead whales to exist at all. And yet, the biggest treasure is still untouched.

That treasure is inside the bones. Whale bones are rich in fat. In fact, the lipids stored in a whale's skeleton can represent about four to six percent of its entire body weight. For a large whale, that is a huge store of energy. But it is locked inside the bone, out of reach for sharks and hagfish. To unlock it, you need a completely different kind of life, one that does not need oxygen, and does not need sunlight.

This is the third stage, the sulfophilic, or sulfur-loving stage. Bacteria that live without oxygen move into the bones and break down the fats inside. To do this, they use sulfate, which is dissolved in seawater, and they release hydrogen sulfide as a by-product. That is the gas that smells like rotten eggs, and for most animals it is toxic. This stage is the longest of all. It can last from 50 to possibly 100 years.

Here is where it gets strange. Other bacteria can use that hydrogen sulfide as a source of energy. Instead of sunlight, they run on chemistry. This is called chemosynthesis. These bacteria can form pale mats over the bones, and they become the base of a whole food web. Some clams near whale falls carry such bacteria inside their bodies, living partly on the chemical energy their partners produce. A dead whale has turned into something like a deep-sea hot spring, but fueled by bone.

How crowded can one skeleton get? In 2003, two researchers, Amy Baco and Craig Smith, compared whale skeletons in this sulfur-rich stage with other deep-sea habitats powered by chemistry. On average, a single skeleton held about 185 species of small animals. That was more than the richest hydrothermal vent field known at the time, which had about 121. Think about that for a moment. One dead whale, lying alone in the dark, can support more kinds of animals than an entire field of volcanic hot springs.

Now meet one of the strangest animals ever found on a whale. In February 2002, researchers from the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute were studying a dead gray whale lying at about 2,893 meters deep. On its bones, they found tiny worms that nobody had described before. The genus was formally named in 2004: Osedax, which means bone devourer. People often call them zombie worms. They look like small red flowers growing on the skeleton.

Here is the surprise. Osedax has no mouth, no gut, and no anus. So how does it eat? Above the bone, it waves colorful feathery plumes that work as gills, taking oxygen from the water. Below, it pushes root-like structures down into the bone itself. Those roots are where the feeding happens. In other words, the worm does not swallow its food. It grows into it. Half of its body lives inside the skeleton it is consuming.

How does a soft worm get into hard bone? It dissolves it. The roots of Osedax secrete acid that wears away the outer layers of the bone. Studies found high levels of enzymes in the roots that help produce that acid. Inside the roots live bacteria that make enzymes to break down collagen, the tough protein in bone. With their help, the worm gets nutrients from the collagen and the fats locked inside. A worm without a mouth is, quite literally, digesting a whale from the inside of its bones.

Osedax has another secret. The worms you see on the bones are females. The males are microscopic. Between 50 and 100 dwarf males can live inside the tube around a single female. They never grow past the larval stage, and the females are more than 20,000 times larger.

Since then, scientists have found Osedax on much more than whales. It has turned up on fish bones, shark teeth, bird and reptile skeletons, and seal bones. Researchers have even placed cow bones on the seafloor as experiments, and the worms colonized them too. More than 30 species have been named so far. Wherever bone reaches the deep seafloor, these strange little flowers seem to find it.

How long has this been going on? Fossils give a surprising answer. In early Oligocene rocks in Washington State, laid down in deep water, scientists found whale bones riddled with tiny borings. The traces look like the ones Osedax makes today, and they are about 30 million years old. That is close to the time when whales were first spreading through the oceans. In other words, as soon as there were whale bones on the deep seafloor, something seems to have been eating them.

But the story may go back even further. In 2015, researchers Silvia Danise and Nicholas Higgs used CT scanning to look inside fossils from England: bones of a plesiosaur and a sea turtle, about 100 million years old. Inside, they found bore holes and hollows that matched the work of Osedax. If they are right, these bone eaters were feasting on giant marine reptiles long before the first whale ever swam. The whale fall may be a new chapter in a much older story.

Whale falls may matter far beyond one skeleton. Some animals at whale falls are related to animals living at hydrothermal vents and cold seeps, other deep-sea places powered by chemistry. Those places can be very far apart. Scientists have proposed that whale falls may act as stepping stones, places where larvae can land and grow on the way between distant habitats. It has even been suggested that whale falls helped some lineages move into and adapt to harsh new environments over evolutionary time.

How close are those stones? Researchers estimate that around 690,000 carcasses of the nine largest whale species are in some stage of decay at any one time. That means an average spacing of about 12 kilometers, and as little as 5 kilometers along whale migration routes.

And what happens when the fat and the sulfur are finally used up? Some scientists describe a final reef stage. By then, the organic material is gone and only the minerals of the bones remain. But even bare bones matter on the deep seafloor, which is mostly soft mud. A hard surface is valuable real estate. Animals that need something solid to attach to, such as sponges, corals, and other filter feeders, can settle on the bones and lift themselves above the mud.

Whales are not the only gifts that sink. Trees and branches washed out to sea can become waterlogged and fall to the deep seafloor. Scientists call these wood falls. They are quickly found by small clams called Xylophaga, which bore into the wood. These clams carry bacteria in their gills that help them digest it. Like a whale fall, a sunken log becomes a brief island of food in the dark, and researchers compare the two to understand how life finds such rare treasures.

How many whale falls are out there right now? We are only beginning to find out. A 2022 study counted 45 natural whale falls, 38 that scientists had placed on purpose, and 78 found as fossils. And in 2023, a survey by the Scripps Institution of Oceanography found at least seven whale falls across 135 square miles of seafloor off California, with sonar hints of up to 60. The deep sea may be scattered with far more of these hidden oases than we have ever seen.

Whale falls also carry carbon into the deep. A typical 40-tonne whale carcass holds about two tonnes of carbon. When it sinks, that carbon is moved from the surface world to the deep seafloor. According to one estimate, a single carcass delivers as much carbon as a hectare of abyssal seafloor would normally receive over 100 to 200 years. Whales, it turns out, are not only giants in life. They are also giants in how they move carbon around the planet.

Look at the scale. A typical whale carcass of 40 tonnes holds about two tonnes of carbon. That equals the carbon that would reach a hectare of the abyssal seafloor in about 100 to 200 years.

But humans interrupted this ancient cycle. Researchers estimate that between 1900 and 1999, the whaling industry killed about 2.9 million whales. Most of those bodies never reached the seafloor. They were cut up and processed for oil and other products. So the deep sea lost a huge number of whale falls. Scientists have suggested that whaling also reduced the deep ocean's ability to store carbon. How many whale-fall species became rare before we even knew they existed? No one knows for sure.

Today, scientists study whale falls with remotely operated vehicles and submersibles. Some research teams deliberately sink the carcasses of whales that have washed up on beaches, and then return again and again to watch what happens. These experiments let them follow every stage, from the first sharks to the last worms. And they keep finding new species, some of which may live only on whale falls. Every visit to a whale fall is a visit to a world we are still learning to describe.

Think about the path of that energy. A blue whale feeds on tiny krill near the surface, in the sunlight. Decades later, its body feeds sharks, hagfish, and crabs in total darkness. Then it feeds worms and snails in the mud. Then bacteria that run on chemistry, worms without mouths, and clams that live on sulfur. Finally, its bones become a reef. Nothing about the whale is wasted. One life becomes the foundation for countless others.

So the next time you look out at a calm ocean, remember what may be happening far below. Somewhere down there, in the cold and the dark, a whale that once swam in sunlight is still feeding a whole community of life, perhaps for longer than a human lifetime.

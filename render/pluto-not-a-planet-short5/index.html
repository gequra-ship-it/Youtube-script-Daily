When NASA's New Horizons
spacecraft flew past Pluto
in July 2015,
scientists expected to
see a dead,
cratered chunk of ice.
The spacecraft launched
in January 2006,
only months before Pluto
lost its planet title,
and traveled for nine
and a half years,
nearly five billion
kilometers.
It rushed past at
about fourteen kilometers
every second, and its
closest approach was about
twelve thousand kilometers
from the surface.
Instead, the images sent
shockwaves through astronomy.
A vast basin of
frozen nitrogen, named
Sputnik Planitia, forms
the left half of
a giant heart shape.
It spans about one
thousand kilometers.
The whole heart is
named Tombaugh Regio,
after the man who found Pluto.
Inside the basin,
the ice is cut
into cells like a
slowly boiling pot or
a lava lamp,
and it has almost no craters.
That means the surface
is young, constantly renewed
by ice that flows
like glaciers.
Towering mountains of
rock-hard water ice rise
up to about three
kilometers high.
Pluto also has five
known moons, and the
biggest is Charon.
Scientists think its
reddish-brown patches come
from chemical compounds
called tholins, which form
when sunlight acts on methane.
Above the mountains,
a thin atmosphere of
nitrogen carries a faint
blue haze.
Scientists also found shapes
that may be ice
volcanoes, and some evidence
hints at a liquid
ocean deep beneath the
frozen crust, though that
is still an open question.
The surface is about
two hundred and thirty
degrees below zero Celsius.
Yet Pluto is not
a frozen corpse drifting
through oblivion.
It is an active
world with a beating
geological heart.
Demoting Pluto was never
an insult.

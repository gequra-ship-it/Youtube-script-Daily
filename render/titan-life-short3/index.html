Here is where planetary
science takes a fascinating
turn.
On Earth, life depends
on liquid water to
dissolve nutrients and
carry out its chemical
reactions.
But some scientists have
proposed that, under the
right conditions, life
on Titan might use
liquid methane as its
solvent instead.
One idea is that
hypothetical organisms
in those frigid lakes
would breathe hydrogen
instead of oxygen and
consume acetylene for energy.
They would be fundamentally
alien, with cell membranes
built without the
water-loving molecules
that define every creature
on Earth.
Would we even recognize
such strange life if
it swam right beneath
our robotic cameras?
Consider what is being
asked of these imagined
creatures.
The cell membrane is
the thin wall that
separates a living thing
from its surroundings,
and on Earth it
is built from molecules
that get along with water.
In a lake of
methane, a membrane would
need a completely different
design.
Such life would not
be a cold version of us.
It would be something
else entirely.
It is worth being
clear about what kind
of statement this is.
Life based on methane
is a hypothesis.
No one has found
such an organism,
and no one has
shown that one is possible.
It is a thought
experiment that scientists
take seriously because
it forces us to
ask what life really needs.
If life can work
without water, then our
definition of a habitable
world is far too narrow.
If it cannot,
then we learn something
important about why water
is so special.
This is why Titan
matters to the search
for life.
It lets us test
an idea that has
lived only on paper:
that biology might not
be limited to the
single recipe we know.
Even if the answer
turns out to be
no, the question sharpens
our understanding of what
makes water so special.
Remember, though, that
this remains a thought
experiment, and thought
experiments are how science
often begins.

Before anything could rise,
engineers had to learn
what the ground could carry.
They drilled test holes
across the site and
examined the rock below.
The news was not ideal.
Instead of hard granite,
they found layers of
weak to very weak
sandstone and siltstone.
There was another problem
hiding in the groundwater.
It carries salts that
attack steel and concrete
over time.
So the foundation was
designed with cathodic
protection, a system meant
to slow the corrosion
of buried metal for decades.
Weak rock sounds like
a disaster for the
tallest building on Earth.
But the answer was
not to find better rock.
The answer was to
spread the load.
Engineers drilled close
to two hundred concrete
piles, each about one
and a half meters
wide, deep into the ground.
Think of each pile
as a long concrete root.
No single pile has
to hold the whole tower.
Together, the piles share
the weight among them.
On top of the
piles, engineers built
a thick concrete mat,
called a raft foundation.
To see why,
picture walking across
deep snow.
In high heels,
you sink at once,
because your whole weight
is focused on two tiny points.
A snowshoe spreads that
same weight across a
broad surface, and you
can walk across the powder.
The raft is an
architectural snowshoe.
It ties the piles
and the tower together
so they work as one.
The numbers are enormous.
The raft is about
3.7 meters thick,
roughly twelve feet.
The foundation took more
than 45,000 cubic meters
of concrete, weighing over
110,000 tonnes.
All of it exists
only to spread out
the load of the tower above.
Giant slabs of concrete
have a hidden problem.
Curing concrete gives off
heat, and the thicker
the slab, the hotter
its middle gets.
In the Dubai desert,
that is even harder to manage.
So engineers tested many
concrete mixes until they
found ones that could
handle the heat.
It worked.
When the tower was
finished, it had settled
by only about fifty
millimeters, roughly two
inches.
Baker called that tiny
for a building of this size.

The Rh factor is
not what you think.
Look at the small
plus or minus sign
after your blood type.
That sign belongs to
the Rh system,
named after the rhesus
monkeys whose blood was
used in experiments in 1940.
Most people think of
Rh as a simple
switch: positive or negative.
The truth is far richer.
The discovery is credited
to Karl Landsteiner and
Alexander Wiener, and the
name stuck even though
later work showed the
monkey protein is not
exactly the same as
the human one.
What everyday language
calls the Rh factor
is really a single
protein called the D antigen.
It is the strongest
immune trigger in the
system, which is why
doctors check it so carefully.
But the full Rh
system is a large
family of more than
fifty antigens, built from
two neighboring genes on
chromosome one.
Someone who is Rh
negative typically lacks
only the D protein,
and still carries the
other Rh antigens,
such as the ones
called C, c, E, and e.
The D protein matters
long before any transfusion.
If an Rh-negative mother
carries an Rh-positive
baby, her immune system
can build antibodies against
the baby's D antigen,
and those antibodies can
threaten later pregnancies.
Today this is largely
prevented with a treatment
called anti-D
immunoglobulin, which
doctors offer during and
after pregnancy.
Now imagine a rare
genetic change wiped out
the whole family.
Not just D,
but every Rh antigen.
Those proteins sit in
the cell membrane like
part of its framework,
so a red cell
without any of them
is a strange thing to picture.
Then, in 1961,
doctors in Australia
examined the blood of
an Aboriginal woman and
found exactly that.
Not one Rh antigen
was present on her
red blood cells.
She was not Rh
positive, and she was
not an ordinary Rh negative.
Hematologists named her
blood type Rh-null.
It is inherited:
a child must receive
the rare change from
both parents, which is
why it often turns
up in families where
the parents are related.
The Rh factor was
never a single switch.

Fire an electron at
two narrow slits.
What happens next is
one of the strangest
results in science.
A foundational experiment
of quantum physics is
the double-slit experiment.
When physicists fired solid
particles of matter,
electrons, through two
narrow barriers, they
expected two neat lines
to appear on the
detector screen.
That is precisely what
classical mechanics and
common sense demanded from
solid physical objects.
Instead, what materialized
was a stunning interference
pattern of alternating
light and dark bands,
the unmistakable signature
of a wave.
How could solid,
discrete units of matter
behave like rippling waves
in an ocean?
The pattern builds up
even when electrons are
sent one at a
time, as if each
single electron passes
through both slits at
once, interfering with
its own ghost.
Stunned by this
impossibility, researchers
placed microscopic detectors
at the slits to
observe which path the
electron actually chose.
And that is when
reality revealed its deeply
disturbing, almost
paranormal secret.
The moment an observation
was made, the interference
pattern vanished.
The electron stopped acting
like a spread-out wave
and behaved like a
single point particle,
creating two plain lines.
The mere act of
measurement fundamentally
changed the outcome.
In the standard
interpretation, this is
called the collapse of
the wave function,
and it suggests that
subatomic reality has no
definite state until it
is measured.
In nineteen twenty-seven,
German physicist Werner
Heisenberg formulated a
cornerstone of quantum
theory: the Uncertainty
Principle.
Heisenberg showed that
it is physically impossible
to simultaneously know
the exact position and
the exact momentum of
a subatomic particle.
The more precisely you
pin down where an
electron is, the more
unpredictable its momentum
becomes.
This is not a
limitation of human
technology, nor is it
a temporary flaw in
our laboratory equipment.
It is an intrinsic
law woven into the
fabric of the cosmos itself.
In standard quantum theory,
the universe sets an
absolute barrier on what
can be known about
a particle at once.
That is where quantum
physics begins.

Fire electrons at a
wall with two narrow slits.
Common sense says you
should see two stripes
on the screen behind it.
But what physicists actually
saw is one of
the strangest results in
science, and it all
comes down to whether
anyone is watching.
In nineteen sixty-one,
the physicist Claus Jonsson
sent electrons through
slits and saw a
pattern of many light
and dark bands,
the signature of waves
overlapping.
Matter was behaving like
a wave.
Electrons are pieces of
matter, so that was
not supposed to happen.
In nineteen eighty-nine,
a team in Japan
led by Akira Tonomura
sent electrons through
the apparatus one at a time.
Each electron landed as
a single dot,
like a particle.
But as thousands of
dots piled up,
the striped pattern slowly
appeared.
In standard quantum theory,
the electron is described
by a wave of
possibilities that spreads
through both openings.
Many physicists put it
loosely by saying the
electron goes through both
slits.
How to picture that
is still debated,
but the pattern itself
is a measured fact.
Now add a detector
at the slits to
find out which path
each electron takes.
The moment the experiment
records that information,
the striped pattern
disappears, and two plain
bands remain.
Learning the path destroys
the interference.
Does this mean a
human mind changes reality
just by looking?
That is the most
popular misunderstanding
in physics.
What matters is whether
the path information gets
recorded in the physical
world.
Even an automatic detector
that nobody reads is
enough to wash the
pattern away.
Nobody has found an
experiment showing that
consciousness plays a
special role.
The observer in real
laboratories is a photon
detector, a bit of
gas, a speck of dust.
Reality at the small
scale is strange,
but it does not
need a person to
look at it.
What does all this
say about order,
cause, and God?

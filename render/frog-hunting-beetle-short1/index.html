Imagine a tiny creature
that sets a trap
for an animal much
bigger than itself.
And the trap is a dance.
This is the larva
of Epomis, a ground beetle.
At night, it waits
on damp ground,
near the places where
frogs and toads hunt.
It looks soft and small.
To a hungry frog,
it looks like an easy snack.
To understand the trick,
you need to know
how a frog sees.
A frog's visual system
is tuned to catch
small moving things.
A tiny dark spot
that twitches tells the
frog to strike.
That reflex feeds frogs
every day, and Epomis
has learned to use it.
The larva does not run.
It stays still,
and then it starts
to move its antennae
and its jaws.
It waves them up
and down, and from
side to side,
in a slow rhythm.
To us it looks
like a strange little dance.
To a frog,
it can look like
a worm wriggling on
the ground.
In 2011, researchers at
Tel Aviv University in
Israel, Gil Wizen and
Avital Gasith, studied
this behavior in the
laboratory.
They counted the movements.
When a toad was
fifteen centimeters away,
the larva waved about
three times a minute.
When the toad was
only one centimeter away,
it waved about five
times a minute.
Larvae in a control
group, with nothing to
lure, barely moved at all.
So the dance is
not random shaking.
It changes with the
distance to the victim.
The closer the target
comes, the harder the
larva works to look like food.
Now the frog turns
toward the lure.
It lowers its head,
and it creeps forward.
The larva does not flinch.
It keeps waving,
again and again,
while the frog comes closer.
That is the heart
of the trap.
The frog thinks it
is the hunter.
It sees a helpless
meal, and it moves
in with confidence.
But every step it
takes is a step
toward something that has
been waiting for exactly
this moment.
The larva is not
strong, and it is
not fast on the ground.
Its whole weapon is
a good disguise and
a very good sense of timing.
It does not need
to chase anything,
because the prey walks
right up to it.
The frog sees dinner.
The beetle sees a victim.

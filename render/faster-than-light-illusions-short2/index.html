Can a shadow outrun light?
Imagine standing in front
of a powerful spotlight
in a vast auditorium,
casting your shadow onto
a giant wall far behind you.
If you slowly raise
your hand, your shadow
rises across the wall.
Now, imagine that the
back wall is moved
light-years away into deep
space, onto a massive
interstellar dust cloud.
If you move your
fingers in front of
the lamp, the shadow
cast onto that cosmic
cloud would sweep across
vast light-years in mere
seconds.
Does this mean darkness
can travel faster than
the light that creates it?
Once again, the answer
lies in what a
shadow actually is.
A shadow is not
a physical object,
nor is it a
tangible substance flowing
across space.
A shadow is simply
an absence, a geometric
region where incoming
photons have been blocked
from arriving.
Just like the sweeping
laser dot, the darkness
at one end of
the dust cloud has
no physical connection
to the darkness at
the other end.
No information, energy,
or mass travels across
the surface where the
shadow falls.
Consider another classic
thought experiment involving
a giant pair of scissors.
Picture a pair of
blades that stretch across
entire astronomical units,
extending far past the
orbit of Mars.
As you slowly close
the handles on Earth,
the point where the
two sharp edges intersect
begins to slide outward
along the blades.
If the angle between
the two blades is
infinitesimally small,
that point of intersection
will race forward toward
the tips at a
velocity far greater than
the speed of light.
Does this mean the
intersection point breaks
the cosmic limit?
Physicists will tell you
that the intersection point
is an abstract mathematical
concept, not a material
object.
There is no physical
particle moving along the
blades; it is simply
a geometric location where
two pieces of metal
overlap in space.
Furthermore, if you close
the handles on Earth,
the mechanical force must
travel through the atoms
of the blades via
electromagnetic interactions.
The blades themselves would
bend, transmitting the
motion through sound waves
in the steel,
which travel tens of
thousands of times slower
than light.
The tips would not
even begin to move
until more than a year later.

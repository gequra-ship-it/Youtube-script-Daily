450 MILLION YEARS AGO
50 MILLION YEARS
A fish the size
of a shoe wore
a shell of bone
from nose to belly.
Why would it carry
that much weight?
Sacabambaspis was a jawless
fish that swam in
Ordovician seas roughly
450 million years ago.
Its front half was
covered in two large
bony shields, one on
the back and one
on the belly.
Behind them, a flexible
tail drove it forward.
It had no fast,
sharp-turning body.
It had armor.
The armor was made
of a very old
kind of bone tissue
called aspidin.
It had layers,
like a sandwich.
A thin layer lay
at the base,
a spongy layer sat
in the middle,
and a hard,
ridged layer sat on top.
That layered build is
a clue that this
armor was designed to protect.
And the armor also
held a sensing system.
Under a microscope,
the surface of the
armor shows grooves and pits.
In early fishes like
this one, such grooves
held a sense organ,
called the lateral line,
that picks up vibrations
in the water.
If that was true
for Sacabambaspis, its
skin worked a bit
like a motion detector.
It could feel something
large moving nearby,
even without sharp eyesight.
So what was it afraid of?
We do not know for sure.
One idea is that
large hunters of that
time, such as the
big sea scorpions called
eurypterids and shelled
cephalopods, made armor
worth having.
That is an inference
from the animals that
lived in the same
seas, not a recorded attack.
To see where this
design came from,
go back another fifty
million years or so,
to the Cambrian Period.
In the Chengjiang fossil
beds of China,
paleontologists found
Haikouichthys and
Myllokunmingia, tiny,
finger-sized animals with
eyes and a flexible
rod along the back.
They are among the
earliest known vertebrates.
They had no bony
armor at all.
For tens of millions
of years, vertebrates stayed
soft.
Then armor appeared,
and with it came
a new idea about bone.
A popular hypothesis is
that bone started outside
the body, as a
coat, and only later
moved inward to become
the skeleton.
That idea is still debated.
But it fits what
we see in these fishes.
Armor came first,
long before anyone could
bite back.

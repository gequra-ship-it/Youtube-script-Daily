Another famous reading
of quantum theory is
the Many-Worlds
Interpretation.
Proposed by Hugh Everett
the Third in nineteen
fifty-seven, it takes the
mathematics of the
Schrödinger equation
completely seriously without
adding a collapse mechanism.
Everett dared to ask:
What if the wave
function never collapses
at all?
What if, every time
a quantum event has
multiple possible outcomes,
all of those outcomes
actually occur in branching
parallel realities?
If Everett is right,
reality is not a
single, curated terrarium
built for mankind,
but an unfathomable,
ever-branching multiverse.
It remains one
interpretation among
several, and it cannot
yet be tested directly.
In nineteen sixty-one,
the physicist Eugene Wigner,
who would later win
a Nobel Prize,
introduced a mind-bending
thought experiment known
as Wigner's Friend.
Imagine a physicist inside
an isolated laboratory
measuring a quantum particle.
To the friend inside,
the measurement is complete,
and the wave function
has collapsed to a
single definitive result.
But to Wigner,
standing outside the sealed
laboratory, the entire
room, including his friend,
remains in an entangled
superposition until Wigner
opens the door!
Laboratory tests with
entangled photons have
been reported to support
a version of this
idea, suggesting that two
observers may not always
agree on a single
set of facts.
When physicists John Wheeler
and Bryce DeWitt combined
quantum theory with the
geometry of the universe,
they produced the famous
Wheeler-DeWitt Equation.
What stands out is
what is missing from
the equation: the variable
for time, represented by
the letter t, has vanished.
The master equation of
the universe reads:
H-psi equals zero.
Some physicists read this
to mean that,
at the most fundamental
level, time may not
flow, and that the
time we experience may
emerge from quantum
entanglement.
This remains a hypothesis,
not a settled result.
Notice how often the
word may appears here.
These are ideas from
physicists working at the
edge of what experiments
can reach, and they
are still debated.
They are interpretations,
not final answers.

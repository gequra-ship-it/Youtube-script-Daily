For decades, many scientists
believed that life was
a fragile thing that
could only survive in
warm, sunlit places.
Then, in 1977,
researchers diving in a
deep-sea submersible found
something that changed
biology.
On the pitch-black ocean
floor, hydrothermal vents
were spewing hot,
mineral-rich fluids from
the Earth's crust,
and around them lived
bizarre ecosystems thriving
in total darkness.
The water there is
scalding, and the pressure
is hundreds of times
higher than at the surface.
Even so, microbes at
the base of this
food chain do not
need sunlight.
They live on chemicals
like sulfur and hydrogen,
powered by the heat
of the planet itself.
One microbe has even
been grown in the
lab at one hundred
and twenty-two degrees
Celsius, hotter than boiling
water.
Scientists have since found
microbes living deep inside
solid rock, in radioactive
waste, and in lakes
buried beneath Antarctic ice.
This discovery changed
astrobiology forever.
If living cells can
flourish around vents at
the bottom of our
own oceans, then frozen
worlds in our solar
system suddenly look far
more interesting.
Evidence strongly suggests
that Jupiter's moon Europa
and Saturn's moon Enceladus
hide oceans of liquid
water beneath their icy
crusts, kept from freezing
in part by tidal
friction, the stretching
and squeezing caused by
gravity.
Spacecraft flying through
the plumes of Enceladus
have found salts,
organic molecules, and
hydrogen.
In 2005, the Cassini
spacecraft spotted jets
of water vapor and
ice erupting from Enceladus,
which is how we
know so much about it.
NASA's Europa Clipper,
launched in 2024,
is now on its
way to study Europa up close.
Some scientists think life
on Earth may have
begun near similar vents,
though that is still
a hypothesis.
No one has found
life on either moon yet.
But the lesson from
our own planet is clear.
Life may be far
tougher than we ever
imagined, and it may
appear wherever chemistry
and energy meet.

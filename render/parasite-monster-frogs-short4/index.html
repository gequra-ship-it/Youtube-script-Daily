Here is a puzzle.
Parasites usually do best
when their hosts stay
alive and healthy.
So why would Ribeiroia
cripple its frog?
When the tadpole finishes
its metamorphosis and crawls
onto land, it is
badly damaged.
A normal frog relies
on powerful hind legs
to leap away from
danger, dive into water,
and catch fast insects.
A frog with tangled
legs cannot jump properly.
Every attempt ends in
clumsy flailing, and even
moving a few inches
takes huge effort.
This is the part
that makes biologists shiver.
The deformity may not
be a side effect
that happens to hurt the frog.
Researchers argue that
it is part of
what makes the whole
plan work.
Remember, the worm has
no eyes and no brain.
It plans nothing.
Evolution did the planning
over a very long time.
Now look at the sky.
A heron, an egret,
or a hunting duck
glides above the reeds.
Waterbirds like these are
final hosts for the parasite.
Only inside a bird's
gut can the adult
worm mature and reproduce.
But a microscopic worm
in a pond cannot
simply fly up and
infect a bird.
Here is the sinister genius.
The parasite builds a
bridge, and the bridge
is predation.
A healthy, agile frog
spots the heron,
leaps into deep water,
and escapes.
A crippled frog is
a sitting duck on
the shoreline.
Scientists call this
parasite-induced trophic
transmission.
When a heron spots
a deformed frog thrashing
in the shallows,
its spear-like beak strikes
downward and the bird
swallows the frog whole.
The frog's life ends.
But for the parasites
encysted in its distorted
legs, the plan has worked.
In the bird's stomach,
the cyst walls dissolve.
The flatworms attach to
the intestinal wall,
mature into adults,
and produce eggs.
The eggs leave the
bird in its droppings.
The bird then flies
on, perhaps for many
miles, and drops them
into new ponds and wetlands.
The eggs hatch into
miracidia, which begin
hunting for ramshorn snails.
The circle is complete.
Through evolution alone,
a brainless flatworm has
manipulated the body of
a vertebrate to steer
its own journey across
the sky.

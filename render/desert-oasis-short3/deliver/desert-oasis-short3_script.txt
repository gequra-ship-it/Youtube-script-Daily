===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

How do you carry water across a desert for many kilometers without losing it to the sun? About three thousand years ago, engineers in ancient Persia found an answer, and it was hidden underground.

They called it the qanat. Builders dug a well at the foot of a hill to reach groundwater. Then they dug a tunnel with a gentle downhill slope from that well to the fields below, using a line of vertical shafts for air and for hauling out soil. Gravity did the rest.

Why underground? Because in the desert, open water evaporates quickly. A canal on the surface would lose much of its water to the sun and wind. In a tunnel, the water stays cool and much more of it arrives. Some qanats are still in use today, and Iran's qanats are listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The idea spread widely. In the Sahara of Algeria, these tunnels are called foggaras. In Oman and the United Arab Emirates, falaj. In the Turpan Basin of western China, karez. And in Peru, the Nazca region has underground channels called puquios. Different cultures found the same solution to the same problem.

Oases also shaped trade. The Silk Road was not a single road but a network of routes linked by oasis towns. Caravans carrying silk, spices and other goods moved from one water source to the next. Cities such as Dunhuang, Samarkand and Palmyra grew rich as stops along the way.

Traditional oasis communities understood scarcity well. Water was usually shared by strict rules, often by time. In some North African oases, each family's share was measured by simple water clocks. In thirteenth-century Tozeur, in what is now Tunisia, the scholar Ibn Chabbat is credited with writing detailed rules for sharing the water. In the desert, fairness was a matter of survival.

There is a quieter danger too. When irrigation water evaporates under the desert sun, the salt stays behind, and year after year it builds up in the soil. This is called salinization. That is why drainage matters as much as bringing water in.

Tunnels, trade routes and strict rules: people built whole worlds around a few hidden drops. The full story of the oasis is on Miya's Lab.

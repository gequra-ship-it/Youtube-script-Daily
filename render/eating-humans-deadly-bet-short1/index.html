Imagine a disease that
makes people tremble,
lose their balance,
and burst into laughter
they cannot control.
It was real,
and it appeared in
a remote corner of
Papua New Guinea.
In the 1950s,
doctors reached the eastern
highlands, where the Fore
people lived.
There, a mysterious illness
was killing many of them.
The Fore called it
kuru, a word that
means shaking.
Outsiders gave it a
darker name: the laughing
death.
It began with clumsiness.
A person would stumble
on a path they
had walked a thousand times.
Then came tremors,
slurred speech, and sudden
fits of laughter that
had nothing to do with joy.
Within months to a
couple of years,
patients could no longer
sit up, swallow, or speak.
Kuru was always fatal.
Doctors were stumped.
There was no fever.
There was no sign of bacteria.
It did not behave
like malaria or influenza.
And the pattern was strange.
Kuru struck women and
children far more often
than adult men.
The clue was hidden
in a funeral custom.
Among the Fore,
relatives of the dead
sometimes practiced mortuary
cannibalism.
Anthropologists who studied
the custom describe it
as an act of
mourning and respect,
a way to keep
a loved one inside the family.
It was not hunger,
and it was not violence.
It was grief.
According to those accounts,
the body was shared by custom.
Men often received the muscle.
Women and children were
more likely to handle
and eat other tissues,
including the brain.
And the brain was
where the danger was
concentrated.
In the 1950s,
the physician Carleton
Gajdusek and the medical
officer Vincent Zigas began
to study the disease together.
Years later, brain tissue
from kuru victims was
injected into chimpanzees.
After a long wait,
the animals developed the
same illness.
Kuru was infectious.
Think about how cruel that is.
A ritual created out
of love became the
path by which a
deadly disease moved from
one person to the next.
Nobody in the valley
could have known.
The germ was invisible,
and the illness might
not appear for many years.
That is how a
sacred act of love
became a toxic transmission
line.
But the real mystery
was only beginning.
What kind of infection
could hide in the
brain for decades,
ignore every known
treatment, and leave no
trace of a germ?
The answer turned out
to be something nobody
expected.

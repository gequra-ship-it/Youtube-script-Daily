MONTHS TO A FEW YEARS
A whale has just
landed on the deep
seafloor, and the guests
are arriving.
Meet the first three.
Among the first big
visitors are sharks.
Pacific sleeper sharks
have been filmed feeding
on whale carcasses on
the seafloor.
These sluggish,
ancient-looking predators
bite into thick blubber
and shake their bodies
to tear off chunks of flesh.
Because the food is
so abundant, animals that
usually hunt alone can
end up sharing the same meal.
Joining the sharks are
hagfish, primitive jawless
fish from a lineage
that has lasted hundreds
of millions of years.
With no true jaws,
they rasp flesh away
with toothlike plates made
of keratin.
Hagfish can burrow right
into a carcass and
eat it from the inside out.
And when threatened,
they release huge amounts
of slime, one of
the strangest defenses
in the animal kingdom.
Then come the crustaceans.
Giant isopods, armored
cousins of the pill
bugs in your garden,
can grow longer than
thirty centimeters and
are known for swarming
carcasses on the deep
seafloor.
They can survive long
stretches without food,
so when a feast
finally arrives, they make
the most of it.
Together with swarms of
smaller scavenging
crustaceans, they can cover
a carcass in a
shifting carpet of armor
and legs.
This stage is astonishingly
fast, even in the
cold and the dark.
Researchers estimate that
scavengers can strip forty
to sixty kilograms of
soft tissue from a
carcass every single day.
Within several months to
about two years,
the flesh, blubber,
and organs of one
of the largest animals
on Earth are gone.
What remains is a
bare skeleton resting on
the sea floor.
But the story is
only just beginning.
With the big scavengers
gone, the whale fall
enters its second phase,
the opportunistic stage,
which lasts for months
to a few years.
The target now is
not big chunks of
meat, but the scraps
scattered across the
surrounding mud.
During the feeding frenzy,
huge amounts of tissue,
fat, and organic dust
sank into the sediment.
This creates a nutrient-rich
halo around the skeleton
that reaches several meters
outward.
Next time you picture
the deep ocean as
empty, remember how fast
a feast can find you.

Physicists have tried to

But wait—what about
theoretical loopholes?
What about wormholes,
or the famous Einstein-Rosen
bridges that theoretically
fold spacetime?
Theoretical physicists
have explored whether a
traveler could cheat cosmic
expansion by taking a
shortcut through higher
dimensions.
Yet the physics of
wormholes presents enormous
barriers.
To keep even a
microscopic wormhole open
for a fraction of
a second would require
exotic matter with negative
energy—a substance that
may not even exist
in the amounts needed.
Even if such exotic
matter could be found,
other problems appear.
Some calculations suggest
that sending anything
through a wormhole could
disturb it so badly
that it would collapse
before the traveler gets
through.
These are open theoretical
questions, not settled
results, but none of
them points toward an
easy shortcut.
What about warp drives?
In 1994, physicist Miguel
Alcubierre demonstrated
mathematically that a
spacecraft could
theoretically achieve
superluminal speeds by
contracting space in front
of it and expanding
space behind it.
Inside the warp bubble,
the ship itself remains
stationary, so local special
relativity is never violated.
It sounds like the
perfect cosmic escape hatch.
But early calculations
put the energy cost
at roughly the mass-energy
of Jupiter, and the
design still calls for
exotic negative energy
that nobody has ever
produced in useful amounts.
Worse, some studies of
warp bubbles suggest that
a ship moving this
way could sweep up
particles along the way,
and release a burst
of high-energy radiation
when it stops.
Researchers still debate
how serious this problem is.
It is one more
reason the idea remains
purely theoretical.
Every route we know
of that promises an
escape toward the edge
of the universe runs
into a wall:
the infinite energy demanded
by relativity, the
accelerating expansion
driven by dark energy,
or the strange requirements
of quantum shortcuts.
As far as we
know, the laws of
nature are not designed
for interstellar nomads.

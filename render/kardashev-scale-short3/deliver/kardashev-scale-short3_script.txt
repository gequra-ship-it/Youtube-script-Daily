===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Look into the night sky. On a clear night, away from city lights, you can see a few thousand stars. Our galaxy holds hundreds of billions of them, and many are billions of years older than our Sun.

According to the Kardashev scale, a civilization that keeps growing keeps using more energy. Some estimates suggest that a civilization expanding across a galaxy could do it in a few million to a few tens of millions of years. That is a blink in cosmic time, and our galaxy is billions of years old, so there has been more than enough of it.

So why is the cosmos so quiet? Why have our radio telescopes found no confirmed signal from anyone else? This puzzle is known as the Fermi paradox.

One proposed answer is the Great Filter, an idea from economist Robin Hanson. Somewhere on the path from lifeless matter to a galaxy-spanning civilization, there may be a step that almost nobody survives.

One version of the idea suspects the step is the jump from Type Zero to Type One. A species that learns to command planetary energy may gain the power to destroy itself before it learns to cooperate.

Think about our own world. Within about seven years of the discovery of nuclear fission, the first atomic bombs existed. We burned fossil fuels to drive global industry, and that is now changing the climate. We are building artificial intelligence and biotechnology with global consequences, while our institutions are still learning how to manage global risks.

Reaching Type One may mean surviving our own technological childhood. If the silence of space tells us anything, it might be that many civilizations stumble during this exact phase. But nobody knows. The silence has other possible explanations, and the Great Filter is only a hypothesis.

Humanity sits near Type zero point seven, balanced on the edge of that razor. Will we reach the stars, or will we join the silence? Explore the full story of humanity's cosmic ranking and our path forward in the full video on Miya's Lab.

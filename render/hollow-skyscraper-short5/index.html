Gravity is steady.
Wind is not.
As you climb higher,
the air changes.
At street level,
trees, hills and other
buildings break the wind
into gentle pieces.
Hundreds of meters up,
almost nothing slows it
down, and it rushes
past with enormous force.
When wind meets a
flat wall, it cannot
pass through.
It must go around.
As it slides past
the corners, it peels
away from the surface
and forms swirls of
air called vortices.
They do not form evenly.
A swirl forms on
one side, breaks away,
and then another forms
on the opposite side.
This is called vortex
shedding.
Each swirl gives the
building a small sideways
push, first one way,
then the other,
in a steady rhythm.
Here is the danger.
Every tall building has
a natural rhythm at
which it likes to sway.
If the swirls arrive
at that same rhythm,
the pushes add up,
just like a child
pumping their legs on
a playground swing.
Each push makes the
motion bigger.
This is called resonance.
Left unchecked, the swaying
grows.
It could stress the
structure, damage the facade
and make the upper
floors uncomfortable to
live in.
This is the real
nightmare for a skyscraper
designer, because no amount
of extra concrete simply
fixes it.
So how do you
tame this invisible beast?
Not with brute strength.
The designers used wind
tunnels.
The studies were run
by an engineering firm
called RWDI, in Guelph,
Ontario, in Canada.
They tested small models
of the tower while
air blasted past,
and measured the forces
on them.
Baker later recalled that
the first tests showed
their initial design would
not work.
So they reshaped it,
again and again.
Baker said you do
not accept the wind
as a given.
It is part of
the design process,
and the team kept
reshaping the building
until its shape worked
with the wind instead
of against it.
The result is a
tower that tapers as it rises.
Its edges are softened
into scalloped steps,
and those steps spiral
up the tower.
A museum exhibit describes
the goal in three
words: confuse the wind.
Here is why that works.
Because the shape changes
at every level,
the wind cannot find
one steady rhythm along
the whole height.
A swirl forming at
one height does not
line up with a
swirl forming at another.
Instead of one coordinated
push, the wind breaks
into small, disorganized
eddies.

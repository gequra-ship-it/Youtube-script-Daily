Before dinosaurs, before
reptiles, and before the
first four-legged animals
crawled out of the
ancient seas, dry land
was a harsh and
silent frontier.
The air was dry,
the ground was bare,
and the sun beat
down without any shade.
Yet even in this
world, small animals were
finding ways to survive
beneath the shadow of
eight-meter pillars called
Prototaxites.
Because no true forests
existed yet, these towers
were among the only
large structures casting
shade across the rocky plains.
To the earliest land
animals, such as tiny
millipedes, early relatives
of spiders, and primitive
springtails, a single trunk
may have looked like
an entire mountain.
Imagine being a creature
the size of a
grain of rice,
crawling up a wall
of pale tissue that
rises into the clouds.
Fossils show something
extraordinary: small tunnels
and chambers inside the
fossilized tissue of
Prototaxites.
Researchers think some
of these were dug
by early arthropods,
which may have sheltered
there from strong
ultraviolet light and drying
winds, and may even
have fed on the tissue itself.
If that is right,
the giant was both
a home and a meal.
We should be careful, though.
Burrows show that animals
used these giants,
but they cannot tell
us exactly how important
the giants were to
the first food webs on land.
Still, it is a
remarkable picture.
Animal life was already
finding a home in
the shadow of these
towers, long before plants
grew tall enough to
make a forest.
A shaded spot,
a damp surface,
and a bit of
food can make all
the difference to a
small animal in a
dry and windy world.
That may be exactly
what these towers offered.
But when the first
deep-rooted trees evolved,
the world changed.
Watch our full documentary
to see how the
rise of true trees
brought the age of
the giants to an end.

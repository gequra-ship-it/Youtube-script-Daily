The nautilus was never
the star of the
prehistoric ocean.
It lived on the
margins, ate scraps,
and grew slowly.
And that may be
exactly why it survived
the asteroid.
Today, it lives along
the steep slopes of
coral reefs, in the
dim twilight zone,
roughly two hundred to
seven hundred meters down.
Many ammonites are thought
to have lived in
the sunlit upper ocean.
Its thick shell helps
it withstand the pressure
there, and it generally
avoids warm, shallow water.
In a world built
on sunlight, depth mattered.
The nautilus has strong,
beak-like jaws, and its
pinhole eyes have no
lens, so it sees
only a blurry picture.
It uses up to
about ninety tentacles
to sense chemicals in
the water and find
food, such as dead
animals and shed crustacean
shells.
It also has a
very low metabolic rate,
and it can go
a long time without eating.
Ammonites probably lived
faster lives in bright,
food-rich water, which
would have meant a
steady need for fresh food.
When the asteroid hit,
dust, soot, and sulfur-rich
particles blocked much
of the sunlight for
months, perhaps years,
and made the planet colder.
Photosynthesis dropped
sharply, and the collapse
climbed up the food
chain, from plankton to
the predators above.
In the deep,
the effects were probably
slower and weaker.
Deep water buffers changes
in temperature, and acidic
water was diluted in
a huge volume of ocean.
At first, dead plankton,
fish, and other animals
sank from above as
marine snow, and a
strong-jawed scavenger
like the nautilus was
well placed to use
that brief feast.
When that rain of
food ended, its slow
metabolism let it survive
long periods with little
to eat, far longer
than an animal that
burns energy quickly.
Waiting out hard times
was exactly what the
nautilus was built to do.
We cannot observe those
ancient animals, so this
is an inference from
living nautiluses.
Even so, the nautilus
came through narrowly.
Today only a handful
of species remain,
and they are caught
for their shells.
In 2017, all nautiluses
were listed under CITES
Appendix II.

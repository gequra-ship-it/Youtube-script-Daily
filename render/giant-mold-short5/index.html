Did you know that
the mushrooms growing in
your local park belong
to a kingdom that
once built the tallest
towers on Earth?
The fungal kingdom has
been on land for
a very long time,
and its story is
stranger than most people
imagine.
Over four hundred million
years ago, giant pillars
called Prototaxites stood
eight meters tall,
while the first land
plants barely reached your
ankles.
But if these giants
ruled the land for
tens of millions of
years, why did they disappear?
The answer lies in
one of the greatest
revolutions in Earth's
history: the arrival of
true trees.
During the Devonian period,
plants like Archaeopteris
developed deep, branching
roots, sturdy wood,
and sprawling canopies
of leaves.
Archaeopteris could grow
to about thirty meters,
nearly four times the
height of the tallest
Prototaxites.
Forests spread across the
continents and cast
permanent shade over the
ground.
Their roots helped break
up rock and build
deep soil, which let
plants spread far from
the damp valleys.
Scientists suspect that
Prototaxites, which probably
depended on surface crusts
and mats of early
life, could not compete
in this new world.
The new wood was
also tough, and fungi
of that time may
not yet have been
able to break it down easily.
Slowly, the great pillars
vanished from the fossil
record by the Late Devonian.
The age of the
fungal towers was over,
and a new age
of forests had begun.
But fungi did not
lose the war.
Fungi as a whole
survived and spread their
thread-like networks through
the soil, where they
link up with tree
roots and trade minerals
for sugars.
Those partnerships are
far older than forests,
and they still support
forests today.
Without them, dead wood
would pile up and
forests would starve for
nutrients.

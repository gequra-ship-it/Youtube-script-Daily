===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Across human history, almost every society has shared one rule: you do not eat other people. We usually treat it as a moral line. But what if that rule is not only about morality? Some scientists argue that biology may have helped write it long before any law did. Today, we follow the evidence, from a mysterious disease in the mountains of Papua New Guinea to a protein that refuses to die, and to the calories inside a human body.

Our story begins in the 1950s, in the eastern highlands of Papua New Guinea. Steep ridges, thick forest, and scattered villages. The Fore people lived here, largely cut off from the outside world. In 1951, an Australian officer named Arthur Carey wrote down a name for a strange local illness: kuru. When Australian patrol officers and, later, doctors arrived, they heard about a sickness that kept taking lives. People began to tremble. They lost their balance. Slowly, they lost control of their own bodies. At its peak, around 1957, roughly 200 people were dying of it every year.

The Fore called the disease kuru, from a word that means to shake or tremble. Outsiders gave it a darker name: the laughing death, because some patients burst into sudden, uncontrolled laughter. The illness moved through three stages. First, a person could still walk, but unsteadily. Then they could no longer walk at all. In the final stage, they could not sit up, speak, or swallow. On average, the whole course lasted about a year. Over the decades, kuru killed roughly 2,700 people.

Look at how unevenly it struck. At its peak, about 200 people died of kuru each year. In total, roughly 2,700 lost their lives. And at the height of the epidemic, kuru was eight to nine times more common among women and children than among men.

In 1957, the physician Vincent Zigas and the American researcher Carleton Gajdusek published the first detailed medical study of kuru. They were baffled. Kuru did not behave like flu, cholera, or malaria. Patients had no fever. There was no sign of the usual inflammation. At first, the doctors even suspected a genetic cause, because the disease ran in families. But why did it strike women and children so much more than men? What was stalking this community?

The answer was hiding in how the Fore mourned their dead. When a relative died, the family did not simply bury the body. Instead, relatives ate it, as an act of grief, love, and respect. Anthropologists call this mortuary cannibalism. To an outsider, it sounds shocking. But for the Fore, it was a way of caring for the dead and keeping them close. Some researchers think the epidemic may have started around 1900 with a single person who developed a rare prion disease on their own, which the funeral custom then spread. In the 1960s, anthropologists who studied these customs proposed that this ritual was spreading the disease.

The body was shared according to custom, and that detail turned out to be crucial. Men usually ate the muscle. Women and young children usually ate the brain and other organs. And the brain is exactly where the infectious agent was most concentrated. That explained the strange pattern. The people who ate the brain were the people who fell ill. A ritual of love had quietly become a path for a deadly infection, passed from the dead to the living, generation after generation.

When pathologists examined the brains of kuru victims under the microscope, they found something striking. The tissue was full of tiny holes, like a sponge. Nerve cells had died and left empty spaces behind. Clumps of abnormal protein, later called kuru plaques, were scattered through the brain. The damage was often heavy in the cerebellum, the part of the brain that coordinates balance and movement, which fits the shaking and stumbling that patients showed. This pattern gives the whole family of these diseases its name: transmissible spongiform encephalopathy. In plain words, a contagious disease that turns the brain into something like a sponge.

Was kuru really infectious? Gajdusek and his colleagues at the US National Institutes of Health tested the idea. They took brain material from kuru patients and introduced it into chimpanzees. Then they waited. The results were published in 1966 in the journal Nature. One chimpanzee, named Daisy, developed a kuru-like illness within about two years. The disease could pass from one brain to another. In 1976, Gajdusek shared the Nobel Prize in medicine for this work. But one big question remained. What exactly was the infectious agent?

For decades, scientists believed every infection needed genetic material, DNA or RNA, to copy itself. Bacteria have it. Viruses have it. Parasites have it. But this agent broke the rule. It was unusually hard to destroy with heat, radiation, and disinfectants that would wreck any ordinary germ. In 1982, the neurologist Stanley Prusiner proposed a radical answer: an infectious particle made of protein alone, with no genetic code at all. He called it the prion, short for proteinaceous infectious particle. In 1997, he received the Nobel Prize for it.

To understand a prion, you need to know one thing about proteins. They only work when they fold into the right shape. Your healthy brain makes a normal prion protein, called PrP-C. Many cells make it, especially nerve cells, and scientists still do not fully understand its normal job. A misfolded version, called PrP-Sc, has the same building blocks but a different, abnormal shape. And here is the frightening part. When the misfolded version meets a normal copy, it can push that copy into the same wrong shape. It does not need to divide or reproduce. It converts. Like a molecular zombie.

That starts a slow chain reaction inside the brain. One misfolded protein converts another, and those convert more. The misfolded proteins stick together into clumps. The nerve cells around them die. And the body has no good way to break these clumps down, so the damage keeps adding up, year after year, until the brain can no longer do its work. There is no cure. Once the symptoms of kuru began, the disease was fatal.

Kuru is not alone. It belongs to a whole family of prion diseases. The most common one in people is Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, which appears in roughly one person per million each year, usually with no known cause. Some prion diseases run in families, because of changes in the prion protein gene. One of them, fatal familial insomnia, slowly destroys a person's ability to sleep. Different names, different routes, but the same basic culprit: a protein folded the wrong way.

So here is a natural question. Could you simply cook the meat until it was safe? Unfortunately, no. Ordinary germs like Salmonella are killed by normal cooking heat. Prions are in a different league. They can survive standard hospital sterilization. Formalin, the chemical used to preserve tissue, does not reliably destroy them. And in one laboratory study, some infectivity remained even after heating to 600 degrees Celsius. A kitchen fire does not come close.

Your immune system does not help much either. A prion is made from a protein your own body already makes, just folded the wrong way. So it does not look like a foreign invader. In these diseases, researchers see no antibody response, no fever, and no inflammation of the usual kind. The infection can travel quietly toward the brain without setting off a single alarm. Imagine a burglar who looks exactly like a member of the family. Nobody thinks to stop him.

Then there is the waiting. Most infections show themselves within days. Kuru took far longer. On average, symptoms appeared ten to thirteen years after exposure. In some cases, the silence lasted more than fifty years. Someone could have attended a funeral feast as a child, lived a healthy life for decades, married, raised children, and then fallen ill as an old person. By the time the first tremor appeared, the damage inside the brain had already been done.

If this sounds familiar, there is a reason. In the 1980s and 1990s, the United Kingdom faced a similar disaster in cattle. Cows had been fed meat-and-bone meal that contained the remains of other cattle. In other words, an industrial form of cannibalism. The result was bovine spongiform encephalopathy, better known as mad cow disease. It was first confirmed in 1986. In 1988, Britain banned feeding the remains of cattle and sheep back to cattle. But the damage was done. Later, the disease crossed into people, as a new illness called variant Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease.

The numbers were enormous. From 1986 to 2015, more than 184,000 cattle in the United Kingdom were diagnosed with the disease. About four million cows were killed to stop it. And by 2014, 177 people had died of the human form.

Cows are not the only animals affected. Sheep have suffered from a prion disease called scrapie since at least 1732. Infected sheep compulsively scrape their wool off against rocks and fences, which gave the disease its name. In 1967, a related disease called chronic wasting disease was identified in mule deer in Colorado. It has since been found in deer, elk, moose, and reindeer, from North America to Scandinavia. It has not been confirmed to infect humans, but scientists are watching it closely.

Cannibalism is not only a modern or tribal story. It is written into the deep past. At Gran Dolina, a cave in northern Spain, researchers found bones about 800,000 years old, from an early human species called Homo antecessor. The bones carry cut marks from stone tools, mixed in with the bones of bison and deer. They belonged to at least eleven individuals, all children or teenagers, and the researchers argued that this was eating for food and possibly a way of removing rivals. In Gough's Cave in England, about 14,700 years ago, people even shaped human skulls into cups. The skulls were carefully cleaned and trimmed, and the researchers think they may have been used for drinking. So our ancestors knew this act. The question is why it never became normal.

Here is one way to test it. Did people ever eat each other simply because it was good food? The archaeologist James Cole, at the University of Brighton, decided to count the calories. His 2017 study, published in Scientific Reports, used modern body composition data to estimate how much energy a human body actually contains, part by part. The answer surprised many people.

The numbers are modest. An adult man holds roughly 126,000 calories in total. Only about 32,000 of those are in the muscle, the part that works like meat. The heart gives about 650 calories, and the brain and spinal cord about 2,700.

Compare that with other prey. Deer muscle holds about 163,000 calories, five times more than human muscle. A woolly rhinoceros offers about 1.26 million, and a woolly mammoth about 3.6 million. For hunters, a large animal was a far better meal than a person.

And hunting people was dangerous. A deer runs away. A person plans, makes weapons, and fights back, often with friends. A hunt for another human risks injury, revenge, and war, all for a small reward in calories. A group that raids its neighbors also invites raids in return. Cole's conclusion was that when ancient people ate other people, food alone may not have been the main reason. Ritual, conflict, or desperation may have mattered more. In other words, the menu never made sense.

Biology adds another warning. Mercury released by burning coal, for example, settles into lakes and seas, where tiny organisms take it up and pass it on. Some pollutants, such as mercury and certain long-lasting industrial chemicals, do not leave the body easily. As they move up the food chain, they build up. Scientists call this biomagnification. It is why large predatory fish can carry more mercury than small ones. Humans eat high on the food chain, and studies have found such chemicals stored in human fat and tissue. How much any one person carries would vary a great deal.

There is another hidden shield that protects you whenever you eat beef, chicken, or fish. It is called the species barrier. Many germs that infect animals struggle to infect people, because our cells, our receptors, and our body chemistry are different. Think of it as a lock that their keys do not fit. Crossing that barrier usually takes a lucky mutation. Most of the time, the germs in animal meat are not built for a human body.

With human tissue, that shield is gone. Any germ living in another person, such as the viruses that cause hepatitis or HIV, is already adapted to human bodies. Its keys already fit our locks. That is why health workers treat human blood and tissue with such care, and why donated organs and blood are screened. During the 2014 Ebola outbreak in West Africa, burial customs that involved touching the bodies of the dead helped the virus spread. How dangerous a single exposure would be depends on the germ. But the protection that animal meat gives you simply is not there.

Even our feelings may carry this lesson. Disgust is not just a social habit. Researchers such as the late Val Curtis argued that disgust evolved to help us avoid infection. Think of the smell of rotting meat, and how fast you turn away. Brain imaging studies connect disgust to a region called the insula. Researchers found that this area becomes active both when people feel disgust themselves and when they see disgust on another person's face. It is a fast, automatic warning that keeps us away from things that could make us sick.

Here is a thought experiment, not a proven history. Imagine two ancient groups. In the first, people tolerate eating their dead. In the second, people feel strong disgust at the idea. Now imagine a prion disease appearing in both. In the first group, it spreads at every funeral, just as kuru did. In the second, it has no easy path. Over many generations, the second group would lose fewer people. In that case, natural selection could favor the disgust. This idea has been explored through reasoning and models, not through direct evidence, so it remains a possibility rather than a fact.

There may be a social side, too. A group that never sees its members as food can trust one another more deeply. People who share hunts, raise children together, and sit around the same fire need to feel safe with each other. Some researchers suggest that rules like this helped hold early communities together. It is a hypothesis, and hard to test. But it shows how a taboo can protect a group in more than one way.

Some genetic evidence points the same way. In 2003, researchers studied the prion protein gene, called PRNP. At position 129, people carry one of two versions, called methionine and valine. Those who carry two different versions, one from each parent, are more resistant to prion disease. The researchers found that the worldwide pattern of these versions fits what you would expect if prion epidemics, possibly kuru-like ones, struck human populations long ago. It is a clue, not a proof.

And in 2009, researchers found something even more remarkable among the Fore themselves. After studying more than 3,000 people, they discovered a new version of the prion protein gene, called G127V. It appeared in the region where kuru had raged, and nowhere else in the world. No kuru patients were found carrying it. In just a few generations, the epidemic seems to have left a mark of protection in the survivors. Evolution, caught in the act.

In Papua New Guinea, the epidemic ended once the practice stopped. After the Australian administration banned it in the 1950s, the Fore had largely stopped eating their dead by around 1960. New infections stopped too. Researchers kept watching the Fore for decades, recording every new case. But because of the long incubation period, people kept falling ill for decades. The last known deaths from kuru came in 2005 and 2009, half a century after the last funeral feasts. The laughing death had finally gone quiet.

The lessons reached modern hospitals. Prion disease has spread through medicine itself: through growth hormone extracted from human pituitary glands, through grafts of human brain membrane, and even through electrodes that had been used in a patient's brain and then cleaned. Ordinary sterilization may not inactivate prions. Australia alone has documented ten cases of prion disease caught through health care. So health agencies recommend destroying instruments used on high-risk cases, or combining heat and chemical treatment. An ancient epidemic helped shape modern safety rules.

So what does all of this add up to? Eating people carries real biological risks: prions that survive cooking and hide for decades, germs that need no adaptation, pollutants concentrated high in the food chain, and a calorie return that was never worth the danger. These facts do not explain every taboo, and cultures differ. But they suggest that the rule against eating people may rest on far more than morality alone.

Next time you hear about cannibalism in a legend or a horror film, remember the quieter horror behind it. It lives in a protein that folds the wrong way, in a disease that waits fifty years, and in a ledger of calories that never added up.

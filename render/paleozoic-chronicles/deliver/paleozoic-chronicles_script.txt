===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Put your hand on your chest and feel your heartbeat. Behind it are ribs, a backbone, and a pair of lungs. Above it are jaws that open every time you speak. Each one was first tried out by strange animals that lived hundreds of millions of years ago, long before the first dinosaur. So where did your body's toolkit come from? To find out, we have to travel back to a chapter of Earth's history called the Paleozoic Era.

The Paleozoic began about 539 million years ago and ended about 252 million years ago. That is close to 290 million years, longer than the time that separates us from the very first dinosaurs. Geologists split it into six periods: the Cambrian, Ordovician, Silurian, Devonian, Carboniferous, and Permian. Along the way, life invented shells, eyes, jaws, forests, limbs, and eggs that could survive on dry land. And it lived through some of the worst disasters our planet has ever seen. Let's follow it from the start.

Around 539 million years ago, the Cambrian Period began. Over the next twenty million years or so, the fossil record fills with animals that have hard shells, jointed legs, guts, and eyes. Most of the major animal groups we know today make their first appearance here. Scientists call it the Cambrian Explosion. Why did it happen so fast? Researchers have proposed rising oxygen, new genetic toolkits, and an arms race between predators and prey. Most likely, several of these worked together.

Much of what we know comes from rare places where even soft bodies were preserved. The most famous is the Burgess Shale, high in the Canadian Rockies. About 508 million years ago, underwater mudflows buried animals so quickly that their soft tissues survived. The paleontologist Charles Walcott found the site in 1909. One creature, Hallucigenia, was so odd that it was first reconstructed upside down, walking on its spines. Later researchers flipped it over, and found its head at the other end.

Swimming above that sea floor was one of the first large predators: Anomalocaris, whose name means unusual shrimp. Older books said it reached a meter long. Newer studies suggest the largest were closer to forty centimeters, still a giant in a world where most animals were a few centimeters long. It had two large compound eyes on stalks, with more than 24,000 lenses in a single eye, and a pair of spiny grasping arms at the front of its head.

But here is a twist. Its round mouth was small, and the plates around it show very little wear. Many researchers now think Anomalocaris could not crack hard shells, and instead hunted soft-bodied prey, perhaps sucking it in. The picture of a monster crunching through armored animals may be more legend than fact. With hunters like this, a new pressure arrived on Earth: the pressure to avoid being eaten.

One group answered that pressure brilliantly: the trilobites. Their bodies were divided lengthwise into three lobes, which gives them their name. Their shells were hardened with minerals, and many could roll into a tight ball. Their eyes were remarkable too. The lenses were made of calcite, a clear mineral crystal. Trilobites appeared in the early Cambrian and lasted for more than 250 million years, through almost the entire Paleozoic.

Then there was Opabinia, an animal so strange that when its anatomy was first presented at a scientific meeting, the audience laughed. It was only a few centimeters long. It had five eyes on stalks, and instead of jaws, a long flexible nozzle in front of its head, ending in a spiny claw. Scientists think it used the nozzle to grab food and pass it back to its mouth.

Not every important animal was big or bizarre. Pikaia was a small, flattened swimmer, only a few centimeters long, also found in the Burgess Shale. It had a notochord, a flexible rod along its back, and blocks of muscle like those of a fish. That makes Pikaia an early chordate, part of the group that includes all animals with backbones. It was probably not our direct ancestor, but it shows what our earliest relatives may have looked like: small, soft, and easy to overlook.

The Cambrian ended about 485 million years ago, after a series of extinction pulses hit trilobites and other groups. Then came the Ordovician Period, and a burst of diversity known as the Great Ordovician Biodiversification Event. Life did not invent many new body plans. Instead, it filled the seas with variety. Sea lilies, lace-like bryozoans, shelled brachiopods, and early corals turned the sea floor into crowded underwater neighborhoods.

The largest animals of these seas were nautiloids, relatives of today's squid and nautilus. Some had long, straight, cone-shaped shells. One of them, Cameroceras, is often said to have reached nine meters, but that number rests on a doubtful old report. Well-supported specimens point to shells of around two meters. Even so, a shelled hunter that size, probably with tentacles and a beak, was one of the biggest animals in the Ordovician ocean.

On land, the continents were still bare rock. Then, during the Ordovician, tiny plants began to creep out of the water. We know them mostly from their spores, which date back about 470 million years. They were probably similar to liverworts and mosses, with no roots and no wood, clinging to damp ground. Small as they were, they began to break down rock, help build the first soils, and change the air.

But the warm Ordovician seas did not last. The supercontinent Gondwana drifted over the South Pole, and great ice sheets spread across it. With so much water locked up as ice, sea levels fell sharply, by tens of meters and perhaps as much as a hundred. The shallow seas where so much life thrived began to drain away. What happens to a reef when the ocean pulls back?

About 445 million years ago, the Late Ordovician mass extinction struck in two pulses. The first came with the cooling and the falling seas. The second came as the ice melted and conditions changed again, with many seas losing oxygen. Around 85 percent of marine species disappeared. It ranks among the five great mass extinctions in Earth's history. Yet life survived, and in the next period it would climb onto land in earnest.

In the Silurian Period, which began about 444 million years ago, plants took a huge step. Cooksonia was only a few centimeters tall: bare green stems that split into Y shapes and ended in little caps full of spores. No leaves, no roots. But some Cooksonia fossils show traces of water-conducting tissue. Plants with this kind of internal plumbing, called vascular plants, could carry water upward. One day, that ability would let them grow into trees.

Where plants went, small animals followed. Arthropods already had tough outer skeletons that helped keep them from drying out. One of the oldest known land animals is Pneumodesmus, a millipede from Scotland. Its age has been estimated at around 428 million years, though newer dating suggests about 414. It had small openings called spiracles for breathing air. Millipedes and early arachnids lived among the damp plants, eating decaying matter, or each other.

Meanwhile, in the water, fish evolved something you used this morning: jaws. The leading idea is that jaws came from the bony arches that supported the gills. Over many generations, the front arches changed shape and became hinged, able to open and close. Jaws turned fish from grazers into hunters that could grab, cut, and crush. By the Silurian, jawed fish were spreading through the seas. Every jaw today, including yours, traces back to them.

But arthropods were still among the top predators, especially the eurypterids, or sea scorpions, relatives of scorpions and spiders. Pterygotus, a sea scorpion of the Silurian and Devonian, reached around one and a half meters long. At the front, it had long claws lined with teeth, made for grabbing prey. Studies of its eyes suggest sharp vision, like that of active hunters today. Its relative Jaekelopterus, at about two and a half meters, is the largest arthropod ever found.

Then came the Devonian Period, often called the Age of Fishes, beginning about 419 million years ago. Its best-known predator is Dunkleosteus, a placoderm, one of the armored fish, with a heavily armored head. It had no true teeth. Instead, its jaws carried sharp bony plates that sharpened against each other as they worked. Studies of its jaw mechanics suggest it could open its mouth in about a fiftieth of a second, fast enough to create suction that pulled prey inward. With a bite like that, it could likely cut through other armored fish.

But how big was it? For decades, popular books said up to nine meters. Then a 2023 study reexamined its skull and body proportions. It concluded that the largest Dunkleosteus were only about three and a half meters long, shorter and stockier than once believed, but still about as heavy as a great white shark more than four meters long. The real animal was smaller than the legend, but no less dangerous.

Several Paleozoic giants have been resized like this. Anomalocaris was once said to reach a meter, but the best estimate is now under forty centimeters. Cameroceras was said to reach nine meters, but well-supported shells suggest about two. And Dunkleosteus, once nine meters, is now estimated at about three and a half. Bigger stories travel faster than careful measurements.

While placoderms ruled with armor, our own ancestors were working on something else: fleshy, lobed fins. The lobe-finned fish include the ancestors of today's coelacanths and lungfish. Inside their fins was a chain of bones that matches the upper arm, the forearm, and the wrist in your own arm. In shallow, weedy waters, these strong fins could prop up the body, and many of these fish could also gulp air.

In 2004, on Ellesmere Island in the Canadian Arctic, a team searching rocks of just the right age found Tiktaalik. It lived about 375 million years ago. It had scales, gills, and fins like a fish, but also a flat head like a crocodile, a neck that could move, and fin bones arranged like a simple wrist. It could likely prop itself up in shallow water. It was a fish, but a fish partway to walking.

After Tiktaalik came early four-limbed animals like Acanthostega and Ichthyostega. Acanthostega had eight digits on each front limb, but it still had internal gills and a tail fin, and its limbs were weak for walking. That suggests something surprising: feet may have first evolved for moving through water and weeds, not for walking on land. Ichthyostega was sturdier, with strong ribs and forelimbs, and may have dragged itself across the shore.

On land, plants were going through their own revolution. During the Devonian, they evolved deep roots, true leaves, and wood. One of the first true trees was Archaeopteris, which could grow up to around thirty meters tall. Forests spread across the continents for the first time. Their roots broke rock apart and built deep soils, changing how rivers flowed and how the land looked.

But those forests may have helped cause a disaster. One hypothesis goes like this. As roots broke down rock, nutrients washed into the seas. Algae bloomed, then died and decayed, using up the oxygen in the water. At the same time, plants pulled carbon dioxide out of the air and cooled the planet. In a series of crises, including the Kellwasser and Hangenberg events, the Late Devonian lost roughly 70 percent of its species. Reefs collapsed, and by the end of the period, the armored placoderms were gone.

The Carboniferous Period began about 359 million years ago. Its name means coal-bearing, because its swamp forests left behind huge coal deposits. Strange trees called lycopsids, like Lepidodendron and Sigillaria, rose from the wet ground, with bark patterned like scales. By some estimates, the largest reached forty meters or more. Below them grew ferns, seed ferns, and giant horsetails.

In the air flew Meganeura, a giant relative of dragonflies called a griffinfly. Its wingspan reached around 70 centimeters, about the size of a hawk's. Like dragonflies today, it was probably a predator, catching other insects in flight with strong jaws and spiny legs. For roughly a hundred million years, insects had the skies to themselves.

On the forest floor crawled Arthropleura, the largest land invertebrate known. It was a giant relative of millipedes. A fossil found on a beach in northern England suggests it could reach about 2.6 meters long and weigh around 50 kilograms. It was once imagined as a fierce predator, but researchers now think it mostly ate plant matter, perhaps nuts and seeds from the leaf litter, and maybe sometimes small animals.

Let's put numbers on it. Today, oxygen makes up 21 percent of the air. In the Carboniferous, some models suggest up to about 35 percent. And the griffinfly Meganeura had a wingspan of around 70 centimeters. The numbers sit side by side, but scientists still debate how tightly oxygen and body size were linked.

Amphibians had a problem. Their eggs had no shell and dried out in air, so they had to return to water to breed. Then, sometime in the Carboniferous, a group of animals evolved the amniotic egg. Inside, the embryo floated in its own private pond of fluid, wrapped in protective membranes. With eggs like that, animals could breed far from water. Today, reptiles, birds, and mammals all descend from these egg-layers.

The Permian Period began about 299 million years ago. By then, most of the land had joined into one supercontinent, Pangaea, stretching from the far north to the far south, surrounded by a single great ocean called Panthalassa. Its interior was so far from the sea that much of it turned dry. Deserts, dunes, and seasonal scrublands spread where swamps had once been.

One of the most famous Permian animals is Dimetrodon, often mistaken for a dinosaur. In fact, it lived more than 40 million years before the first dinosaurs. It was a synapsid, a member of the group that later gave rise to mammals, which makes it more closely related to you than to any dinosaur. The largest species reached over four meters long, with teeth of different shapes and a tall sail on its back.

The top predators of the Late Permian were the gorgonopsians. The largest, Inostrancevia, reached about three and a half meters long, with saber-like canine teeth more than ten centimeters long. These hunters filled a role much like that of big cats today, more than 250 million years earlier.

In the shadows lived smaller animals with a big future: the cynodonts. Procynosuchus, from the Late Permian, was about sixty centimeters long. Cynodonts had teeth specialized for different jobs and powerful jaw muscles, and some later cynodonts dug burrows. Over tens of millions of years, one group of cynodonts would give rise to the first mammals. These small, overlooked animals carried our family line toward the coming storm.

Then, about 252 million years ago, came the worst crisis in the history of animal life. In what is now Siberia, enormous volcanic eruptions built a vast region of lava known as the Siberian Traps. Over hundreds of thousands of years, they poured out millions of cubic kilometers of lava. Worse, the magma pushed through underground layers of coal and other carbon-rich rock, releasing huge amounts of carbon dioxide and methane.

The planet heated up fast. Evidence from fossil teeth suggests tropical seas warmed by about 8 degrees Celsius, reaching temperatures near 40 degrees at the surface. Warm water holds less oxygen, and large parts of the ocean became starved of it. Some regions turned toxic with hydrogen sulfide. Many scientists think acid rain and damage to the ozone layer added to the stress on land.

This was the end-Permian mass extinction, often called the Great Dying. Estimates vary, but roughly 80 percent or more of marine species disappeared, along with about 70 percent of land vertebrate species. The trilobites, which had lasted for more than 250 million years, were gone forever. So were the sea scorpions and many old groups of corals. Even insects, which rarely suffer mass extinctions, were hit hard.

Here is how the Paleozoic disasters compare. At the end of the Ordovician, around 85 percent of marine species were lost. In the Late Devonian, roughly 70 percent of species. And at the end of the Permian, about 81 percent of marine species by one recent estimate, with other estimates going higher. Life came close to the edge, more than once.

So who survived? One famous survivor was Lystrosaurus, a stocky, pig-sized plant-eater with a beak and two tusks, and a distant relative of mammals. It is thought to have burrowed throughout its life. In the earliest Triassic rocks of South Africa's Karoo Basin, Lystrosaurus makes up more than 90 percent of the vertebrate fossils. Why did it do so well? Burrowing, a flexible diet, and fast growth may all have helped.

Look back at the whole journey. In the Paleozoic, life invented hard shells, complex eyes, jaws, bone, roots, wood, seeds, limbs, and the amniotic egg. Many of the tools your body uses were first tried out in these ancient seas, swamps, and deserts. The backbone traces back to small swimmers, the jaw to gill arches, and the bones of your arm to fleshy fins. After the Great Dying, the Mesozoic began, and with it, the age of dinosaurs.

So the next time you feel your heartbeat, take a deep breath, or open your mouth to speak, remember where those parts came from: a long line of survivors stretching back more than 500 million years.

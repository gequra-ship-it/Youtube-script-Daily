===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Picture a pond on a warm night. Frogs sit at the edge of the water, waiting. For a frog, the rules of dinner are simple. If something small moves, you shoot out your tongue, and the insect is gone. Frogs have hunted this way for millions of years, and insects have paid the price. Beetles, flies, crickets, and caterpillars all end up on the menu. But what happens when one insect refuses to play the victim? Today, we meet a beetle that turns that rule upside down. On the food web diagrams in school books, the arrow points one way, from the insect to the frog. Predator and prey, and no confusion about which is which.

In damp places across the Old World, from Africa to the Mediterranean and into Asia, there lives a beetle larva that grows to only about two centimeters long. It is slim and soft-looking, pale yellow or white, with black and orange markings. To a passing frog, it looks like the easiest midnight snack in the world. But this larva is not waiting to be eaten. It is waiting for something else. It is waiting for the frog. You could walk past one on a damp night and never notice it under the leaves.

Meet Epomis, a genus of ground beetles with about thirty species. Ground beetles are one of the largest beetle families on Earth, with about 40,000 known species, and most of them hunt small invertebrates like worms, snails, and caterpillars. Epomis larvae are different. They feed only on amphibians: frogs, toads, newts, and salamanders. And they feed on living animals much larger than themselves. Scientists call this a predator-prey role reversal. So how does a creature smaller than your thumb pull it off?

To answer that, we first have to step inside the mind of a frog. Frogs and toads are built to react to motion. In 1959, a famous study called What the Frog's Eye Tells the Frog's Brain found cells in the frog's eye that respond strongly to small, dark, moving objects. It came from Jerome Lettvin and his colleagues at MIT. Later research on toads showed that a part of the brain called the optic tectum starts the turn toward prey and the snap. To a toad's brain, something small that wiggles is food. When researchers removed the tectum in toads, the turn toward prey disappeared.

Researchers even found that toads judge prey by its shape and its direction. A thin object that moves along its own length, like a worm crawling forward, counts as food. A millipede walking along its length fits the same picture. The same shape moving sideways is usually ignored. Scientists nicknamed these the worm and the anti-worm. The rules are fast, and they work. A frog does not need to think about a fly. It only needs to see the right kind of movement. In lab experiments, simple moving dummies shaped like a worm were enough to make toads turn and snap. And that is exactly the weakness Epomis uses.

The Epomis larva sits still on the ground and waits. When a frog or toad comes near, it starts to move its antennae and its jaws. Researchers call this luring. To us, it looks like a strange little dance. To a hungry frog, it looks like a small creature wriggling on the ground. In other words, it looks like dinner. The larva is not hiding from its predator. It is calling it over.

Researchers measured this lure. They moved a young green toad in a small netted cage toward a larva, step by step. When the toad was fifteen centimeters away, the larva made about 2.8 lure movements a minute. When the toad was one centimeter away, it made about 4.9. With an empty cage, it made only about one a minute. The closer the predator came, the harder the bait worked.

From the frog's point of view, this is an easy meal. It turns toward the movement, creeps closer, and fixes both eyes on its target. That is the normal sequence for a hunting toad: turn, approach, fix, and snap. The larva does not run, and it does not hide in the leaf litter. It keeps waving. Every step brings the frog closer to a moment its ancestors have repeated countless times. Except this time, the script has been rewritten. After a successful snap, a toad swallows and often wipes its mouth with a front foot.

Then the frog strikes. Its sticky tongue flicks out in less than a tenth of a second. A 2017 study at the Georgia Institute of Technology found that a frog's tongue is about ten times softer than a human tongue. Its saliva turns runny on impact, soaks into the prey, and then thickens again as the tongue pulls back. Earlier research on horned frogs measured a tongue pull of up to 1.4 times the frog's body weight. Against a fly or a cricket, that is usually the end of the story. The tongue lands, the prey sticks, and it vanishes into the frog's mouth. Nature films have shown this moment countless times. But the Epomis larva is waiting for exactly this moment. What happens next is the reason scientists filmed hundreds of these encounters.

In almost every encounter, the larva dodges the tongue. Then, before the frog can react, it latches on to the nearest part of the amphibian's body, usually around the mouth, the throat, or the chest. How does it time this so well? The researchers wrote that the mechanism of this swift counterattack is still unknown. Nobody knows exactly how a soft-bodied larva times its escape so precisely. What we do know is the result. The predator has just become the prey.

Look closely at the larva's head, and you can see why it does not fall off. Its jaws are unusual. Each one ends in a double hook. These double-hooked jaws are one of the features that set Epomis larvae apart. Once they are set into the frog's skin, the larva hangs on, and the hunt is over. It is a little like a climber's grappling hook, only on a creature smaller than your thumb.

Now the larva starts to feed, and it does so in two different ways. A young larva feeds a lot like a parasite. It sucks the frog's body fluids. Many ground beetle larvae digest food partly outside their bodies before taking it in, which may help explain how a tiny larva can feed on something so big. As the larva grows into its later stages, it often switches from sucking to chewing the tissue. In other words, it starts as a parasite and ends as a true predator.

The frog is much larger than the larva, and yet it is the frog that disappears. In the lab, the feeding ended with the amphibian consumed, leaving behind only a pile of bones. Think about the scale. An animal that hunts insects every night is finished by an insect smaller than a coin. And for the larva, this is not one lucky meal. It is the plan for its whole childhood. The researchers saw the same ending again and again. Once a larva had latched on, the amphibian was eventually consumed.

Epomis larvae grow through three stages. At the end of each stage, the larva drops off its host and molts in a hidden place. Then it needs a new amphibian. The studies we found do not say how long each stage lasts. So a single larva may have to beat a frog or a toad three times before it can grow up. Each time, it uses the same trick: wait, wave, dodge, and hook on. Three hunts, three hosts, and very little room for a mistake.

Why would any insect depend on such a dangerous food? Amphibians commonly eat ground beetles. The researchers suggested that this behavior may have started as an extreme form of defense. A larva that could survive an attack had an advantage. Over time, surviving the frog may have turned into eating the frog. It is a hypothesis, not a proven history, but it would explain why the larva's whole life is built around the animal that should eat it. A similar interaction had also been reported for another species, Epomis nigricans, which hints that the habit may run through more of the genus.

For years, there were only scattered reports of Epomis larvae found clinging to amphibians in the wild. Then, in 2011, two Israeli researchers, Gil Wizen and Avital Gasith, put the story to the test in the lab. They used 420 larvae of two species, Epomis circumscriptus and Epomis dejeani, and five species of amphibians. Their findings on the larvae appeared in the journal PLoS ONE, and a second paper on the adult beetles appeared in ZooKeys.

Here is how a trial worked. A single larva waited in a small container, and then one young amphibian was let in. Each amphibian was used only once. Video recording began ten seconds before the amphibian arrived and ran in ten-minute clips, so the critical seconds were not missed. Most trials used three kinds of frogs and toads. Newts and salamanders were rare, so they were used only sparingly. In total, the researchers used 420 larvae.

The results were striking. The researchers ran 382 trials. Only seven times did the amphibian manage to gulp a larva, and each time it quickly spat it back out. In one more case, a young toad actually swallowed a larva. The larva stayed inside the toad's stomach for about two hours. Then the toad spat it out alive, and the larva went on to attack and eat the toad. Almost every time, the amphibian lost.

These numbers come from lab containers, where a frog and a larva cannot walk away from each other. In the wild, encounters may play out differently, and the studies do not give success rates from the field. The amphibians in the trials were young frogs and toads that had only recently left the water, not giant adults. Even so, they were much larger than the larva, and the method worked almost every time.

Once its larval stages are done, the larva goes on to become an adult beetle. The adult is a striking animal, about fifteen to twenty-six millimeters long, often metallic blue or green, with a yellow-orange rim along its wing covers and yellow legs and antennae. At first glance, it looks like an ordinary ground beetle. Inside the pupa, the soft body of the larva has been rebuilt into a hard, armored adult. So does the frog hunting end with childhood? It does not.

Adult Epomis beetles are generalist predators. Beetles in their wider group are known to eat other invertebrates and even the carcasses of vertebrates. But the Israeli researchers found something more. In the lab, adults also attacked live amphibians: green toads, Savigny's tree frogs, Levant water frogs, banded newts, and fire salamanders. Only one of the two species, Epomis dejeani, ate the newts. So the larva is a specialist, and the adult is a generalist that still hunts frogs.

Here is a strange detail. During the day, adults of both species share shelters with amphibians, hiding under the same cover. At night, the relationship changes. Out in the field, the researchers found beetles feeding on green toads. Attacks on the other amphibians were seen only in the lab. Imagine resting next to a neighbor all day, without knowing that it might be planning its dinner. In another case reported from the wild, three beetles were found eating toads from behind.

The adult does not use a lure. It uses an ambush. In the lab, the beetle jumped onto the amphibian's back and started biting the lower back area. The frog leaps and twists, trying to throw it off, but the beetle hangs on like a rodeo rider. A frog's bulging eyes give it a very wide view of the world, but its tongue can only strike forward. Against something riding on its back, its best weapon is useless.

Then comes the bite that matters. The beetle makes a horizontal cut across the lower back of the amphibian. Within about one to two minutes, the animal stops moving. A frog that cannot move cannot escape, and a jump is its main way out. After that, the beetle feeds, chewing on the back and the sides. Within a few hours, only the head and the limbs remain.

You might guess that the beetle simply cuts through the spinal cord. But according to one of the researchers, the cut does not harm the spine. His leading idea is that the bite damages the muscles that connect the hind legs, but he says that idea still needs to be confirmed. So even the adult's signature move holds a small mystery. It clearly works, but no one has yet proved exactly why.

Here is another puzzle. Green toads, one of the beetle's main victims, carry poison glands in their skin, including two large glands behind the eyes, called parotoid glands. These toxins protect toads from many predators. Yet Epomis larvae and adults attack green toads anyway. How they cope with the poison is not explained in the studies we found. Is the beetle resistant? Does it simply avoid the glands? No one knows for sure.

Lures are not unique to Epomis. An anglerfish dangles a glowing lure in the dark ocean, a fin spine that has turned into a fishing rod with a light at its tip. An alligator snapping turtle lies still with its mouth open and wiggles a pink, wormlike tongue to draw fish inside. But those predators are bigger than their prey. What makes Epomis so unusual is that it lures an animal much bigger than itself, an animal that is supposed to be its predator, and then survives the attack it invited.

Epomis is not the only small hunter that breaks the size rules. Praying mantises have been recorded catching small birds. A 2017 review collected 147 cases in 13 countries, on every continent except Antarctica. The oldest report dates back to 1864, and the victims included 24 species of birds. Most of the cases came from the United States, and many involved hummingbirds in gardens, where the birds came to feeders or flowers. A mantis ambushes, much like the adult Epomis does.

Then there are the giant water bugs. The largest species can grow more than ten centimeters long, longer than a baseball card. Their young hunt tadpoles and small fish, but adults take down prey from fish to ducklings and even venomous snakes. They pierce their prey with a dagger-like beak and inject enzymes that break down its tissue, then suck the liquid back up. On large prey, a meal can take hours. In 2019, the entomologist Shin-ya Ohba reviewed these records. Size, it turns out, is not the same as rank.

So what is the lesson of the frog? Its strength and its weakness are the same thing. Fast, simple rules let it catch prey in a fraction of a second, with no time to think. Toads can learn to change what they treat as prey, but in the moment of the strike, the rules decide. A lure that moves like a worm passes every test. The larva does not break the frog's rules. It follows them perfectly.

So where does all of this leave us? Some big questions are still open. No one knows exactly how the larva times its dodge. The adult's bite is thought to damage the leg muscles, but that idea has not been confirmed. The poison of green toads does not seem to stop the beetle, and the studies do not say why. And no one has measured how often the larva wins in the wild. A two-centimeter animal, and it still keeps its secrets.

These beetles are hard to find. They are small, the adults are active at night, and the larvae molt in hidden places between meals. In Israel, the two species live on the central coastal plain, yet the researchers never found them at the same site. Across the Old World, the genus is spread widely, with most species in Africa. Somewhere tonight, near a pond you will never visit, a larva may be waving its antennae in the dark.

Next time you hear frogs calling by a pond on a summer night, listen a little longer. It sounds peaceful. But down in the wet leaves, a two-centimeter hunter may be waiting for one of those singers to make the biggest mistake of its life.

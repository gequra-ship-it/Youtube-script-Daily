===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Picture a quiet pond on a warm night. A frog sits at the water's edge, watching for anything small that moves. For a very long time, the rule here has been simple: frogs eat insects. But in the Middle East, a beetle larva about two centimeters long has flipped that rule around. Today, we follow how it does it.

The animal is a ground beetle from the genus Epomis. These beetles live in the Old World, and most of the roughly thirty species are found in Africa. Their larvae feed only on amphibians. Frogs, toads, newts, and salamanders are the only prey they take.

That diet is not a quirk. Earlier research found that the larvae need amphibians to complete their life cycle. For a beetle, that is a dangerous requirement. Frogs and toads are large, fast, and always hungry.

Ground beetles are one of the largest groups of beetles, with tens of thousands of species. Most are predators, and they usually hunt small invertebrates, such as worms and insects. They are also food. Amphibians commonly eat ground beetles, both adults and larvae. So the usual arrow in this food web points from the beetle to the frog's mouth. Epomis turns that arrow around. But how does a creature smaller than your thumb do it?

In 2011, two Israeli researchers, Gil Wizen and Avital Gasith, set out to find the answer. They studied two species of Epomis, called circumscriptus and dejeani. They placed the larvae with frogs and toads, and they filmed every encounter. The footage showed a hunting method that nobody had described in this detail before.

Start with the hunter. The larva is long and slim, white or yellow, with black and orange markings. It can reach about twenty millimeters. At the back of its body there are two small extensions, and at the front there are two jaws, each ending in a double hook. On paper, it looks like a meal for any frog that walks by.

To see how it works, start with the frog. Its hunting rules are simple. Pay attention to things that move. Attack things that are small. Avoid things that are large. Those rules work well against flies and crickets. They fail only when something small has learned to use them.

The Epomis larva sits and waits. When a frog comes near, it waves its antennae and moves its jaws, one after the other. As the frog gets closer, the larva speeds up its movements. To the frog, this looks like something small and alive. It looks like dinner.

Imagine you are the frog. It is dark, you are hungry, and something small is moving in front of you. Nothing about it looks dangerous. You do what has always worked. You lean in, and you take aim.

Then the frog strikes. A frog's tongue takes about a tenth of a second to reach its target. Against almost any insect, that is the end of the story. But the larva almost always dodges the tongue. The frog's best weapon has just missed.

Once the tongue misses, the larva grabs the frog's face or throat with its double-hooked jaws, and it holds on. The frog is now carrying a passenger. Researchers say that exactly how the larva pulls off this counterattack is still not fully understood.

The larva begins by sucking fluids from the frog's body, much like a parasite. Then it chews the tissue. The frog is much larger than the larva, and yet it is the frog that gets eaten. In the end, only bones remain. Scientists call this predator-prey role reversal.

Lures are not unique to Epomis. An anglerfish dangles a glowing lure in the dark ocean. An alligator snapping turtle wiggles a pink, wormlike tongue to draw fish into its mouth. What makes this beetle stand out is the dodge. The larva baits an animal far larger than itself, then survives the strike aimed at it.

Here is how the face-offs ended. The researchers recorded almost 400 of them. Only seven times did the amphibian get a larva into its mouth. In one case, a toad swallowed a larva, and the larva stayed in its stomach for two hours. The toad then spat it out. The larva was apparently unharmed, and it went on to kill and eat the toad. Almost every time, the amphibian lost.

These results come from filmed laboratory encounters. The adult attacks on toads and frogs were filmed too. In an enclosure, the animals cannot walk away from each other, so success rates in the wild could differ. The studies we found do not report wild numbers. What the footage does show is the method, and the method works.

Each larva passes through three growth stages, and in each stage it kills one victim. After the third meal, the larva turns into a pupa. Later, an adult beetle emerges. And here is the twist: the adult is a hunter too.

The adult looks like a typical ground beetle. It is about fifteen to twenty-six millimeters long, often metallic blue or green, with a yellow-orange rim along its wing covers. Its legs and antennae are mostly yellow. Unlike the larva, it does not use a lure. It uses stealth.

During the day, adult Epomis beetles have been found sharing shelter with amphibians. At night, they hunt them. The beetle sneaks up from behind and grips the amphibian with its legs. The frog, which watches the world in front of it, never gets the chance to use its tongue.

Then the beetle bites. With its jaws, it makes an incision in the pelvic region, near the base of the hind legs. The bite appears to cut the muscles of the legs. That matters, because a frog's main escape is a jump.

Within a few minutes, the amphibian is paralyzed. A few hours later, little remains but a head and limbs. The spine is not damaged, so this is not a cut through the spinal cord. The leading explanation is damage to the leg muscles, though that still needs to be confirmed.

Adults are not strict specialists. Earlier research found that they eat land invertebrates and dead vertebrates. The Israeli study added that adults also prey on live amphibians. So the adult diet is more varied than the larva's. But amphibians are on the menu for both.

In the lab, the beetles took down several kinds of amphibian: green toads, Savigny's tree frogs, Levant water frogs, banded newts, and fire salamanders. The two species in Israel overlap only partly in what they eat. Only one of them, Epomis dejeani, was seen attacking newts.

How does a beetle end up hunting its own hunters? Scientists speculate that it began as a way to escape. Amphibians commonly eat ground beetles, so a larva that could survive an attack had an advantage. Over time, that escape trick may have become a way to feed, and it made Epomis dependent on amphibians.

The frog's strength is also its weakness. Simple rules let it catch flies fast, with no time to think. But simple rules can be tricked. A lure that moves like prey, and is small like prey, passes every test the frog applies. The larva does not break the frog's rules. It follows them.

This story challenges a habit we have. We tend to assume that the bigger animal, the one with the backbone, wins. But a food web is not a ladder. A small animal can survive a bigger predator, if it learns that predator's habits well enough. Here, a few millimeters of beetle turned the arrangement around.

Some questions are still open. Researchers say that how the larva counterattacks is unknown. For the adult's bite, damage to the leg muscles is the leading idea, but it is not confirmed. Toads also carry toxins in their skin, and the studies we found do not explain how the beetle copes with them. Even so, the main finding is solid: in almost every recorded face-off, the beetle won.

These beetles are hard to find. They are small, and the adults hunt at night. In Israel, two species live on the central coastal plain. Their ranges are similar, but the researchers did not find them at the same sites. Across the Old World, the genus is spread widely, with the most species in Africa.

Let's put it together. A larva waves its antennae to look like prey. The frog attacks, and the larva dodges and hooks on. It feeds on the frog and kills it. Later, as an adult, it sneaks up from behind and bites the hips. Two different tricks, one target.

Next time you stand by a pond at night, listen to the frogs. It sounds peaceful. But somewhere in the Middle East, on the ground near the water, a small larva may be waving its antennae, waiting for a frog to come close. Out here, the biggest animal is not always the one that wins.

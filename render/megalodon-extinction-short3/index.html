Could a shark much
smaller than Megalodon
have helped cause its
extinction?
It sounds impossible,
but the answer may be yes.
You might picture a
dramatic battle between
a colossal giant and
a smaller challenger.
Nature is rarely that simple.
During the Pliocene epoch,
the ancestors of modern
great white sharks were
spreading across the oceans.
They were much smaller
than Megalodon, probably
about the size of
great whites today,
around five or six meters.
In a direct fight
with a fully grown
Megalodon, they would have
had no chance.
But they did not
need to win a fight.
In 2022, researchers
measured zinc isotopes
in fossil shark teeth
to learn what different
sharks ate.
Early great whites and
Megalodon turned out to
occupy a similar level
in the food chain.
That suggests they may
have competed for some
of the same prey,
such as small whales,
seals, and large fish.
Here is where size
becomes a problem.
A giant, warm-bodied shark
needs enormous amounts
of food just to keep going.
A smaller shark needs
only a fraction of
those calories.
Great whites were agile,
fast, and able to
live in both warm
and cool water.
When food became scarce,
a smaller hunter could
survive on leaner meals
that could never sustain
a Megalodon.
Young Megalodon may have
felt this first.
While adults were hard
to challenge, the young
may have been competing
with great whites for
the same prey,
and in a hungry
ocean, the more efficient
competitor has the advantage.
Scientists are careful here.
The study shows overlap,
not proof, and it
is only one piece
of the puzzle.
Climate and prey also
mattered.
So did the arrival
of other fast,
clever hunters, such as
toothed whales, which today
include killer whales that
hunt in coordinated pods.
The ocean was getting
more crowded, and every
competitor wanted the same
shrinking supply of food.
In the game of
survival, raw strength
can lose to efficiency.

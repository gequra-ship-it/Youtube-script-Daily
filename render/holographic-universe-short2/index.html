60 BILLIONTHS OF A DEGREE
What happens to information
when it falls into
a black hole?
For decades, that single
question troubled modern
physics.
Imagine dropping an
encyclopedia across the
event horizon, the point
of no return.
Is the knowledge inside
gone forever?
Quantum theory has a
strict rule: information
is never truly destroyed.
In principle, the complete
state of a system
today determines its past
and its future.
So a black hole
should not be able
to erase a book.
Then, in nineteen
seventy-four, Stephen
Hawking showed that black
holes are not perfectly black.
They emit a faint
thermal glow, now called
Hawking radiation.
For a black hole
with the mass of
our Sun, it is
only about sixty billionths
of a degree above
absolute zero.
Nobody has detected it
from a real black hole.
The glow carries energy
away, so in theory
the black hole shrinks
and evaporates.
But the radiation looks
purely thermal, a random
glow that does not
carry the contents of
the book.
Where did the information go?
In nineteen seventy-six,
Hawking argued that it
was lost for good.
Physicists split into camps.
In the nineteen nineties,
Hawking and Kip Thorne
bet John Preskill that
black holes destroy
information.
In July two thousand
four, at a conference
in Dublin, Hawking conceded,
and gave Preskill a
baseball encyclopedia as
the prize.
Thorne did not concede
at the time.
Many physicists had already
been persuaded by a
different idea.
In nineteen ninety-seven,
Juan Maldacena proposed
that gravity in a
certain kind of space
is equivalent to a
quantum theory on its
boundary.
Because the boundary theory
obeys the usual quantum
rules, information cannot
be lost there.
If the two descriptions
truly match, it cannot
be lost in the
black hole either.
In two thousand nineteen,
two independent groups
used holographic ideas
to calculate how the
radiation should behave
if information is preserved,
a shape called the Page curve.
These are calculations
in simplified models,
not a complete solution
for real black holes,
but they point to
the same answer:
the information gets out.
Maldacena's proposal is
still a conjecture,
and the space in
his example is not
our universe.
But the paradox pushed
physicists toward a
startling suspicion: space
itself might emerge from
quantum information.

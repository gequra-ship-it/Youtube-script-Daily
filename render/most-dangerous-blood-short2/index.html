How can a type
O patient reject type O blood?
It sounds impossible,
and it was a
real medical puzzle.
Consider a rare condition
called the Bombay phenotype.
It was first described
in 1952 by doctors
working in Bombay,
a city now called
Mumbai, in India.
Imagine a patient with
this condition arriving
at a hospital and
needing a transfusion.
Standard tests show no
A flags and no
B flags, so the
medical team naturally
calls the patient type O.
Type O is often
thought of as the
safest blood there is.
Yet when the doctors
test the patient's blood
against ordinary type O,
the samples clump together
in the test tube.
The mystery pushed
scientists to look at
how the flags are built.
The A and B
antigens do not attach
straight to the cell.
They are built on
a base molecule called
the H antigen,
like a flagpole planted
in the cell surface.
Enzymes add one sugar
to that pole to
make an A flag,
or a different sugar
to make a B flag.
In ordinary type O,
the pole simply stays bare.
But in the Bombay
phenotype, a rare change
in a gene called
FUT1 means the working
pole is never built at all.
With no H antigen
of their own,
people with the Bombay
phenotype make antibodies
against it.
Ordinary type O cells
are covered in H,
so those antibodies attack
them fiercely.
For most people with
the condition, it causes
no illness at all.
It matters when blood
is needed, because the
only safe red cells
come from another Bombay
donor.
Estimates say roughly one
in ten thousand people
in parts of India
have it, against about
one in a million
in much of Europe.
Even so, finding a
match can feel like
searching for a single
grain of sand on
a long beach.
And if the condition
goes unnoticed, ordinary
type O blood could
be given by mistake.
The lesson is that
type O is not
one single thing.
A cell can lack
A and B flags
and still carry the
H pole, or lack even that.
Only a deeper test,
one that looks for
H, can tell the two apart.
Even the safest blood
type has a hidden rule.

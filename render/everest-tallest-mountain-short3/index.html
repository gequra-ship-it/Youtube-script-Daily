What if the most
meaningful measurement
is not how far
a peak rises above
the water, or how
tall it stands from
its base, but how
far its summit reaches
out into space?
Which point on the
surface of our planet
is farthest from its center?
If you assume the
answer is Everest,
prepare for a surprise.
For this ruler,
we must travel to
the volcanic spine of
Ecuador, in South America.
Here stands Mount
Chimborazo, an ancient,
ice-capped stratovolcano
in the Western Cordillera
of the Andes.
By its official altitude,
Chimborazo seems entirely
out of the running.
Its summit rises to
6,263 meters above the
ocean, far down the
list of global summits.
It is not even
the highest peak in
the Andes; that title
belongs to Aconcagua in
Argentina.
Yet its summit is
the farthest point on
the Earth's surface from
the center of our planet.
How can a mountain
more than two thousand
meters shorter than Everest
reach farther into space?
The secret lies in
the imperfect shape of
the Earth.
In textbooks, our planet
is a smooth,
perfect blue marble.
In reality, it is
an oblate spheroid,
slightly flattened at the
poles and bulging at
the middle.
Our planet spins at
roughly 1,670 kilometers
per hour at the
equator, and that rotation
pushes the equatorial
regions outward.
The radius at the
equator is about twenty-one
kilometers longer than
the radius at the poles.
Chimborazo sits about one
and a half degrees
south of the equator,
right on top of
this planetary bulge.
Everest sits much farther
north, at roughly
twenty-eight degrees
latitude, well away from
the maximum bulge.
Measured from the center
of the Earth,
the summit of Chimborazo
is approximately 6,384
kilometers away.
Everest comes up short
by more than two
thousand meters.
Everest is higher above
sea level, but sea
level itself sits farther
from the center of
the Earth at the
equator than at the poles.
A mountain's distance from
the center depends on
where it stands,
and Chimborazo stands on
the bulge.

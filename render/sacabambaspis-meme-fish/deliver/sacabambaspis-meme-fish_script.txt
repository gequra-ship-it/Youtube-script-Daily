===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

Look closely at this face. Two round eyes sit right at the front of its head, and below them a small slit of a mouth hangs open in what looks like permanent surprise. It looks like a clay project that went slightly wrong. But this is a model of a real animal, a fish called Sacabambaspis, about a foot long, that lived roughly four hundred and sixty million years ago. Behind that goofy expression hides a genuinely important chapter in the story of animals with backbones.

The model sits in the Finnish Museum of Natural History in Helsinki. A photo of it went viral on Twitter in 2022, and in 2023 people in Japan fell in love with it. Fan art, comics, stickers, plush toys, and key chains followed. People simply could not believe that something so bewildered had survived in ancient seas. So here is the real question. Was the model a joke, or did this little fish actually look like that? To answer it, we have to travel a very long way back.

The model was made by the Estonian palaeontologist Elga Mark-Kurik for a fossil fish exhibit that opened in 1995. She was a scientist, not a professional sculptor. According to BBC Science Focus, she shaped it from foam, cast it in silicone, and gave it a silvery glaze. The eyes, reportedly, came from a craft store, and they were googly doll's eyes. For more than twenty years the model stayed fairly quiet in its display. Then the internet found it.

Sacabambaspis lived in the Ordovician Period, which lasted from about four hundred and eighty-five to four hundred and forty-three million years ago. It came after the Cambrian explosion, when complex animals had already spread through the oceans. Most of the land we know today was arranged very differently, and a great deal of it was gathered around the southern supercontinent Gondwana. On land there were no trees, no insects, and no animals with four legs. All the drama of life played out in warm, shallow seas.

Imagine swimming in those seas. Trilobites crawled over the bottom, protected by hard shells. Straight-shelled nautiloids such as Cameroceras drifted above, with long cone-shaped shells, and some estimates say the biggest reached about six meters. Early sea scorpions were beginning to appear too. In this crowd, animals with backbones were not the rulers of the ocean. Our distant relatives were small, and they were mostly doing what small animals do best, which is staying alive.

The scientific story of Sacabambaspis begins high in the Andes of Bolivia. In 1986, the palaeontologists Gagnier, Blieck, and Rodrigo described the animal from fragments of armor. They named it after the village of Sacabamba, in the Cochabamba region, where the first fossils were found. The last part of the name comes from the Greek word aspis, which means shield. Later, more complete specimens came out of the same Bolivian rocks.

Those rocks belong to a layer called the Anzaldo Formation. The best estimate puts the age of the fossils at roughly four hundred and sixty million years, somewhere between about four hundred and sixty-eight and four hundred and fifty-three million. At that time, what is now Bolivia sat near the shallow edge of Gondwana. Pieces of armor from the same kind of fish have also turned up in Argentina, Australia, and Oman. A fish that is now buried under mountains once swam in a warm shallow sea.

Sacabambaspis belongs to a group scientists call ostracoderms, a name that means shell-skinned. More precisely, it belongs to the Arandaspida, one of the early groups of armored jawless fish. These animals did not have a bony skeleton inside the body like a trout. Instead, plates of bone grew in the skin and covered the head and body like armor. Sacabambaspis was a small animal in a hard coat, and that coat is the reason we know it at all.

Here are the numbers. Sacabambaspis was about a foot long, and up to roughly thirty-five centimeters if you count its thin tail. The mouth was lined with nearly sixty rows of small plates. And the oldest fossils are about four hundred and sixty million years old. Not bad for a fish with such a funny face.

The front part of the fish was covered by a hard head shield, about ten centimeters long. Behind it, the body was covered with small scales, and it ended in a thin, whip-like tail. Imagine a tiny armored submarine with a flexible rear end. The shield protected the head, the gills, and the main organs, and the scales let the back half bend. It was not built for speed. It was built to be hard to eat.

Now to the famous face. In the fossils, the eyes sit at the very front of the head, close together, each one supported by a ring of bone. Most fish today have eyes on the sides of the head, which gives a wide view of danger. A forward-facing pair of eyes is not what you would expect. Why Sacabambaspis had this arrangement is still not fully explained. But the placement itself comes from the fossils, not from the sculptor.

There is one more catch. Soft tissues like retinas and lenses almost never survive in fossils. So nobody knows what the eyes of a living Sacabambaspis looked like. The model's round, black-and-white stare, the one that made the internet laugh, is the sculptor's choice. The fossils tell us where the eyes sat. They do not tell us how wide or how startled the fish looked. The goofy look is partly science and partly art.

Sacabambaspis had no jaw. It belonged to the agnathans, the jawless fish. Its mouth was a narrow slit, with a lower lip made of thin strips of tiny scales. It could not bite into anything, and it could not chew. That sounds like a serious handicap, but for tens of millions of years jawless fish did very well. Their mouths simply worked in a different way.

The mouth area was packed with small bony plates, nearly sixty rows of them. Scientists think these plates could move, and that they helped the fish suck food in. If that is right, Sacabambaspis probably fed by suction, taking in small things from the water and the sediment, such as plankton or bits of organic matter. The exact menu is still a guess. But the plates tell us something clear. This was not a fish for catching prey. It was a fish for gathering crumbs.

Here is what Sacabambaspis did without. It had no jaws. It had no paired fins, no fins on its sides at all. It had a tail fin and nothing else. So there were no fins to help it steer, balance, or hover, the way a modern fish can.

How did it get around? Without side fins, the tail did the work. It probably swam by wriggling its back half from side to side, and some researchers have compared that to a tadpole. Because the head was a rigid shield, the front of the body could not bend. That would have made sharp turns and fine control difficult. So when you picture this fish, do not picture a graceful swimmer. Picture a small, stiff armored fish, working hard and steering by its tail.

Bone is dense, and Sacabambaspis carried a lot of it. Scientists suspect that it spent much of its time near the sea floor, where a heavy body is less of a problem. The shape of the tail fits with that idea too. Fish with swim bladders can adjust how much they float, but there is no sign that this animal had that kind of organ. These are reasonable inferences, not proven facts. They do paint a picture of a creature living low and slow.

The head shield holds a quiet surprise. Its surface has tiny pores that lead into canals, and these canals were part of a lateral line system. That is the sense that lets modern fish feel movement in the water around them. For Sacabambaspis it would have been a way to detect a disturbance, such as an approaching predator, even in murky water. Two small pits near the front of the shield may have held light-sensitive organs. The armor was also a sensor.

The armor was made of a material called aspidine, a type of bone that has no living cells inside it. It was decorated with tiny bumps shaped like oak leaves. For a long time people assumed such bumps were made of dentine, the material in your teeth. But in this group they seem to contain none. So even something as plain as a fish's coat of armor still has open questions.

Why would early vertebrates grow armor at all? One idea is protection. The seas held large nautiloids and other predators, and a hard coat makes a small animal harder to swallow or crush. That is a hypothesis, and scientists still debate it. Armor may also have helped store minerals, such as calcium and phosphate. Whatever the first reason was, the result changed the story of vertebrates. Hard tissue in the skin became part of the toolkit.

To see where Sacabambaspis came from, go back another fifty million years. In the Cambrian rocks of Chengjiang, in China, palaeontologists found tiny fish-like animals named Haikouichthys and Myllokunmingia. They were only a few centimeters long. They had a stiff rod along the back, a pair of eyes, and zigzag blocks of muscle. They had no armor, no bones, and no hard scales. They were soft, small, and among the earliest animals that we place on the backboned branch.

Here is the timeline. Haikouichthys swam about five hundred and eighteen million years ago, soft and unarmored. Sacabambaspis came about four hundred and sixty million years ago, armored, with no jaw. And the Late Ordovician mass extinction struck about four hundred and forty-four million years ago, wiping out roughly eighty-five percent of marine species.

Think of your skeleton. Your ribs, your skull, your spine all sit deep under muscle and skin. But many researchers think the first bone in vertebrates did not appear inside the body at all. It appeared in the skin, as armor, in animals like the arandaspids. The skeleton inside the body came later and became the structure that holds us up today. In that sense, the story of your bones may start with a fish wearing a shield.

Sacabambaspis was not alone. In central Australia, in a layer called the Stairway Sandstone near Alice Springs, scientists found Arandaspis, a close relative. It had the same kind of head shield and the same missing jaw. Fossils from Bolivia, Australia, Argentina, and Oman suggest that these fish lived along the shallow margins of the same ancient supercontinent. Their remains are scattered across the modern world, because the continents have moved a very long way since.

In North America, in a rock layer called the Harding Sandstone in Colorado, scientists found other armored fish of the same age, including Astraspis and Eriptychius. Astraspis has a name that means star shield, because its armor was covered in star-shaped bumps. Different animals, different oceans, and a similar idea. Armored jawless fish were not a lucky accident in one place. They were a successful way of life in the Ordovician.

One Bolivian site holds a remarkable crowd. More than thirty specimens were found packed into a very small area, together with the shells of lingulid brachiopods, small animals that also lived in the shallows. This looks like a mass death, probably caused by a powerful storm. The idea is that fresh water and sediment suddenly poured into the shallow sea, and the animals could not cope. It is the best explanation we have, and it gives us a rare snapshot of a single bad day.

Picture that day, as the researchers do. A storm probably built over the coast, and rain poured into the rivers. A flood of muddy fresh water rushed out into the bay, and the fish, adapted to the sea, could not cope. Then sediment settled over them and buried them quickly, which is exactly what a fossil needs. For a creature that looks so comical, this is a surprisingly serious end. It is also why we know its shape so well.

Fossils are not photographs. Rock presses on them for hundreds of millions of years, and shells and plates get flattened or broken. A palaeontologist has to work out the original shape from the pieces, and they do it by comparing many specimens. Then an artist has to make choices about the parts that did not survive. That is how a reconstruction is born. It is the best guess from the evidence, and a different artist could make a different guess.

So how good is the Helsinki model? The overall plan is right. It has a head shield, a mouth slit, closely spaced eyes at the front, and no side fins. The googly eyes and the surprised look are artistic choices. The skin, the colors, and the expression are not preserved, so nobody can check them. I could not find a published study that grades the model against the fossils, so the most honest answer is this. It is a decent, hand-made guess, and its charm is partly an accident.

After the Ordovician, armored jawless fish spread into many new forms through the Silurian and Devonian periods. A group called the osteostracans developed large sensory fields on their heads and, importantly, paired fins at the front of the body. Paired fins gave them much better control in the water. So the clumsy design of Sacabambaspis was not the end of the line. Over the following tens of millions of years, evolution kept experimenting with what a fish could be.

Then came one of the biggest changes in vertebrate history. Most researchers think the jaw grew out of the first of the supporting arches that held up the gills. Over time, those arches turned into hinged jaws that could grasp, bite, and crush. The animals that had them are called gnathostomes, the jawed vertebrates. Jaws opened up new ways of life, from hunting active prey to cracking shells. Today almost every backboned animal you can name has them.

Some of the early jawed fish were armored too. They are called placoderms, and in the Devonian Period they became some of the top predators in the sea. One of the best known is Dunkleosteus, a giant several meters long with a head shield and bony blades in place of teeth. Those blades were self-sharpening and could cut through tough prey. Compared with that, an animal like Sacabambaspis would have looked small and slow, but its group had been around for a very long time.

It is tempting to say that jawed fish simply replaced jawless ones. The record is more complicated. Armored jawless fish lived alongside jawed fish for more than a hundred million years, from the Ordovician to the late Devonian. Sacabambaspis itself belongs to the arandaspids, a group known only from Ordovician rocks, so it never met Dunkleosteus. What we can say is that the jawless fish eventually faded, and that jawed vertebrates came to dominate the sea.

The Ordovician itself ended in a crisis. About four hundred and forty-four million years ago, ice sheets grew over Gondwana, and sea levels dropped. Shallow seas, the home of many animals, shrank or vanished. Then conditions changed again, and parts of the ocean lost oxygen. Together these changes caused one of the biggest mass extinctions in Earth's history, which wiped out roughly eighty-five percent of marine species. Shallow, warm waters were no safe place to be.

Not every jawless fish disappeared. Lampreys and hagfish, known together as cyclostomes, are alive today, and they have no jaws. They also have no bony armor. Whether their ancestors lost it or never had it is still debated. A lamprey has a round mouth lined with teeth that works like a suction cup, and it attaches to its prey. They offer a rare look at how a jawless vertebrate lives. They are cousins of the same ancient lineage, not copies of Sacabambaspis.

It is important to be careful here. Sacabambaspis is not our grandparent. It belongs to a side branch of the family tree, a cousin that left no descendants. What we share with it is a deep ancestry among early vertebrates. The mineral that makes your bones and tooth enamel, calcium phosphate, was already being used in armor like this. So the fish is not our ancestor, but it shows us what some of our distant relatives were doing.

Palaeontologists can now look inside fossils without breaking them. Using CT scans and powerful X-ray beams, they can see through solid rock and rebuild tiny structures in three dimensions. These methods are used on early fish fossils to examine bones, canals, and cavities. They do not make a fossil complete. But they can turn a lump of stone into a detailed digital model, and they keep changing what we know about the earliest backboned animals.

Scans of some related armored jawless fish show something remarkable. The hollow space inside the head shield has the shape of a brain with the same basic parts as ours: a forebrain, a midbrain, and a hindbrain. So the plan of the vertebrate brain is very old. I could not find a published scan that does this for Sacabambaspis itself, so we cannot say the same for it directly. But its relatives suggest that behind the funny face sat a brain with a familiar layout.

There is a lot we still do not know. Nobody knows how long Sacabambaspis lived, how it reproduced, or what color it was. We cannot say what its soft tissues looked like, or exactly what it ate. Even the shape of its tail has been debated. New fossils could answer some of this, and some may stay a mystery forever. That is not a failure of science. It is the normal edge of what a fossil record can tell us.

So why did a fish model become an internet star? Part of the answer is the face. Two wide-set eyes and a small mouth are enough to make us see an expression, even on a lump of silicone. And part of it is the story behind it, a small, gentle-looking animal from a hard world. Nobody can say exactly why one picture catches on and another does not. This one simply did.

Strangely enough, the meme did good work for science. People who had never heard of the Ordovician started searching for the animal behind the joke. In Japan, the fish got the kind of affection usually given to mascot characters, with merchandise, fan art, and school festival displays. A hand-made model from 1995 and a pair of craft-store eyes ended up introducing millions of people to early vertebrates. That is not a bad result for a clay-looking fish.

Sacabambaspis also helps correct a common picture. Evolution is not a ladder with fish at the bottom and humans at the top. It is more like a bush, with many branches, side paths, and endings. Some branches led to the animals that live today. Others, like the arandaspids, thrived for a while and disappeared. Being on a branch that ends does not make a species a failure. For millions of years, this fish did exactly what it needed to.

Look once more at those wide-set eyes. Whatever the real ones looked like, they once watched a sea with no trees on the coast and no birds in the sky. They saw giant nautiloids drift by and storms roll in from the open ocean. A small armored fish lived in that world, hid in it, and fed in it. Four hundred and sixty million years later, its picture is on a phone screen in your hand.

So the next time that face appears in your feed, smile, but also look a little closer. This is a real animal, with a real armor of bone, a sense of touch for water, and a place on the family tree of backboned life. It was not a joke of nature. It was a successful small fish in a very different world, and one of the best reminders that early vertebrates were already strange, resourceful, and surprisingly well equipped.

[S33] 15:23.7–15:53.4 (29.7s) 일러스트 · Not Replaced Overnight
It is tempting to say that jawed fish simply replaced jawless ones. The record is more complicated. Not every jawless fish disappeared. Lampreys and hagfish, known together as cyclostomes, are alive today, and they have no jaws. They also have no bony armor.

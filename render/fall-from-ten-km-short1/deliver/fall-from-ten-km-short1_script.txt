===== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 (장면당 한 문단) =====

What happens to your body if you suddenly fall out of an airplane at ten thousand meters? The first enemy is not the ground rushing toward you. It is the cold, thin air itself. And yet history records a handful of people who fell from this height and lived.

At ten kilometers up, the air pressure is only about a quarter of what it is at sea level. With so little oxygen reaching your blood, an ordinary person has roughly thirty seconds, sometimes even less, of useful consciousness. After that, the starving brain can no longer cope, and you pass out.

Then comes the cold. The air temperature at this height is typically around minus fifty degrees Celsius, and the wind rushing past your skin strips away heat. Without protective gear, frostbite can begin within minutes. The sky at this altitude looks a lot more like another planet than anywhere we live.

Meanwhile, you are falling. At first you speed up at nine point eight meters per second squared. But the air pushes back. The faster you fall, the more air you slam into, and the stronger the drag becomes. Soon the upward drag matches the downward pull of gravity, and you stop speeding up.

That steady speed is called terminal velocity. In the thin air up high, it can briefly top three hundred kilometers per hour. Lower down, where the air is thicker, it settles at roughly two hundred kilometers per hour. That is about fifty-five meters every single second.

Surviving the fall is not the real question. The real question is the stop. Concrete, asphalt, and solid rock stop a body over a few millimeters, and bones and organs cannot take that force. Survival depends on how far, and how long, it takes your speed to drop to zero.

The accounts of survivors point to surfaces that give way. A dense forest canopy can act as a multi-stage brake, as twigs snap and thicker branches bend and break. Deep snow on a steep slope compresses gradually, and the slope turns a head-on hit into a long slide. Each stretches the stop.

What about water? It feels like the soft choice, but at two hundred kilometers per hour, water is almost incompressible and cannot get out of the way fast enough. It can behave much like a hard surface, and hitting it flat-on is very often fatal.

What could destroy a
brain without being a
virus, a bacterium,
or a parasite?
For decades, the answer
was a mystery that
shook medical science.
Let us start in a laboratory.
Pathologists once studied
the brains of people
who died of kuru.
Under the microscope,
the tissue was full
of tiny holes.
Nerve cells had died,
and what remained looked
like a sponge.
Doctors now call this
pattern spongiform change.
In 1959, the researcher
William Hadlow noticed
that it looked a
lot like scrapie,
a disease of sheep.
Soon, experiments showed
that the disease could
be passed from one
animal to another.
So it was infectious.
But something was off.
The agent survived
treatments that destroy
ordinary germs.
Normally, an infection
needs genes to multiply.
A virus has DNA or RNA.
A bacterium has DNA.
That was a basic
rule of biology.
Yet here was an
agent that seemed to
break the rule.
In the early 1980s,
the neurologist Stanley
Prusiner suggested a radical
idea.
Maybe the agent was
made of protein alone,
with no genes of its own.
He called it a prion.
Many scientists doubted him.
But the evidence grew,
and in 1997 he
received the Nobel Prize.
So how can a protein spread?
Your brain naturally makes
a protein called PrP.
In its normal form,
it folds into a healthy shape.
A prion is the
same protein, folded the
wrong way.
Think of a key
that has been bent.
Here is the frightening part.
A misfolded protein can
push a normal one
into the same bent shape.
Then that one bends the next.
And the next.
The chain reaction moves
slowly through the brain,
nerve cell by nerve cell.
As misfolded proteins pile
up, brain cells die.
The tissue breaks down,
and holes appear.
Prions also resist the
enzymes that usually clear
away damaged proteins.
That is part of
why the damage cannot
be stopped once it begins.
No DNA.
No RNA.
Just a misfolded protein,
and a brain that
slowly turns to sponge.
It is one of
the strangest ideas in
modern biology.
And it came from
a tragedy in the
mountains of Papua New Guinea.

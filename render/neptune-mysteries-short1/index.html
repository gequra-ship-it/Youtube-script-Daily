The very story of
Neptune begins with a ghost.
In the early nineteenth
century, observers noticed
that Uranus was not
following its mathematically
predicted path around the Sun.
It seemed to accelerate
at certain points and
drag behind at others,
as if an invisible
hand were tugging at
its massive frame.
Something massive had to
be out there,
pulling on Uranus from
the dark.
Two brilliant mathematicians
began calculating the exact
location of this phantom
body using only pure
mathematics and Newton’s
laws of gravity.
Working independently,
they predicted where an
unknown planet must reside
to cause such strange
orbital wobbles.
In 1846, observers pointed
a telescope toward the
exact patch of sky
predicted by mathematical
calculations.
Within just one degree
of the predicted coordinate,
a tiny blue disc
emerged from the blackness.
Neptune became the first
planet found with the
tip of a pencil
before it was found
with the eye.
When you first lay
eyes on Neptune,
the sheer vibrancy of
its color captures your
breath.
It is a deep,
rich blue, far more
saturated and striking
than the pale cyan
hue of its twin, Uranus.
Methane is known to
absorb red wavelengths
of light while reflecting
blue light back out
into space.
But methane alone cannot
explain why Neptune shines
with such an intense,
deep lapis lazuli tone.
Scientists suspect there
may be an additional,
unidentified ingredient
floating within Neptune’s
upper atmosphere.
One prevailing hypothesis
suggests that a
semi-transparent haze layer
of photochemical smog hangs
suspended above the planet.
Perhaps Neptune’s active
interior continually churns
these particles, thinning
the haze and allowing
the deeper blue to
shine through.
Uranus, by contrast,
might have a sluggish
atmosphere that allows
a milky, stagnant haze
to collect and mute
its colors.
The true recipe of
Neptune’s signature paint
remains an open mystery.
Not even Neptune's color
is fully explained.

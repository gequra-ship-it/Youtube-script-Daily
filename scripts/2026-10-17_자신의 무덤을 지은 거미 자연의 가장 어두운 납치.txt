■ 전체 흐름 (대본 순서대로 8~15줄):
- 거미가 자기 무덤을 짓는다면이라는 가정과 질문으로 시작
- 거미줄 위의 사냥꾼과 다가오는 기생벌
- 순식간의 마비 공격과 알 부착
- 거미가 일상을 이어가는 며칠과 유충의 성장
- 유충이 숙주를 죽이지 않고 체액만 먹는 균형
- 거미의 행동을 바꾸는 화학 신호에 대한 과학자들의 가설
- 마지막 밤, 평소와 다른 고치용 거미줄 건축
- 유충이 거미를 먹고 고치를 짓고 성충이 됨
- 자연의 잔혹함을 보는 시각
- 선충이 숙주를 물가로 이끄는 사례, 곰팡이가 거미를 높은 곳으로 올려 보내는 사례
- 서로 다른 계통이 독립적으로 신경계를 조종하는 수렴
- 정원을 다시 보게 하는 마무리
■ 핵심 사실·숫자:
- 기생벌 유충은 숙주를 바로 죽이지 않고 체액을 먹으며 자람

[영상 제목]: The Spider That Built Its Own Tomb

=== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 ===

What if a spider built its own tomb, and the architect was not the spider? Picture an ordinary garden spider hanging in a perfect, glistening web. It is a patient hunter with lightning reflexes, the master of its little world. Then a tiny wasp drifts in, and soon the spider begins to behave in ways it never would on its own. It spins a strange structure that is not a trap for prey, and it does not know that the structure is meant for something else. How can a creature be turned into a builder against its own will? The answer is one of the darkest tricks in nature.

Yet, lurking just beyond the edge of the leaves is a visitor that does not care about the spider's venom or speed. A solitary parasitoid wasp hovers silently on delicate, transparent wings. Unlike social wasps that build paper nests and defend a queen, this hunter travels alone with a chilling, singular mission. It does not want to feed on the spider, nor does it want to steal the silk. It is searching for an incubator, a living nursery for a child it will never raise. As the wasp circles, it studies the spider from above, waiting for the briefest lapse in attention.

Could a predator so efficient and alert really fall victim to a creature smaller than itself? The strike happens with breathless speed. In a fraction of a second, the wasp dives straight into the web, completely bypassing the sticky snare lines with agile footwork. Before the spider can turn to defend itself, the wasp lunges and delivers a swift, surgical sting to the spider's underside. This is not a fatal dose of venom, but a temporary neurotoxin designed to do one thing. It instantly shuts down the spider's motor functions, leaving it awake but utterly paralyzed.

While the spider hangs motionless, suspended in a waking nightmare, the wasp gets to work without wasting a single second. Carefully inspecting the host's body, the wasp selects a very specific location, usually near the base of the spider's abdomen. Here, the spider's legs cannot easily reach to groom or scrape away foreign objects. With absolute mechanical precision, the wasp glues a single, glistening egg directly onto the spider's outer cuticle. The task is finished in moments. Without looking back, the mother wasp flies away into the forest canopy, leaving behind an invisible sentence of doom.

A few minutes later, the temporary paralysis begins to wear off. The spider stretches its legs, tests the tension of its silk lines, and resumes its normal routine. To an outside observer, nothing seems out of the ordinary. The spider still repairs broken threads, still captures stray flies, and still retreats to its hiding place when the midday sun grows too hot. It has no conscious awareness that a tiny biological time bomb is now glued to its back. Life continues as though the encounter never happened, but beneath that calm routine, the trap is already set.

Several days pass, and the microscopic egg cracks open. Out crawls a soft, pale wasp larva, blind and legless, perfectly shaped for a parasitic existence. It does not burrow deep into the spider's vital organs, because killing the host now would mean its own death. Instead, the tiny larva uses its sharp mouthparts to pierce a tiny hole through the spider's tough outer skin. It anchors itself firmly in place, tapping directly into the spider's bloodstream, known as hemolymph. From this moment onward, two separate lives become locked in an intimate, parasitic union.

Hemolymph is the life fluid that carries nutrients and hormones throughout the spider's body. The growing larva feeds on this fluid day after day, taking just enough to sustain its rapid growth while keeping the spider alive and functioning. It is an exquisite and terrifying biological tightrope. If the larva feeds too aggressively, the host will die of exhaustion, and the larva will perish before it can mature. Therefore, the parasite acts like a careful tenant, siphoning off resources while the spider spends its nights hunting desperately to replenish its draining energy.

How does a tiny larva, resting on the outside of a spider's body, convince its host to completely abandon its instincts? This is one of the most chilling questions in modern biology. The larva does not have direct physical access to the spider's brain. Instead, researchers suspect that the parasite acts as an illicit chemical factory, secreting specialized bioactive compounds directly into the host's bloodstream. These chemicals circulate through the spider's body, crossing into its central nervous system and subtly altering the chemical conversations that dictate its behavior.

On the final night of the spider's life, the chemical command takes full control. The sun dips below the horizon, and the spider begins to tear down its familiar, beautiful web. Under normal circumstances, an orb-weaving spider constructs a broad, flat spiral of sticky silk designed specifically to catch flying insects. Every angle is measured, every gap calculated to intercept prey in mid-flight. But tonight, the spider ignores the classic blueprint that has guided its lineage for countless generations. A bizarre, unnatural obsession grips its eight legs.

Instead of building a wide, delicate spiral, the spider begins to weave an entirely different structure. It lays down thick, dense, non-sticky threads, crisscrossing them over and over in a tight pattern. It reinforces the anchor lines, attaching them securely to sturdy twigs and leaves with an astonishing amount of extra silk. The spider does not weave a web to catch food; it weaves a heavily fortified fortress. Why would an animal spend its remaining hours weaving a sanctuary for its own murderer? The spider works tirelessly through the night, driven by a phantom script it cannot comprehend.

Biologists often refer to this bizarre creation as a cocoon web. It is a masterpiece of custom engineering, modified specifically to protect a wasp pupa rather than catch food. The silk lines are noticeably thicker, showing incredible tensile strength capable of withstanding heavy wind and sudden rain. In some wasp species, the chemicals compel the spider to weave a dense central platform, complete with a silk canopy that serves as an umbrella. The spider is pouring the absolute last of its biological capital into an architectural fortress it will never use.

Is it an injection of neurotransmitters, or perhaps an imitation of the spider's own hormones? While researchers continue to analyze the exact cocktail of chemicals, one hypothesis is that the larva hijacks the spider's natural molting routine. When a spider prepares to shed its skin, it often builds a simpler, sturdier resting web to protect itself during vulnerability. The parasite may be tapping into this ancient survival program, amplifying the signal until the spider overbuilds the structure beyond all natural proportions. The wasp did not invent a new program; it simply pirated an existing file in the spider's brain.

By the time the first light of dawn begins to filter through the trees, the cocoon web is finally complete. The spider, thoroughly exhausted and depleted of almost all its silk reserves, retreats to the center of the reinforced platform. It stops moving, waiting in the quiet sanctuary it spent the entire night creating. Its purpose, dictated by an alien force, has been fulfilled to the letter. The larva, now large and plump, senses that the shelter is ready. The time for subtle manipulation is over, and the final stage of the tragedy begins.

With ruthless efficiency, the larva shifts from a polite parasite into a deadly predator. It bites through the spider's cuticle once again, but this time it does not hold back. The larva rapidly consumes the remaining fluids, tissues, and internal organs of the faithful architect that just sheltered it. Within hours, the spider's life fades completely, leaving behind nothing more than a shriveled, hollow husk. The lifeless shell drops from the platform or dangles silently in the breeze, discarded like a spent tool whose job is finished.

Now alone on the sturdy silk stage, the well-fed larva begins its own construction project. It draws upon the rich nutrients stolen from the spider's body to spin a thick, fibrous cocoon around itself. Suspended safely in the center of the reinforced web, the cocoon hangs sheltered from heavy raindrops and hidden from wandering eyes. The larva enters a deep sleep, completely protected by the very hands of the creature it destroyed. Inside that silk chamber, a magnificent and macabre metamorphosis takes place over the coming weeks.

Eventually, the cocoon splits open, and a sleek, fully formed adult wasp emerges into the warm morning air. It tests its wings, wipes its antennae, and launches into the forest canopy, leaving behind the empty tomb. Somewhere in the surrounding undergrowth, another young spider sits quietly on an orb web, waiting for flying insects, entirely unaware that this cycle of deception has begun anew. The wasp flies off to mate, find another host, and repeat the dark ballet of chemical control.

What makes this phenomenon even more haunting is that parasitoid wasps are not the only entities capable of turning spiders into mindless wanderers. Consider the mysterious mermithid nematodes, long and slender parasitic worms that develop inside terrestrial spiders. These worms grow silently within the spider's body cavity, absorbing nutrients until they reach a length many times greater than the host itself. When the worm reaches maturity, it faces a profound dilemma: it must return to an aquatic environment to mate and lay eggs, but spiders typically avoid open water at all costs.

To solve this problem, the nematode takes direct control of the spider's behavioral compass. In a dramatic reversal of natural instincts, the infected spider suddenly leaves its dry shelter and wanders aimlessly toward the nearest body of water, whether a quiet puddle, a forest stream, or a garden pond. Drawn by an overwhelming, unnatural thirst or a complete loss of water aversion, the spider plunges directly into the surface. The moment the spider hits the water, the giant worm bursts through the abdomen and swims away, leaving the drowned host behind.

There is also the silent, spectral world of entomopathogenic fungi, such as species from the genus Gibellula. Spores from these specialized fungi drift through the damp forest air and land on the spider's exoskeleton. Using specialized enzymes, the fungus dissolves a pathway through the armor and spreads thread-like hyphae throughout the host's body. As the infection peaks, the fungus alters the spider's behavior, compelling it to abandon its hunting territory and climb to the underside of a tall leaf.

Once in this elevated position, the spider clamps down with its jaws, locking itself in place in an eternal grip of death. The fungus then consumes the host completely and erupts through the spider's joints in delicate, white, powdery plumes. From this high vantage point, the newly formed fungal stalks release millions of microscopic spores into the air currents, showering down upon unsuspecting spiders below. The dead spider becomes a macabre lighthouse, broadcasting death across the forest floor.

When we observe these different parasites—wasps, worms, and fungi—a striking pattern emerges across the canvas of evolution. Different branches of life, separated by hundreds of millions of years of distinct ancestry, have independently developed the ability to crack the neurological codes of other animals. They do not merely kill; they commandeer the living machine. What does it really mean to possess free will when biology can rewrite our choices so quietly? For a spider, agency is an illusion that vanishes the moment a foreign molecule locks into the right receptor.

Nature does not operate on moral principles of kindness or malice. It is an immense, ongoing experiment driven by efficiency, adaptation, and survival. The spider is neither a victim of cosmic injustice nor a hero; it is simply a brilliant engineer caught in an ancient contest against an equally brilliant saboteur. The wasp is not cruel; it is a devoted mother employing the only strategy available to ensure that her lineage endures in an unforgiving world.

Tomorrow morning, as you sip your coffee and step outside into the crisp air, take a moment to look at the garden around you. The world seems peaceful on the surface, bathed in soft golden light and gentle birdsong. But look closer at the dewdrops hanging from a silk thread, or the subtle shape of a leaf curled in the breeze. A thousand quiet battles are taking place simultaneously in the soil, the air, and the branches. Realities are being hijacked, fortresses are being built for uninvited guests, and the grand wheel of life keeps turning without pausing for sentiment.

Life is infinitely more complex, mysterious, and beautifully unsettling than it appears at first glance. The next time you see a spider weaving its geometric wheel in the corner of your window, perhaps you will look at it with a renewed sense of wonder and respect. It is a fragile survivor in a world full of unseen puppet masters, creating its masterpieces while it still owns its mind.

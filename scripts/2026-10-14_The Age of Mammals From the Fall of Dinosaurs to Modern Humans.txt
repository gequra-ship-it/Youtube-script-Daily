=== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 ===

Take a quiet look at the palm of your hand.

Feel the subtle warmth pulsing just beneath your skin.

Where did this steady internal heat actually come from?

Every heartbeat carries an unbroken memory of an ancient world.

Sixty-six million years ago, our ancestors were hiding in the dirt.

They were tiny creatures trembling beneath the feet of giants.

Then, in a single blinding flash, the giants vanished forever.

How did those fragile shadows rise to rule the entire planet?

Welcome to Miya's Lab.

Today, we journey through the grand history of the Cenozoic era.

Imagine standing in a silent forest during the late Cretaceous.

The trees are towering conifers and broad flowering plants.

Huge sauropods shake the earth with every heavy step.

Serrated teeth gleam in the shadows beneath the dense ferns.

Mammals already exist in this vibrant, dangerous world.

Yet they are small, mostly nocturnal, and constantly cautious.

Many look like modern shrews or tiny opossums.

They scurry under fallen logs while the titans dominate daylight.

Their warm fur protects them from the damp chill of night.

They listen closely with exceptionally delicate middle ear bones.

These sensitive ears once formed parts of an ancient reptile jaw.

Over millions of years, those tiny bones drifted and specialized.

In the dark, hearing every snapping twig means surviving another hour.

Then comes the day that divides earthly time into two chapters.

A massive asteroid strikes the shallow waters of modern Mexico.

The impact unleashes an unimaginable surge of kinetic energy.

Shockwaves tear across continents at terrifying speeds.

Wildfires ignite across vast forests in mere minutes.

Soot and vaporized rock shoot straight into the upper atmosphere.

A thick, suffocating blanket of darkness envelops the entire globe.

Sunlight cannot reach the forest floor for many months.

Without sunlight, green leaves wither and photosynthesis grinds to a halt.

Herbivorous dinosaurs starve as their lush pastures disappear.

Soon, the great carnivores collapse from a complete lack of food.

Nearly three-quarters of all living species are wiped clean.

Yet beneath the smoking ash, tiny heartbeats continue to thrum.

Some small mammals survive deep inside cool underground burrows.

Others shelter in dark riverbanks, eating insects and decaying roots.

Being small suddenly becomes the greatest superpower on Earth.

You do not need tons of vegetation when you weigh fifty grams.

A few seeds and buried grubs are enough to sustain a family.

When the dust finally clears, the sky reveals a quiet world.

The colossal rulers of the Mesozoic are gone forever.

Only one group of dinosaurs survives into this new dawn.

We still see them singing on our telephone wires today as birds.

Aside from these feathered survivors, the land stands wide open.

This dramatic boundary marks the birth of the Paleocene epoch.

Nature suddenly possesses millions of empty ecological stages.

Who will claim the silent plains and the regenerating forests?

At first, giant flightless birds try to seize the crown.

Massive terror birds stalk the open meadows with crushing beaks.

Enormous reptiles, like giant ground snakes, thrive in the tropical warmth.

Yet mammals begin to experiment with form and size at dizzying speeds.

Within a few million years, their body sizes expand remarkably.

Some lineages develop broad teeth for chewing tough new leaves.

Others sharpen their claws and reshape their jaws for hunting.

Mother mammals possess an extraordinary secret advantage.

They feed their newborn young with rich, nourishing milk.

This means offspring do not depend immediately on foraging outside.

Parents can shelter their vulnerable babies inside protected dens.

Furthermore, many mammals develop advanced placentas inside their bodies.

A placenta allows embryos to grow safely in a controlled environment.

Young are born far more developed and resilient than fragile eggs.

The global climate soon climbs toward an astonishing thermal peak.

Tropical palm trees sprout in regions near modern Alaska and Greenland.

Crocodiles bask lazily in warm waters near the Arctic Circle.

This steamy greenhouse world is known as the Eocene epoch.

Dense, humid jungles stretch almost from pole to pole.

In these endless canopies, early primates leap between mossy branches.

They develop grasping hands with flat nails instead of curved claws.

Their eyes gradually shift forward to face the same direction.

This forward gaze creates stereoscopic vision and precise depth perception.

Can you accurately judge the distance to the next swaying branch?

Those who miscalculate do not leave descendants behind.

Meanwhile, along swampy coastlines, another strange transition begins.

Certain four-legged land mammals wade into warm shallow bays.

Perhaps they are hunting slow fish or escaping terrestrial predators.

Over generations, their bodies grow sleeker and their legs shorten.

Their nostrils gradually migrate toward the top of their skulls.

Millions of years later, these clumsy paddlers become majestic whales.

Think about that breathtaking transformation for a quiet moment.

The largest animal to ever swim the seas began as a walking creature.

Life constantly reshapes old tools to conquer entirely new realms.

Eventually, the prolonged tropical fever begins to break.

Continents drift relentlessly across the shifting crust of the planet.

Antarctica separates from South America and Australia.

A fierce, freezing ocean current begins to circle the south pole.

Cut off from equatorial warmth, Antarctica slowly freezes into ice.

This thermal barrier cools the entire planet step by step.

The lush, continuous rainforests begin to shrink toward the equator.

The Oligocene epoch arrives with cooler, significantly drier winds.

In this drying world, dense jungle canopies break apart into open woodlands.

Animals that relied on soft tropical fruits face sudden food shortages.

Tougher shrubs and abrasive plants replace juicy jungle foliage.

Mammals must evolve sturdier teeth or perish in the cold.

Some creatures solve this problem by growing truly colossal.

Consider the massive indricotheres, ancient cousins of the rhinoceros.

They tower meters into the air to browse the highest surviving leaves.

They are among the largest land mammals to ever walk the Earth.

Yet maintaining such enormous bulk requires vast amounts of vegetation.

As the climate continues to cool, weather patterns grow unpredictable.

Then comes the Miocene epoch, bringing a revolutionary botanical change.

Across broad continents, a new kind of plant spreads like wildfire.

It is humble, resilient, and remarkably durable: true grass.

Grass does not grow from the tip like an ordinary forest leaf.

It grows from the base, right near the surface of the soil.

If an animal bites off the top, the grass simply grows back.

This simple evolutionary trick makes grasslands nearly indestructible.

However, grass hides a brutal defense mechanism against hungry herbivores.

Its tissues contain microscopic particles of hard silica, essentially glass.

Chewing grass is like chewing mouthfuls of fine, abrasive sandpaper.

Herbivores with soft, short teeth quickly grind their enamel down to nothing.

Starvation looms for any creature unprepared for this gritty diet.

Natural selection immediately begins forging new dental miracles.

Horses and antelopes evolve remarkably tall, high-crowned teeth.

Layers of hard enamel and cementum wear down at different speeds.

This uneven wear creates self-sharpening ridges ideal for grinding silica.

At the same time, open grasslands provide absolutely nowhere to hide.

A predator can spot a grazing herd from miles across the flat horizon.

Survival transforms into a deadly, high-speed arms race.

Herbivores lengthen their lower limb bones to maximize running stride.

They elevate their bodies onto the tips of hardened toenails, becoming hooves.

Side toes gradually shrink and fuse to form strong, springy levers.

Predators respond with longer legs, flexible spines, and stealthy hunting tactics.

Cats and dogs diversify, refining the art of the sudden, explosive chase.

The Earth is beginning to look strikingly familiar to our modern eyes.

Majestic herds thunder across vast savannahs under sweeping skies.

Yet dynamic continental collisions are still rearranging the map.

Africa gently collides with Eurasia, allowing spectacular animal exchanges.

Elephants migrate into Asia and Europe, while carnivores invade Africa.

Later, the Isthmus of Panama rises from the depths of the ocean.

This slender strip of land connects North and South America.

Opossums and armadillos march boldly northward across the new bridge.

Bears, cats, and camels travel southward into lush South American valleys.

This Great American Biotic Interchange reshapes ecosystems across two continents.

Meanwhile, the rise of Panama redirects major ocean currents worldwide.

The Atlantic and Pacific oceans are severed, altering global weather patterns.

The world enters the Pliocene epoch, drifting toward an even colder regime.

In East Africa, the drying climate creates dramatic ecological shifts.

The great Rift Valley forms, carving rugged cliffs and deep depressions.

Vast continuous rainforests fracture into scattered patches of woodland and savannah.

For arboreal primates, this landscape poses an existential crisis.

The safe canopy pathways are disappearing season after season.

To reach the next patch of fruiting trees, you must cross open ground.

Crossing the grass on all fours leaves you blind to stalking predators.

A leopard can easily ambush an ape crawling through tall yellow stems.

What happens if you stand upright on your hind legs?

Suddenly, your eyes rise high above the concealing grass.

You can spot a predator prowling hundreds of paces away.

Standing upright also exposes less of your body to the scorching midday sun.

Cool breezes can circulate freely around your heated torso.

Most importantly of all, your two front hands are completely free.

They no longer need to bear your weight during every awkward step.

You can carry nourishing food back to vulnerable infants.

You can pick up a heavy stone or a pointed wooden branch.

Bipedal walking is not merely a different posture; it is a destiny.

Hominins take their first tentative steps across the sun-drenched savannah.

Their feet develop sturdy arches, and their big toes align forward.

With free hands, they begin to explore the physical properties of their world.

A broken river pebble reveals a sharp, glass-like edge.

With that sharp edge, you can slice meat from an abandoned carcass.

You can smash tough long bones to extract rich, fatty bone marrow.

This nutrient-dense marrow provides an explosion of concentrated calories.

No other ape can access marrow locked inside dense, stony bone.

Our ancestors find a way using brains, hands, and fractured stones.

Gradually, over hundreds of thousands of years, brain sizes begin to expand.

Maintaining a large brain requires an enormous share of daily energy.

Roughly twenty percent of your resting metabolic calories feed your brain right now.

That energetic cost could only be paid through nutrient-dense foods.

As the Pliocene yields to the Pleistocene, the planet enters a deep freeze.

Massive sheets of glacial ice advance from the polar north.

Gigantic glaciers grind across continents, retreating and advancing in cyclic rhythms.

This is the famous Ice Age, a world of harsh extremes.

Woolly mammoths grow dense coats of shaggy fur and layers of insulating fat.

Saber-toothed cats stalk heavy prey across wind-swept mammoth steppes.

Giant ground sloths lumber through forests, stripping branches bare.

In this brutally challenging landscape, early humans face ultimate tests of survival.

They do not possess thick fur coats to shield against howling blizzards.

They do not have elongated ivory tusks or razor-sharp fangs for defense.

Instead, they possess something far more adaptable than physical armor.

They learn to harness the dancing flame of fire.

Fire provides brilliant light inside pitch-black caves.

It keeps terrifying predators shivering outside the warm circle of embers.

Cooking over flames softens tough fibrous tubers and sterilizes raw meat.

Cooked food is far easier to digest, freeing even more energy for developing minds.

Around those crackling hearths, something profoundly new awakens on Earth.

Humans sit together in the darkness, sharing stories through complex spoken sounds.

They begin to anticipate tomorrow, plan for winter, and remember yesterday.

They paint running bison and leaping horses onto dark limestone walls.

They bury their fallen companions with ochre and delicate flower petals.

Empathy, abstract imagination, and cultural memory take root in the soil.

Then, around ten to twelve thousand years ago, the last great ice sheets retreat.

The climate stabilizes into a gentle, remarkably predictable warmth.

Across multiple fertile river valleys, humans notice a curious phenomenon.

Discarded seeds near seasonal campsites sprout into familiar edible plants.

Instead of endlessly following migrating herds, why not nurture those seeds?

Agriculture begins, and with it, wandering bands settle down into villages.

Permanent walls rise from sun-baked mud and woven reed mats.

Surplus grain is stored in clay jars, guarding against unexpected famine.

For the first time in natural history, not everyone must spend daylight hunting.

Some people mold clay pots, others observe the cyclical motion of stars.

Towns expand into bustling cities, and cities form organized civilizations.

Writing emerges as small incisions on soft clay tablets to count sheep and grain.

Recorded history begins, a mere blink against the vast backdrop of deep time.

Think back to that dark morning sixty-six million years ago.

The world seemed entirely finished when the great asteroid struck.

Smoke smothered the sun, and the undisputed masters of the planet fell.

Yet life did not wave a white flag of defeat.

Fragile, shivering mammals stepped out from damp underground burrows.

They did not know that their distant lineage would cross stormy oceans.

They did not know their descendants would run across grassy plains as wild horses.

They could never imagine that their offspring would build telescopes to study stars.

Every challenge the planet delivered became an invitation to adapt.

When the world froze, mammals grew thick fur and forged social bonds.

When the forests vanished, our ancestors stood tall on two feet to see farther.

Now, take another look at your hands resting quietly before you.

Those five fingers were once shaped for gripping swaying jungle branches.

Your warm skin carries the thermal legacy of creatures that survived nuclear winters.

Your curious mind inherits millions of years of relentless evolutionary trials.

We are not detached visitors walking across the surface of the Earth.

We are the living, breathing continuation of the great Cenozoic story.

Every breath you draw connects you to the first tiny survivor in the ash.

How will we choose to write the next chapter of this magnificent legacy?

[숏츠 1] 제목: The Day Sandpaper Changed Animal Teeth

Did you know that humble grass once broke the teeth of ancient giants?

About twenty million years ago, lush forests shrank across the planet.

In their place, an entirely new plant conquered the continents: true grass.

Unlike soft forest leaves, grass blades are filled with microscopic glass particles called silica.

Chewing this gritty grass was like grinding teeth with industrial sandpaper.

Animals with short, soft teeth quickly wore their enamel down to the gums.

Starvation wiped out entire branches of ancient mammals across the globe.

To survive, ancestors of horses and antelopes forged evolutionary miracles.

They grew incredibly tall teeth with specialized self-sharpening enamel ridges.

At the same time, open fields forced them onto the tips of their toenails.

Those hardened toenails eventually evolved into the springy hooves we see today.

[숏츠 2] 제목: Why Your Brain Burns Your Lunch

Why does your brain consume a fifth of everything you eat today?

Millions of years ago, early hominins faced starvation on drying African plains.

Fruit trees were disappearing, replaced by vast seas of tall grass.

Without massive claws or sharp fangs, our ancestors had to find another way.

They picked up heavy river stones and smashed discarded animal bones.

Inside lay rich, fatty bone marrow that no other predator could reach.

This sudden jackpot of dense calories fueled an unprecedented evolutionary leap.

Over generations, our ancestors used those surplus calories to build larger brains.

Later, mastering fire softened food and unlocked even more energy for our minds.

Today, your brain weighs only two percent of your total body mass.

Yet it relentlessly burns twenty percent of your daily resting metabolic calories.

You are carrying the metabolic legacy of stone-wielding survivors.

=== 한국어 요약 (전체 번역이 아닌 요약본) ===
■ 영상 제목: Why Did the Rulers of Ancient Seas Vanish? (고대 바다의 지배자들은 왜 사라졌을까?)
■ 한 줄 요약: 약 2억 7천만 년 동안 바다를 지배했던 삼엽충의 놀라운 생물학적 성공과 진화적 적응, 그리고 세 차례의 대멸종을 거쳐 페름기 말 해양 산성화와 무산소증으로 최종 절멸하기까지의 역사와 현대적 교훈을 다룬다.
■ 전체 흐름 (대본 순서대로 8~15줄):
- 바닷가 조약돌 화석을 언급하며 인류나 공룡보다 훨씬 오랜 기간 번성했던 삼엽충의 기원을 소개한다.
- 현생 인류(약 30만 년) 및 공룡(약 1억 6,500만 년)과 비교해 삼엽충이 약 2억 7천만 년 동안 장기 생존했음을 강조한다.
- 2만 종이 넘는 다양한 크기와 형태, 3개의 세로 엽과 분절된 모듈식 신체 구조를 설명한다.
- 광물질 방해석(calcite) 결정으로 이루어진 독특한 겹눈과 몸을 둥글게 마는 기계적 방어 기작(enrolment)을 조명한다.
- 5억 년 전 캄브리아기 바다에서의 폭발적 번성과 잦은 탈피로 인한 풍부한 화석 기록을 다룬다.
- 약 4억 4,000만 년 전 남극 빙하 확장과 해수면 급감으로 인한 오르도비스기 말 대멸종을 극복한 과정을 서술한다.
- 거대 바다전갈, 두족류, 턱을 가진 어류의 등장에 맞서 가시 돌기와 잠망경 눈 등을 발전시킨 군비경쟁을 묘사한다.
- 약 3억 7,000만 년 전 데본기 후기 해양 무산소증(anoxia)으로 대다수가 사라지고 프로에투스목(proetids)만 유일하게 살아남았음을 설명한다.
- 석탄기와 페름기 동안 좁아진 유전적 다양성 속에서도 끈질기게 생존을 이어간 프로에투스목의 한계를 짚는다.
- 약 2억 5,200만 년 전 시베리아 대규모 화산 분출로 촉발된 페름기 말 대멸종(Great Dying)의 시작을 밝힌다.
- 온실가스 폭증에 따른 급격한 해양 산성화로 탄산칼슘 껍질이 녹아내리고, 해저 무산소 데드존이 확산되어 삼엽충이 완전 절멸에 이른다.
- 삼엽충의 멸종은 진화적 실패가 아닌 위대한 성공이었으며, 투구게 등 현대 절지동물과의 연관성 및 오늘날의 기후 위기에 주는 경고를 전한다.
■ 핵심 사실·숫자 (5~10개):
- 삼엽충은 고생대 바다에서 약 2억 7천만 년(roughly 270 million years) 동안 번성함 (공룡은 약 1억 6,500만 년, 현생 인류는 약 30만 년).
- 고생물학계에 기재된 삼엽충 종(species) 수는 2만 종(20,000 species) 이상임.
- 삼엽충의 겹눈은 광물질 탄산칼슘의 일종인 순수 방해석(calcite) 결정으로 구성되었으며, 수중 왜곡을 없애는 이중 렌즈 시스템을 갖춤.
- 포식자로부터 연약한 다리와 아가미를 보호하기 위해 몸을 완벽한 구체로 말아 잠그는 방어 기작(enrolment)을 발전시킴.
- 약 4억 4,000만 년 전 오르도비스기 말 대멸종(빙하 확장 및 해수면 급감)을 견뎌냄.
- 약 3억 7,000만 년 전 데본기 후기 해양 무산소증 위기 이후 오직 프로에투스목(proetids) 단 한 계통만 생존함.
- 약 2억 5,200만 년 전 페름기 말 대멸종(Great Dying) 당시 해양 생물종의 약 96%가 절멸하였으며, 시베리아 화산 분출로 인한 해수 산성화와 무산소증으로 삼엽충이 영구 절멸함.
■ 숏츠 5편:
- 숏츠 1: The Creatures with Eyes Made of Stone (돌로 된 눈을 가진 생물) — 부패하기 쉬운 부드러운 유기 조직 대신 순수한 방해석(calcite) 광물 결정으로 겹눈을 만든 삼엽충의 독특한 시각 구조를 소개한다. 수중 광학 왜곡을 없애는 정교한 이중 렌즈 시스템 덕분에 화석화된 후 수억 년이 지난 오늘날에도 과학자들이 그 렌즈를 통해 고대 바다를 들여다볼 수 있음을 밝힌다.
- 숏츠 2: They Ruled Earth Longer Than Dinosaurs (공룡보다 더 오래 지구를 지배한 존재) — 현생 인류의 약 30만 년 역사나 공룡의 약 1억 6,500만 년 존속 기간을 아득히 뛰어넘어, 약 2억 7천만 년 동안 지구 바다를 지배했던 삼엽충의 압도적인 생존력을 조명한다. 2만 종 이상의 다채로운 형태로 번성하며 두 차례의 글로벌 대멸종까지 극복했던 이들이 왜 영구히 사라졌는지 의문을 던진다.
- 숏츠 3: The Prehistoric Living Armored Ball (선사의 살아있는 장갑 구체) — 거대 바다전갈과 사나운 두족류 같은 초기 포식자들의 공격을 피하기 위해 현대 공벌레처럼 몸을 완벽히 둥글게 말아 방어판을 맞물렸던 삼엽충의 '몸 말기(enrolment)' 기작을 설명한다. 이후 턱을 가진 어류가 등장하자 껍질을 부수는 치명적 포식에 맞서 뾰족한 가시와 잠망경 눈을 진화시키며 치열한 군비경쟁을 벌였음을 보여준다.
- 숏츠 4: When the Earth's Oceans Turned to Acid (지구의 바다가 산성으로 변했을 때) — 2억 5,200만 년 전 시베리아 화산 폭발로 이산화탄소가 바다에 대량 용해되면서 해양이 치명적인 산성으로 변해버린 '위대한 죽음(Great Dying)'을 다룬다. 탄산칼슘으로 단단한 껍질을 유지해야 했던 삼엽충은 산성 바닷물에 방어구가 녹아내리고 산소마저 고갈되어 최후의 파멸을 맞이하게 된다.
- 숏츠 5: The Lone Family That Almost Saved an Empire (제국을 구할 뻔했던 유일한 가문) — 3억 7,000만 년 전 데본기 후기 대멸종으로 화려했던 거대 삼엽충들이 모두 사라지고 작고 단순한 형태의 프로에투스목(proetids)만 간신히 살아남았던 역사를 조명한다. 이 홀로 남은 계통이 1억 년 가까이 삼엽충의 명맥을 이었으나, 극도로 좁아진 유전적 다양성 때문에 페름기 말 최종 위기에서 끝내 적응하지 못하고 멸종했음을 설명한다.

[영상 제목]: Why Did the Rulers of Ancient Seas Vanish?

=== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 ===

Have you ever picked up an ordinary stone from the beach and wondered how old it truly is? Maybe you noticed a subtle, ribbed pattern etched into the rock, shaped like a tiny shield. You might brush it off as a trick of erosion, but what if you are holding the remains of an ancient traveler? Long before the first blade of grass grew on land, and long before the first dinosaur took a breath, creatures like this completely dominated the global ocean. They ruled this planet for nearly three hundred million years. Why did an empire that durable suddenly disappear?

To understand just how strange that disappearance is, we have to look at our own brief story. Our human species has lived on Earth for only a tiny fraction of evolutionary time. Even if you count all our hominid ancestors, we have walked the continents for merely a few million years. The famous dinosaurs thrived for roughly one hundred and sixty-five million years. Yet trilobites quietly outlived them all, crawling through primeval waters across an astonishing span of roughly two hundred and seventy million years. How does a creature survive that long, only to vanish forever?

When you hear the word fossil, you might imagine a dusty museum corner. But trilobites were once as vibrant and ubiquitous as beetles are in our forests today. Paleontologists have cataloged over twenty thousand distinct species across the globe. Some were no larger than a grain of sand, drifting effortlessly through sunlit surface waters. Others grew to the size of a large serving platter, creeping slowly across dark oceanic mud. Their bodies came in almost every imaginable variation, equipped with delicate spikes, bizarre shovels, and elegant flowing spines. They were everywhere, touching nearly every corner of ancient marine life.

Have you ever wondered where their peculiar name comes from? It simply describes the three distinct longitudinal lobes that run down their backs. A central axial lobe protected their delicate vital systems, flanked on either side by two pleural lobes guarding their legs and gills. Their bodies were divided horizontally too, into a head shield, a flexible segmented thorax, and a tail section. This modular body plan was an evolutionary masterpiece. It allowed trilobites to adapt to nearly every marine niche, from freezing polar trenches to warm equatorial shallows. They were built to endure almost anything.

Perhaps the most astonishing marvel of their anatomy lay right on their heads. Unlike modern animals with soft organic eye tissue, trilobites grew compound eyes made of pure mineral crystals. Their optical lenses were literally crafted from solid calcite, a crystal form of calcium carbonate. Imagine looking at the world through thousands of tiny, perfectly aligned rock prisms. These rigid stones did not warp or rot after death, which is why scientists can still study their precise optical geometry today. Some species even developed double-lens systems that eliminated optical distortion under water. Could you imagine seeing through living stone?

Seeing danger was only half the battle, because the ancient oceans were full of hazards. When threatened, many trilobite species developed a mechanical response that you can still observe in modern garden pill bugs. They rolled their segmented bodies into tightly sealed, impenetrable balls. Special grooves along the edges of their hard mineral head and tail plates locked together seamlessly. Any soft, vulnerable legs and breathing gills were tucked safely inside a spherical stone fortress. For millions of years, this simple maneuver protected them from almost every known predator that drifted above the seabed.

During the Cambrian period, more than five hundred million years ago, marine life erupted in a burst of evolutionary creativity. In that warm, shallow world, trilobites were undisputed masters of the sea floor. Some swam upside down in search of food, while others plowed through nutrient-rich silt, sifting out microscopic meals. Because they grew by periodically shedding their tough outer exoskeletons, a single animal could leave behind dozens of fossilized shells throughout its lifetime. That is why their stony armor is so incredibly abundant in rock layers across modern mountains, deserts, and valleys today.

No golden age lasts forever. Around four hundred and forty million years ago, the planet encountered its first severe global crisis: the Late Ordovician mass extinction. Massive ice sheets rapidly expanded across ancient southern continents, locking away enormous volumes of water and causing sea levels to plummet worldwide. The warm, shallow continental shelves where trilobites thrived suddenly dried up or cooled dramatically. Many specialized families that could not tolerate freezing temperatures simply vanished. Yet, despite suffering catastrophic population losses, resilient survivors pulled through. They proved that their design could outlast a planetary freeze.

When the climate eventually stabilized, the oceans became far more competitive and dangerous than before. The peaceful mud plains turned into hunting grounds. Enormous sea scorpions with powerful grasping claws prowled the ocean bottom, while giant cephalopods with sharp beaks snatched prey from above. Soon after, the first heavily armored, jawed fish arrived on the evolutionary scene. Suddenly, simply rolling into a stone ball was no longer enough. Hard jaws could easily crush a thin calcite shell. Trilobites found themselves caught in an intensifying arms race against faster, smarter, and stronger predators.

How did trilobites respond to these terrifying new neighbors? They adapted with astonishing speed and sheer morphological creativity. Many species grew dense forests of sharp, forbidding spikes across their head shields and back plates. Others evolved extended periscope eyes that allowed them to bury themselves entirely in the sediment, watching for danger while remaining invisible. Some lost their eyes altogether, adapting to life in the perpetual darkness of deep abyssal trenches where visual predators could not hunt. Every new defensive spine tells a silent story of evolutionary survival under immense pressure.

Then came the Late Devonian crisis, roughly three hundred and seventy million years ago. This prolonged catastrophe dealt trilobites a blow from which they would never truly recover. Scientists suspect that widespread ocean anoxia, a severe and suffocating drop in dissolved oxygen, suffocated marine ecosystems over several million years. Shallow coral reef systems collapsed entirely. Vast dead zones spread across the ocean floor, choking the benthic environments that trilobites called home. Family after family disappeared into the geological record, leaving behind empty ecological niches that other emerging organisms quickly claimed.

When the environmental dust finally settled after the Devonian turmoil, the mighty trilobite empire was reduced to a mere shadow of its former glory. Out of numerous thriving orders, only a single group, known as the proetids, managed to survive. Gone were the flamboyant giants, the bizarre spade-nosed scavengers, and the heavily spined monstrosities. The surviving proetids were mostly small, unassuming creatures with modest, oval bodies. They were generalists, clinging to life along remaining shallow waters. They had weathered the storm, but their evolutionary flexibility had been severely narrowed down.

During the Carboniferous and early Permian periods, Earth transformed dramatically. Vast swampy forests covered the land, eventually forming the coal beds that power modern human factories. In the oceans, sharks and bony fish ruled the open waters, leaving little room for trilobites to reclaim their ancient dominance. Still, these humble survivors held on for tens of millions of years. They were no longer the rulers of the seabed, but they were remarkably persistent. Have you ever wondered why they survived two catastrophic global extinctions, only to meet their end in a third?

The final act of their story began approximately two hundred and fifty-two million years ago, at the close of the Permian period. Geologists refer to this event as the Great Dying, and it remains the most catastrophic extinction in our planet's known history. Nearly ninety-six percent of all marine species were wiped out, making it far more destructive than the asteroid strike that later doomed the dinosaurs. The world did not end in a single fiery instant, but through a grinding, toxic nightmare that lasted for hundreds of thousands of years.

What triggered this unprecedented global nightmare? Massive volcanic fissures in what is now modern Siberia erupted with unimaginable fury, tearing through the continental crust. Millions of cubic kilometers of molten basalt flooded across the landscape, burning through thick subterranean deposits of coal and carbonate rocks. This catastrophic volcanic furnace released vast plumes of carbon dioxide, sulfur compounds, and toxic methane into the atmosphere. The global climate surged in temperature, heating ancient oceans to bathtub temperatures and altering atmospheric chemistry on a scale life had never encountered before.

As colossal amounts of carbon dioxide dissolved into the surface water, the chemical balance of the global ocean changed drastically. The seas became dangerously acidic. For animals with shells made of calcium carbonate, this was an existential death sentence. Building and repairing a hard calcite exoskeleton requires a delicate balance of minerals in the surrounding water. In an acidic ocean, those essential mineral building blocks dissolve away. Imagine trying to build a stone house while the very mortar you use dissolves in the acidic rain. Trilobites simply could not maintain their iconic armor.

To make matters far worse, warm water holds significantly less dissolved oxygen than cold water. As global sea temperatures soared, the oceanic conveyor belts that circulate oxygen from polar regions to the deep seabed slowed to a near halt. Lethal dead zones, entirely devoid of oxygen and saturated with poisonous hydrogen sulfide, expanded across the ocean floor. Trilobites were primarily benthic animals, meaning they crawled along the seabed. When the very mud beneath them turned toxic and completely suffocated, they had nowhere left to run.

You might wonder why they could not adapt just one more time. After all, they had survived severe global freezes and predatory revolutions before. But evolution requires time and genetic variety. Because the Devonian extinction had pruned their family tree down to just one narrow lineage, trilobites lacked the genetic diversity needed to evolve rapid workarounds. Furthermore, the speed and scale of the Permian disaster were simply too extreme. When your shell dissolves, your food web collapses, and your breathing water turns to poison, survival becomes an impossible equation.

Somewhere in the warm, acidic shallows of the latest Permian sea, the very last trilobite curled up into its defensive ball for the final time. Its crystal eyes could not see any friendly shelter in the murky, oxygen-starved water. Its tightly locked armor, an engineering marvel that had preserved its kind for nearly three hundred million years, could not protect it against the invisible chemistry of a poisoned ocean. When that lone creature stopped moving, an evolutionary dynasty that had witnessed the birth of complex life quietly slipped into eternity.

When we hear that a species went extinct, we often instinctively think of it as a failure of survival. But is that really a fair verdict? Look at the numbers. Trilobites thrived for approximately two hundred and seventy million years. During that unfathomable stretch of time, thousands of generations adapted, radiated into every corner of the sea, and developed technological marvels like crystal vision. Our own species has barely existed for three hundred thousand years. By any objective evolutionary measure, trilobites were not a failure; they were one of the greatest success stories in Earth's history.

Even though true trilobites are gone forever, their distant echoes still linger around us today. If you have ever watched a horseshoe crab scuttle along a muddy coastline at low tide, you have seen a living shadow of that primeval world. While horseshoe crabs belong to a different branch of arthropods, their armored carapaces, segmented undersides, and ancient lifestyles remind us of how trilobites once moved. When you examine modern crustaceans, insects, and spiders, you are looking at distant cousins that share the same jointed-leg blueprint trilobites perfected so long ago.

Their dramatic disappearance also holds a profound mirror to our modern world. Today, our global factories, transport networks, and cities are releasing greenhouse gases at an unprecedented rate, warming our oceans and altering their delicate chemical balance. Corals and shell-building creatures in our modern seas are already struggling to form their skeletons in acidifying waters. Looking back at the rocks beneath our feet reminds us that the fundamental laws of chemistry and ecology do not negotiate. Even the most durable, heavily armored masters of the planet cannot survive if their living habitat collapses.

Take a quiet moment during your daily commute to look around at the concrete buildings, paved roads, and steel towers. All of this feels permanent, sturdy, and unyielding. Yet, to the deep geological history of our planet, human civilization is merely a brief heartbeat. Layers of sandstone and limestone have buried thousands of civilizations before any human ever spoke a word. Every stone countertop in your kitchen, cut from ancient marine sedimentary rock, might hold pulverized remnants of life that crawled in sunlit seas hundreds of millions of years ago.

The next time you walk along a quiet shore and pick up a smooth, weathered stone, look at it with gentle curiosity. Run your fingers across its cool, ridged surface and remember the silent travelers who once called that very rock their home. They lived, they adapted, and they saw an ancient world that no human eye will ever witness in person. Extinction eventually claimed them, as it may one day claim us all. But their legacy remains forever carved into the stone bones of our living Earth.

[숏츠 1] 제목: The Creatures with Eyes Made of Stone

Did you know that hundreds of millions of years ago, creatures walked the bottom of the sea with eyes made of literal rock? It sounds like an invention from a fantasy novel, but it is pure evolutionary reality. Modern animals, including you and me, see through soft organic lenses made of liquid proteins and flexible cells. When an animal dies, those delicate tissues decompose within days. But trilobites evolved an entirely different approach. Their compound eyes were built from solid, optical-grade calcite crystals, a mineral form of calcium carbonate. Imagine gazing into primeval waters through thousands of microscopic stone prisms, perfectly aligned to capture incoming light. Because calcite is a rigid mineral, their eyes could not flex to change focus. Instead, ancient nature engineered astonishing double-lens systems inside their crystal optics, canceling out distortion and allowing them to track predators and prey simultaneously. Because these lenses were crafted from durable rock, they did not rot away after death. Today, when paleontologists carefully slice through a five-hundred-million-year-old trilobite fossil, they can still peer through the very same crystal lenses that guided these ancient navigators across the floor of a forgotten ocean. What else made these rock-eyed creatures masters of the sea for hundreds of millions of years? [숏츠 2] 제목: They Ruled Earth Longer Than Dinosaurs

Human civilization feels ancient to us, but what if I told you our entire history is barely a blink compared to trilobites? Modern humans have existed on Earth for roughly three hundred thousand years. Even if you count all our tool-making ancestral relatives, we have walked the land for merely a few million years. The mighty dinosaurs, celebrated for their immense evolutionary endurance, roamed the continents for around one hundred and sixty-five million years. Yet trilobites quietly outlived them all, crawling through prehistoric seas across an astonishing span of nearly two hundred and seventy million years. Think about that timeframe for a moment. Trilobites watched continents drift apart and collide. They survived two of the most devastating mass extinctions in planetary history, including global freezes that locked entire oceans into ice sheets. Over twenty thousand different species evolved, ranging from creatures the size of a sand speck to armored behemoths larger than dinner plates. They perfected mineral vision, mastered defensive armor, and conquered every corner of the global seabed. How could a creature survive that long, through ice ages and predatory wars, only to suddenly vanish forever? Watch our full-length documentary to uncover the terrifying planetary catastrophe that finally brought down the greatest dynasty Earth has ever seen.

[숏츠 3] 제목: The Prehistoric Living Armored Ball

If you have ever gently touched a pill bug in your garden and watched it instantly curl into a tight ball, you witnessed a defensive tactic perfected over five hundred million years ago. In the ancient Cambrian and Ordovician seas, survival was a brutal game. Giant sea scorpions prowled the muddy ocean bottom, and prehistoric squid-like cephalopods struck with razor-sharp beaks. To stay alive, trilobites engineered one of the most successful defensive maneuvers in evolutionary history: enrolment. When danger drew near, a trilobite curled its segmented body tightly inward, tucking its delicate legs and soft breathing gills safely inside. Special locking grooves along the margins of its head shield and tail plate clicked together with microscopic mechanical precision. The creature transformed into an impenetrable, spherical fortress made of solid mineral plates. For tens of millions of years, this simple rolling trick made trilobites practically invincible to early predators that lacked crushing jaws. But evolution never stands still. Soon, armored jawed fish arrived, sparking a deadly arms race that forced trilobites to sprout defensive spikes, camouflage horns, and periscope eyes. How did they survive these terrifying oceanic hunters, and what finally pierced their unbreakable armor? Find out in our full documentary on the epic rise and fall of trilobites.

[숏츠 4] 제목: When the Earth's Oceans Turned to Acid

What happens when the entire ocean turns into acid? Two hundred and fifty-two million years ago, planet Earth experienced the Great Dying, the most catastrophic extinction in planetary history. It wiped out roughly ninety-six percent of all marine species, and it brought down the final curtain on the trilobites. The disaster was sparked in Siberia, where massive volcanic fissures unleashed millions of cubic kilometers of molten basalt, scorching massive subterranean coal beds. Colossal plumes of carbon dioxide flooded the atmosphere, triggering runaway global warming. As that carbon dioxide dissolved into the sea, ocean chemistry changed with lethal speed. The global ocean became dangerously acidic. For trilobites, who built their protective armor out of calcium carbonate, this was an inescapable nightmare. In acidic seawater, the essential mineral building blocks needed to grow and repair a shell simply dissolve away. It was like trying to construct a stone fortress while the mortar dissolves in the rain. To make matters worse, soaring water temperatures caused oxygen levels on the sea floor to collapse, creating vast, suffocating dead zones. With their shells dissolving and their breathing water poisoned, the last trilobites curled into their armor for the final time. [숏츠 5] 제목: The Lone Family That Almost Saved an Empire

Did you know that the trilobite empire was almost wiped out long before its final extinction? Around three hundred and seventy million years ago, during the Late Devonian crisis, a prolonged ecological nightmare struck the global oceans. Vast dead zones devoid of oxygen spread across the seabed, collapsing shallow reef ecosystems. Almost every famous branch of the trilobite family tree was utterly erased. The flamboyant giants, the bizarre spade-nosed scavengers, and the heavily spined monsters all vanished forever. Out of numerous thriving orders that once dominated the Cambrian waters, only a single group survived: the proetids. These were not majestic ocean giants. They were modest, small, oval-shaped generalists that quietly clung to survival in the remaining shallow waters. For the next one hundred million years, through the Carboniferous and early Permian periods, this lone lineage kept the trilobite flame burning while giant sharks and bony fish dominated the seas around them. But having their entire family tree pruned down to just one narrow branch became an evolutionary trap. When the ultimate catastrophe struck at the end of the Permian, they no longer possessed the genetic diversity needed to adapt.

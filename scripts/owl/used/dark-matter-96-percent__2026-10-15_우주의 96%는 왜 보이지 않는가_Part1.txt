[영상 제목]: The 96 Percent We Cannot See

=== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 ===

Look around the room you are in right now. Touch the desk in front of you, feel the cool air filling your lungs, or gaze at the glowing screen in your hands. Everything you see, touch, and experience feels solidly real. It feels like the entire story of reality.

But what if all of this is merely an insignificant fraction of what actually exists? Modern astrophysics tells us something deeply unsettling. Everything humanity has ever studied, from the smallest atom to the most colossal galaxy, accounts for only about 5 percent of the cosmos.

Let that number settle for a moment. Five percent. If you gathered every star you have ever seen, every planet, every speck of dust, every living thing, and put it all in one pile, that pile would be only a small part of the whole. The rest of the universe is something else entirely, and it is something we cannot see.

Welcome to Miya's Note. Today, we are going to follow the trail of clues that led astronomers to this humbling conclusion. We will meet two of the biggest mysteries in all of science, dark matter and dark energy. And we will ask the question that sits underneath them both: if most of the universe is invisible, how do we even know it is there?

First, let us think carefully about that 5 percent. Every grain of sand on Earth, every drop of ocean water, and every breath you take is made of normal atomic matter. So is our Sun, along with roughly one hundred billion stars in our galaxy alone. Even the distant supermassive black holes and glowing clouds of cosmic dust belong to this small family.

Think about how much that includes. Everything in every museum, every library, every mountain range, every ocean. All the stars that fill the night sky, and all the stars hidden behind them. We have spent thousands of years studying this small family of things, and we have learned an astonishing amount about it.

Yet, when scientists calculate the total mass and energy of the cosmos, all ordinary matter adds up to just under 5 percent. The remaining 95 percent is utterly invisible, fundamentally silent, and completely unknown. That sentence deserves a second reading. Not mostly known, with a few gaps. Utterly invisible, fundamentally silent, and completely unknown.

How did we realize that most of reality was missing? The answer did not come from a single dramatic moment. It came from astronomers who looked closely at something that seemed ordinary: how spiral galaxies rotate. A spiral galaxy is a great wheel of stars, turning slowly around its center. It was a good place to test whether our understanding of gravity holds up.

According to the established laws of physics, stars near the outer rim of a galaxy should move much slower than stars near the crowded center. It works just like our solar system, where distant planets drift lazily while inner worlds race around the Sun. The farther you are from the center of the mass, the weaker the pull, and the slower you need to move to stay in orbit.

So scientists measured the actual speed of the stars far from the center. And they encountered a profound shock. The outer stars were moving astonishingly fast. They did not drift lazily at all. They raced around the galaxy at speeds that the established laws of physics said they should not have.

In fact, these stars were moving so rapidly that the visible gravity of the galaxy could not possibly hold them. Think of a ball whirled on a string. If you spin it too fast, the string cannot hold, and the ball flies away. By all ordinary logic, these swirling galaxies should have torn themselves apart and flung their stars into the dark void long ago.

Yet, they remain beautifully intact. Something is holding them together. Scientists suspect that an immense, invisible halo of mysterious mass surrounds each galaxy, providing the extra gravitational glue needed to hold everything together. Notice the careful words: scientists suspect. This is a hypothesis built to explain what we observe, not a substance anyone has held in their hands.

Because this substance emits no light, absorbs no light, and reflects no light, we simply call it dark matter. The word dark does not mean it is a dark color, like black paint. It means it is invisible to every kind of light, so we cannot see it with any telescope. We can only notice what it does.

What could dark matter actually be? It accounts for roughly 27 percent of the entire universe, dwarfing everything we know by more than five times. For every bit of ordinary matter that makes up stars and planets and people, there is more than five times as much of this mysterious substance.

It is not made of protons, neutrons, or electrons. Those are the ingredients of everything we know. If a cloud of dark matter passed through your living room right now, you would not see it, hear it, or feel it touch your skin. It interacts with our world almost entirely through gravity.

That is the only handle we have on it. Gravity. Dark matter tugs on stars and galaxies, and we notice the tugging. Leading hypotheses suggest it may consist of undiscovered subatomic particles that barely interact with normal matter at all, but no one knows for sure. Honest science says it plainly: we know where it seems to be, and we do not know what it is.

As if a missing 27 percent were not baffling enough, the universe had an even bigger surprise waiting for us. For decades, scientists assumed that gravity would gradually slow down the expansion of the universe that began with the Big Bang. It seemed natural. Throw a ball upward, and gravity slows it down. The pull of all the matter in the universe should be slowing the expansion in the same way.

But when researchers observed distant exploding stars across cosmic time, they discovered the exact opposite. The expansion of our universe is not slowing down. It is speeding up. Something is actively pushing the fabric of spacetime apart, overcoming the mutual gravitational pull of trillions of galaxies.

Imagine tossing a ball into the air and watching it rise faster and faster, instead of slowing down and falling. You would immediately suspect that something unseen was pushing it. That is the feeling astronomers had when they looked at those distant explosions. Something unseen was pushing the entire universe.

To explain this relentless cosmic acceleration, scientists introduced the concept of dark energy. Dark energy makes up roughly 68 percent of everything that exists. It is the largest piece of the cosmic pie, bigger than ordinary matter and dark matter combined.

Unlike matter, which clumps together into stars and planets, dark energy appears to be smoothly woven into the very structure of empty space itself. It does not gather in lumps. It is spread evenly through the space between the galaxies, and through the space inside this room.

As the universe expands and creates more empty space, more of this mysterious energy emerges, accelerating the cosmic expansion even faster. This is a strange thought. Space is not merely a container. New space brings new dark energy with it, so the push never runs out.

It represents the dominant force of our universe, yet its true nature remains one of the deepest mysteries in all of science. We can measure its effect. We can say how much of it there seems to be. But what it actually is remains unknown.

Before we put it all together, it helps to pause and ask what kind of knowledge this is. When we say dark matter, we are not describing something we have captured in a laboratory. We are describing a gap. On one side, we have careful measurements of how galaxies spin and how the universe expands. On the other side, we have the visible matter we can count. The two do not agree, and dark matter is the name given to the difference.

That distinction matters. A name is not an explanation. Imagine a detective who finds footprints in the snow but never sees the person who made them. The detective can say the walker was heavy, or moving fast, or heading north. But the detective cannot say who it was. Dark matter is like those footprints. We read its effect on the stars, and we reason about what could have left such a mark.

The same honesty applies to dark energy. The measurements say the expansion is speeding up. The name dark energy is a label for whatever is responsible. Scientists describe it as smoothly woven into space itself, and that is a description of how it behaves, not an answer to what it is. Two enormous labels, then, sit on top of two enormous questions.

It is also worth noticing how different these two mysteries feel. Dark matter acts like a glue. It gathers around galaxies, it pulls inward, and it helps hold spinning wheels of stars together. Dark energy acts in the opposite way. It does not gather anywhere. It pushes outward across the whole universe, and it grows more important as space grows larger. One holds things together. The other pushes everything apart.

Think about what that means for the story of the cosmos. A universe with only ordinary matter would be a much simpler place to understand. Everything we could see would be everything there was. Instead, the universe turned out to have a hidden side, and that hidden side is not a small footnote. It is the majority of the total.

There is a lesson here about how we look at the world. For most of human history, the visible sky was the whole sky. Every ancient observer who looked up saw stars and assumed that stars were the substance of the heavens. It took careful measurement, patient comparison, and a willingness to be surprised to learn that the bright points of light were only the surface of something deeper.

Now consider how modest the clues were. A star turning a little too fast. A distant explosion looking a little too faint. None of these observations was dramatic to the naked eye. They were numbers in a notebook, and the numbers refused to agree with expectations. Yet when astronomers trusted the numbers instead of the expectations, an entire hidden universe came into view.

Here is another way to hold the idea in your mind. Picture the surface of the ocean on a stormy night. Waves rise, foam gleams white, and the foam is all you can see in the dark. If you only studied the foam, you might build a detailed theory of foam. But the foam rides on water, and the water is vastly larger than the foam. Our stars and galaxies are the bright foam. The dark ocean beneath is where most of the universe lives.

So what should we feel about all of this? Perhaps not fear, and not confusion, but a quiet kind of excitement. A mystery of this size means there is still something enormous left to discover. Every generation of scientists hopes to be the one that finally learns what dark matter is made of, and what dark energy truly does. We do not yet know whether the answer will be a new particle, a new idea, or something no one has imagined.

Until then, the honest summary is simple. We know how much seems to be there. We know where dark matter seems to gather. We know that the expansion is speeding up. And we do not know what either of the dark components really is. That is not a failure of science. It is the place where science is doing its most interesting work.

Put these pieces together, and the complete picture of our reality becomes astonishingly humbling. Normal matter makes up roughly 5 percent. Dark matter claims about 27 percent. And dark energy commands an overwhelming 68 percent. Together, dark matter and dark energy make up roughly 95 to 96 percent of the total cosmic pie.

Imagine walking into an enormous library containing millions of books, only to realize that you can read merely five pages in a single volume, while the rest are written in an invisible ink. You would not say you understood the library. You would say you had barely begun. That is where we stand with the universe.

And yet, this is not a story of defeat. Think about what it took to find these clues. Astronomers noticed that stars at the edge of galaxies moved too fast. They noticed that distant exploding stars revealed a speeding expansion. They followed small oddities in the data, and those oddities pointed to something enormous.

Every great discovery in astronomy begins with a clue that does not fit. A star moving too quickly. A galaxy that should have flown apart. Science does not shrink from such surprises. It follows them, and in doing so it learns the size of its own ignorance.

So when you step outside tonight and look up at the night sky, take a quiet breath and look past the glowing pinpricks of light. The stars and galaxies are not the main show. They are merely tiny specks of bright foam floating on top of a vast, unseen ocean.

We are conscious creatures made of a rare, luminous minority, striving to decipher the dark sea that cradles us. Perhaps the most remarkable thing about the universe is that a small, bright minority of it has learned to ask what the rest is.

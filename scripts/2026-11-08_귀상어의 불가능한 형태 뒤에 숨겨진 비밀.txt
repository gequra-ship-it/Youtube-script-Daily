=== 한국어 요약 (전체 번역이 아닌 요약본) ===
■ 영상 제목: The Secret Behind the Hammerhead's Impossible Shape (귀상어의 불가능한 형태 뒤에 숨겨진 비밀)

■ 한 줄 요약: 귀상어의 넓고 납작한 머리 구조인 '세팔로포일'은 시야 확장, 전자기장 탐지, 입체 후각, 유체역학적 기동성, 사냥 무기 역할을 동시에 수행하는 진화적 도구이지만, 그 구조로 인해 현대 어업 장비에 취약해져 심각한 멸종 위기에 처해 있다.

■ 전체 흐름 (대본 순서대로 8~15줄):
- 전통적인 유선형 상어 형태와 완전히 다른 기이한 옆으로 퍼진 머리를 지닌 귀상어의 외형적 특징을 소개한다.
- 약 4억 년간 유지된 기존 형태를 깨고 등장한 귀상어의 머리 구조 '세팔로포일(cephalofoil)'이 둔해 보인다는 통념과 달리 뛰어난 기동성을 보임을 설명한다.
- 양 끝에 위치한 눈을 통해 확보한 360도 파노라마 시야와 양안시 중첩을 통한 깊이 인식, 그리고 주둥이 바로 앞의 작은 사각지대라는 특징을 다룬다.
- 머리 표면에 넓게 분산된 로렌치니 기관(ampullae of Lorenzini)이 모래 속에 숨은 생물의 미세 전류를 탐색하는 금속탐지기 역할을 함을 보여준다.
- 모래 속에 묻혀 있는 가오리의 심장 박동 전류를 감지해 찾아내는 특화된 사냥 방식을 설명한다.
- 좌우 콧구멍의 간격을 통해 냄새가 닿는 미세한 시간차로 방향을 계산하는 입체 후각(stereoscopic smell)의 원리를 다룬다.
- 항공기 카나드(canard)처럼 작동하는 머리가 유체역학적 양력을 형성해 비행기처럼 급격한 회전과 기동을 가능하게 함을 밝힌다.
- 머리의 평평한 아랫면으로 가오리를 해저 바닥에 핀다운(pin down)해 독침을 무력화하며 머리와 턱에 다수의 독침이 박혀도 견디는 저항력을 설명한다.
- 과거의 점진적 진화 가설과 달리, 분자유전학 분석 결과 날개귀상어(winghead shark)처럼 가장 넓은 머리를 가진 종이 먼저 분기했다는 유전학적 반전을 소개한다.
- 다양한 환경에 맞춰 머리 형태를 조절하며 번성해 온 여러 귀상어 종들의 다양성을 설명한다.
- 넓은 머리로 인해 자망과 연승 등 상업적 어구에 쉽게 걸리고 포획 스트레스로 인한 높은 폐사율 및 샥스핀 수요로 개체수가 급감한 위기 상황을 짚는다.
- 인간의 미적 편견과 달리 생존을 위해 정교하게 설계된 귀상어 형태의 생물학적 의미를 되새긴다.

■ 핵심 사실·숫자 (5~10개):
- 전통적인 상어 형태는 약 4억 년(nearly four hundred million years) 동안 바다를 지배해 왔다.
- 귀상어의 옆으로 확장된 머리 구조의 공식 명칭은 '세팔로포일(cephalofoil)'이다.
- 양 끝의 눈 덕분에 거의 360도(three-hundred-and-sixty-degree)에 달하는 시야를 확보하지만, 코 바로 앞 중앙에 작은 사각지대가 존재한다.
- 주둥이에 젤로 채워진 미세한 감각 구멍인 '로렌치니 기관(ampullae of Lorenzini)'을 넓게 분산 배치하여 모래 밑 전류를 탐지한다.
- 좌우 콧구멍이 멀리 떨어져 있어 냄새가 도달하는 시간차를 인식하는 '입체 후각(stereoscopic smelling)'을 사용한다.
- 큰귀상어(great hammerhead)는 몸길이가 5미터(over five meters) 이상까지 자란다.
- 분자유전학 분석 결과, 머리가 가장 극단적으로 넓은 날개귀상어(winghead shark) 계통이 귀상어과에서 가장 초기에 분기한 것으로 나타났다.
- 귀상어는 높은 신진대사율을 가지고 있어 포획 시 극심한 생리적 스트레스(capture stress)로 인해 탈진하여 사망하는 경우가 많다.

■ 숏츠 5편:
- 숏츠 1: The Shark with Built-In Panoramic Vision (파노라마 시야를 탑재한 상어) — 세팔로포일 양 끝에 위치한 눈 덕분에 귀상어는 거의 360도에 달하는 넓은 시야와 정밀한 깊이 인식을 동시에 갖추게 되었습니다. 그러나 눈 사이 거리가 너무 먼 탓에 코 바로 앞 중앙에 작은 사각지대가 생겨 작은 물고기가 숨는 틈새가 발생하기도 합니다. 이 특이한 광학 구조는 3차원 바다 환경에서 먹잇감과 위협을 놓치지 않는 탁월한 탐색 도구로 작동합니다.
- 숏츠 2: Nature's Ultimate Living Metal Detector (자연 최고의 살아있는 금속탐지기) — 귀상어는 모래 밑에 숨은 가오리처럼 눈에 보이지 않는 먹잇감을 찾기 위해 미세 생체 전류를 감지하는 로렌치니 기관을 활용합니다. 일반 상어와 달리 이 감각 구멍들이 넓고 평평한 머리 전체에 퍼져 있어 훨씬 넓은 면적을 한 번에 스캔하는 대형 금속탐지기 역할을 합니다. 이 덕분에 모래 속에 숨겨진 먹잇감의 미세한 심장 박동 신호까지 정확히 포착해 기습할 수 있습니다.
- 숏츠 3: How the Hammerhead Pins Down Deadly Prey (귀상어가 치명적인 먹잇감을 제압하는 법) — 귀상어의 망치 머리는 탐색 센서일 뿐만 아니라 위험한 먹잇감을 물리적으로 억누르는 전투 무기로도 쓰입니다. 독침을 지닌 가오리를 사냥할 때, 귀상어는 넓고 평평한 머리 밑면으로 가오리를 해저 바닥에 강하게 눌러 독침 공격을 원천 차단합니다. 성체 귀상어의 턱과 머리에는 가오리 독침이 수십 개씩 박혀 있기도 하지만 독에 대한 강한 저항력으로 이를 견뎌냅니다.
- 숏츠 4: The Genetic Plot Twist Behind the Hammer (망치 머리 뒤에 숨겨진 유전학적 반전) — 과학자들은 귀상어가 일반 상어 형태에서 작은 돌기를 거쳐 점진적으로 넓은 머리로 진화했을 것이라 오랫동안 추측했습니다. 그러나 모든 귀상어 종의 DNA를 분석한 결과, 가장 머리가 거대한 날개귀상어가 가계도에서 가장 먼저 분기한 것으로 밝혀졌습니다. 즉, 진화의 시작점에서 거대한 머리가 먼저 급격히 나타났으며, 이후 일부 종이 서식지에 맞춰 머리 크기를 줄여나간 것입니다.
- 숏츠 5: Why the Ocean's Greatest Wing Is in Danger (바다 최고의 날개가 위기에 처한 이유) — 수천만 년 동안 귀상어를 바다의 최상위 포식자로 만들어 준 넓은 세팔로포일은 현대 어업 환경에서 치명적인 약점이 되었습니다. 넓은 머리 때문에 상업용 자망과 어구에 쉽게 걸리며, 빠른 신진대사로 인해 잡히자마자 극심한 포획 스트레스로 쉽게 탈진해 폐사합니다. 샥스핀 수요와 혼획으로 인해 위대한 진화적 걸작인 귀상어의 개체수가 전 세계적으로 급감하고 있습니다.

[영상 제목]: The Secret Behind the Hammerhead's Impossible Shape

=== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 ===

Have you ever picked up a tool from your workbench and wondered why it was given that exact form? A hammer balances weight at its very tip, concentrated for a singular purpose. We design our machines with sleek contours and streamlined curves, believing that simplicity is the purest sign of efficiency. If you were asked to design the ultimate ocean predator, you would probably sketch a smooth torpedo with narrow fins and a sharp snout. Nature spent hundreds of millions of years perfecting that very silhouette in sharks. Yet, swimming along the sunlit reefs and open currents today is an animal that completely defies that logic.

Its head is not pointed, nor is it sleek in any ordinary sense. Instead, it stretches out sideways into a broad, flattened wing, placing its eyes at opposite extremes like an impossible biological sculpture. When human sailors first pulled these creatures from the waves centuries ago, they could hardly believe what they were seeing. Why would millions of years of natural selection produce something so bizarre? Was this strange architecture an evolutionary mistake, or is it one of the most brilliant engineering solutions the living world has ever produced? To uncover the truth, we have to look beneath the surface.

For nearly four hundred million years, standard sharks dominated the seas with roughly the same hydrodynamic blueprint. They were built for cutting through the water column with minimal resistance, chasing prey with straightforward speed. But then, relatively recently in the vast timeline of life, a new branch of the shark family took an astonishing gamble. They abandoned the traditional conical head and began flattening their skulls into an extraordinary structure called the cephalofoil. To our eyes, pushing a wide horizontal bar through dense seawater looks clumsy. It looks as though it would drag the animal backward and waste precious energy on every single stroke.

Yet when you watch a hammerhead swim, there is no clumsiness at all. It maneuvers with a level of agility that leaves conventional sharks far behind. It can pivot in an instant, twisting its body at angles that seem to defy the physics of water. For generations, marine biologists argued over what single advantage could possibly justify such a radical redesign of the skull. Was it designed for swimming, for hunting, or for seeing? As modern technology finally allowed researchers to look inside this unusual skull, the answer became clear. The hammer was not built for just one function. It is a biological Swiss Army knife.

Consider your own vision for a moment. Your eyes face forward, giving you a sharp sense of depth, but leaving you blind to everything happening directly behind your shoulders. Prey animals, like horses or rabbits, have eyes placed on the sides of their heads, granting them an expansive panorama but poor depth perception. What if an animal could have both? By positioning its eyes at the distant tips of that wide cephalofoil, the hammerhead achieves something truly extraordinary. Each eye sweeps across a vast hemisphere of ocean, granting the shark an almost uninterrupted three-hundred-and-sixty-degree view of its world.

As the shark gently tilts its head from side to side while swimming, its binocular overlap expands dramatically. This allows it to gauge depth with remarkable precision, tracking small, darting fish against the endless blue. But this panoramic view comes with a curious trade-off. Because the eyes are pushed so far apart, there is a small blind spot directly in front of the shark's nose. Divers have occasionally noticed that if a tiny fish swims right up to the center of a hammerhead's snout, the predator might completely lose track of it. Yet in the open sea, where threats and meals come from every direction, that panoramic vision is an overwhelming advantage.

Vision, however, is only the beginning of what this skull can do. Water is often murky, dark, and filled with suspended sand, rendering eyesight useless. When sight fails, sharks turn to an invisible world of electric currents. All living creatures generate faint electrical fields whenever their muscles contract or their hearts beat. Sharks detect these whispers of electricity through tiny, gel-filled pores scattered across their snouts, known as the ampullae of Lorenzini. In a typical shark, these sensory pores are clustered tightly over a pointed nose, like a compact sensor cone.

In the hammerhead, that sensory array has been spread out across a wide, flat wing. Imagine searching a vast sandy beach with a handheld metal detector that covers only a few inches at a time. It would take hours of painstaking steps to find anything hidden beneath the surface. Now imagine holding a sensor bar that stretches five times wider across the ground. With every forward step, you sweep a massive swath of territory, catching faint signals that a narrower detector would miss entirely. That wide head acts as a broad-spectrum metal detector scanning the ocean floor.

This broad sensor array is designed for a very specific hunting strategy. One of the hammerhead's favorite meals is the stingray, a creature that spends its days buried underneath a blanket of sand, practically invisible to the naked eye. As the hammerhead glides inches above the seafloor, sweeping its head in gentle, rhythmic arcs, its electrical sensors register the hidden stingray's heartbeat. Even if the ray is completely buried and motionless, it cannot hide the electrical pulse of its life. The hammerhead detects the subtle signature, halts in mid-water, and strikes with sudden precision.

Smell also operates on a level of sophistication that few other predators can match. If you look closely at the leading edge of the hammer, you will find the shark's nostrils spaced far apart at the outer edges of the head. When a faint plume of blood or scent drifts through the current, it rarely hits both nostrils at the exact same millisecond. The tiny delay between the scent arriving at the left nostril and the right nostril tells the shark's brain precisely which way to turn. It is stereoscopic smelling, a directional compass built directly into the geometry of the head.

Then there is the sheer mechanics of movement. Water is hundreds of times denser than air, meaning every shape moving through it must constantly balance lift and drag. Aircraft engineers often place small auxiliary wings, known as canards, near the nose of an airplane to provide extra control during high-speed maneuvers. The cephalofoil acts much like those aircraft canards. When the shark tilts its head upward or downward, the flattened wing generates hydrodynamic lift, allowing the animal to climb or dive at breathtaking speeds without relying solely on its pectoral fins.

This dynamic lift allows for astonishingly tight turning radiuses. While a great white shark requires a wide arc to turn around, a hammerhead can whip its body around almost on its own axis. When hunting evasive fish that dart through coral reefs or jagged rock formations, this extreme maneuverability makes the hammerhead an inescapable shadow. The prey turns sharp left, and the shark rolls and mirrors the movement instantly, riding the hydrodynamic control generated by its own skull. What looked at first glance like an awkward obstacle turns out to be a high-performance wing.

Yet the hammer is not merely an instrument of perception and flight. It is also an active weapon. Stingrays do not give up without a fight; they possess serrated, venomous barbs at the base of their tails, capable of inflicting severe wounds. When a hammerhead locates a stingray, it does not simply bite recklessly. Instead, researchers have observed the shark using the flat underside of its head to physically pin the thrashing stingray against the sandy seafloor. By using its head as a biological shield, the shark immobilizes the dangerous barb until the ray is exhausted and defenseless.

Over time, mature hammerheads often accumulate dozens of broken stingray barbs embedded inside their jaws, snouts, and cephalofoils. Their bodies seem remarkably resistant to the venom, treating these defensive weapons as little more than minor splinters. The broad skull acts as a specialized restraint tool, tailored specifically to handle dangerous prey that other predators might hesitate to confront. From sensing to maneuvering, and finally to subduing, every single millimeter of this unique anatomical structure serves an undeniable purpose in the daily drama of survival.

How did such a dramatic shape come into existence in the first place? For many decades, scientists assumed that the evolution of the hammerhead followed a slow, cautious path. It seemed logical to guess that a normal shark ancestor developed a slight widening across its snout, which gradually expanded over millions of generations. Under this traditional view, species with modest, shovel-like heads, such as the bonnethead shark, were thought to be the primitive ancestors, while species with enormous, outstretched wings represented the final, modern evolutionary peak.

It was a neat, comforting story of steady progress from small to large. But when molecular geneticists began examining the DNA of different hammerhead species, the results shattered that long-held assumption. When the evolutionary tree was reconstructed using genetic evidence, scientists found that the species with the widest, most extreme heads, such as the winghead shark, actually branched off earliest in the family lineage. The smaller, more subtle hammer shapes did not come first. Instead, they appeared much later in the evolutionary timeline.

This unexpected discovery suggested that the hammerhead family may not have crept slowly toward a wider head. Instead, nature seems to have made a bold evolutionary leap right near the beginning of their history. The ancestors of modern hammerheads likely developed a dramatic, fully formed cephalofoil relatively quickly, unlocking a brand-new way to perceive and navigate the marine world. Later in their evolutionary story, certain lineages may have actually downsized their hammers to fit different environmental niches, trading extreme sensor width for compact agility in shallower habitats.

Today, there are multiple distinct species of hammerheads swimming in our oceans, ranging from the modest bonnethead to the magnificent great hammerhead, which can grow over five meters in length. Each species has fine-tuned the shape, curve, and proportions of its cephalofoil to match its specific diet and habitat. Some hunt crabs in shallow seagrass beds, while others patrol vast oceanic islands, gathering in massive schools of hundreds of individuals beneath underwater volcanic peaks. Their shape is not a bizarre quirk of biology; it is a versatile foundation that allowed an entire family to flourish across the globe.

Yet, this remarkable specialization has also left them extraordinarily vulnerable in the modern ocean. The very feature that made them masters of the marine world makes them easy targets for commercial fishing gear. Their wide heads become entangled in gillnets and longlines far more easily than the streamlined bodies of traditional sharks. Furthermore, hammerheads possess a highly active metabolism and experience severe physiological stress when captured, often dying from exhaustion before they can even be released back into the water.

Across the globe, populations of several hammerhead species have suffered drastic declines over the past few decades. The demand for shark fins and the relentless pressure of industrial fishing have pushed these ancient architects to the brink of survival. It is a sobering paradox of our time. An anatomical masterpiece that took tens of millions of years of natural experimentation to perfect can be undone in a matter of decades by human activity. When a species with such specialized adaptations vanishes, an entire sensory way of experiencing the ocean vanishes with it.

The next time you look at the natural world, consider how often our first assumptions lead us astray. When we see something unusual, our instinct is often to judge it as strange, awkward, or impractical. We expect the world to conform to familiar patterns, whether in the machines we build or the animals we observe. But nature does not design for our aesthetic comfort; it designs for survival. What looks like an eccentric detour is often a brilliant leap of biological imagination, solving problems we had not even learned to identify.

Far beneath the crashing waves, in the dim blue light where human eyes cannot see, the hammerhead shark continues its silent patrol. Its wide wings tilt against the current, mapping the subtle electrical pulses and shifting scents of the open water. It is neither clumsy nor mistaken, but a creature built with breathtaking precision for an unseen realm. In a universe governed by steady physical laws, life constantly finds the most unexpected paths forward, reminding us that true ingenuity often hides inside the shapes we least understand.

[숏츠 1] 제목: The Shark with Built-In Panoramic Vision

If you had to invent the ultimate ocean hunter, would you ever put its eyes on opposite ends of a giant hammer? It sounds ridiculous, yet nature actually did this. By stretching its head sideways into a wide structure called a cephalofoil, the hammerhead shark completely transformed how a predator sees the world. Your human eyes point straight ahead, giving you great depth perception but leaving you blind to everything behind your back. Most fish have eyes on the sides, giving them a broad view but terrible depth awareness. The hammerhead managed to achieve both at the exact same time.

Because its eyes are separated by an extraordinary distance, each eye takes in an enormous field of vision. When the shark swims and gently sweeps its head from side to side, the fields of view overlap, creating binocular vision with sharp depth perception across an almost three-hundred-and-sixty-degree panorama. It can monitor prey swimming above, beneath, and behind it without turning its entire body. 

However, this wild optical setup comes with one hilarious flaw. Because the eyes are pushed so far outward, there is a tiny blind spot directly in front of the shark's nose. Marine biologists have watched small fish swim straight up to the center of a hammerhead's snout, effectively hiding in plain sight because the shark literally cannot see what is an inch before its face. 

Yet in the vast open water, where food darts across three-dimensional space, having a near-complete panoramic sphere of vision is a superpower that few other creatures possess. This bizarre head is far from an evolutionary mistake; it is an optical radar scanning every corner of the blue. But panoramic vision is only the first trick this incredible head has to offer. [숏츠 2] 제목: Nature's Ultimate Living Metal Detector

Did you know that underneath the ocean floor, thousands of creatures are hiding completely invisible to the eye? Stingrays, for instance, bury themselves under thick layers of sand to avoid predators. If you were a normal shark relying on your eyesight, you would swim right past them without noticing a thing. But the hammerhead shark hunts with a tool that works like a biological metal detector. 

All living animals produce faint electrical currents every time their hearts beat or their muscles move. Sharks detect these invisible signals using microscopic, gel-filled pores called the ampullae of Lorenzini. While traditional sharks have these pores clustered over a pointed snout, the hammerhead spreads them out across the entire width of its massive, flattened head. 

Imagine sweeping a sandy beach for lost coins using a sensor that is only two inches wide. You would have to walk back and forth for hours to cover a tiny patch of ground. Now imagine holding a scanner that stretches five times wider with every single step. That is exactly what the hammerhead does. It glides inches above the seabed, swinging its wide sensory wing in rhythmic arcs. 

Even if a stingray is entirely buried under the sediment, it cannot stop its heart from beating. The hammerhead picks up that faint electrical whisper, locks onto the exact coordinates, and attacks without hesitation. The wide head is not awkward; it is an industrial-grade sensory array scanning the abyss. [숏츠 3] 제목: How the Hammerhead Pins Down Deadly Prey

Why does a hammerhead shark have a head shaped like a literal hammer? As it turns out, it does not just look like a tool. The shark actually uses it as one. When hunting dangerous prey in the wild, having sharp teeth is rarely enough. One of the hammerhead's absolute favorite meals is the stingray, an animal armed with a venomous, serrated barb capable of piercing thick flesh and delivering agonizing poison. 

Most predators hesitate to attack a cornered stingray from above because a single thrash of its tail can cause fatal injuries. But the hammerhead has developed a brilliant combat tactic. Once it locates a hidden ray, it does not rush in to bite. Instead, it swims over the prey and uses the broad, flat underside of its head to forcefully slam the stingray against the ocean floor. 

By pinning the ray against the sand with its heavy cephalofoil, the shark completely neutralizes the dangerous tail. It holds the thrashing animal trapped under its skull until the ray exhausts itself and can no longer fight back. Biologists frequently find adult hammerheads with dozens of broken stingray spines embedded inside their jaws, snouts, and heads. Remarkably, their bodies show an astonishing resistance to the venom, treating these deadly barbs like minor splinters. 

The hammerhead's skull is not just a sensor; it is a heavy-duty biological shield and restraint system designed for gladiatorial combat beneath the waves. [숏츠 4] 제목: The Genetic Plot Twist Behind the Hammer

For nearly a century, scientists believed they had the hammerhead shark completely figured out. If you look at the different species living today, there is a clear range of head shapes. The bonnethead shark has a modest, shovel-shaped head that is barely wider than its body. On the other end of the spectrum, the winghead shark features a massive, outstretched head nearly half the length of its entire body. 

Naturally, researchers assumed evolution worked gradually. The prevailing theory was that an ordinary shark ancestor developed a small bump on its snout, which slowly expanded over millions of years, moving from the bonnethead all the way to the giant winghead. It was a neat, orderly story of steady evolutionary progress. 

Then came the modern era of molecular genetics. When scientists extracted DNA from all nine hammerhead species and reconstructed their family tree, the entire theory collapsed. The genetic data revealed that the species with the most extreme, enormous hammers, like the winghead shark, actually branched off first. The earliest hammerheads did not start with tiny bumps; they burst into history with massive, fully developed wings. 

The smaller, subtle hammer shapes did not appear at the beginning. They evolved much later, as certain species shrank their hammers back down to fit shallow seagrass beds and specialized diets. Nature did not take small, cautious steps toward the hammer. It made a bold, radical leap right at the start. [숏츠 5] 제목: Why the Ocean's Greatest Wing Is in Danger

The hammerhead shark is one of the greatest engineering triumphs in evolutionary history. Its wide head provides three-hundred-and-sixty-degree vision, stereoscopic smelling, a massive electromagnetic sensor array, and the ability to turn on a dime like a fighter jet. For tens of millions of years, this radical body plan made the hammerhead an undisputed master of the open ocean. 

Yet today, that very same biological masterpiece has become the animal's greatest curse. The wide cephalofoil that senses hidden prey across vast stretches of sand easily snags on commercial fishing gear. While sleek, streamlined sharks might slip through the gaps of industrial gillnets, a hammerhead's wide wings become hopelessly entangled almost immediately. 

To make matters worse, hammerheads have an exceptionally high metabolism and suffer intense physiological shock when trapped. While some shark species can survive for hours on a hook or line, hammerheads experience extreme capture stress, often dying from exhaustion before fishermen can pull them to the surface. 

Driven by the international trade in shark fins and the pressures of modern commercial fishing, populations of great and scalloped hammerheads have plummeted drastically across the globe. An extraordinary anatomical marvel that took nature millions of years to craft is disappearing within a single human lifetime. When we lose a predator this specialized, we lose an entirely unique way of experiencing and understanding the marine world.

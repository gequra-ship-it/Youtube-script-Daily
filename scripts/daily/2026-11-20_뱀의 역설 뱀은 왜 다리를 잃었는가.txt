=== 한국어 요약 (전체 번역이 아닌 요약본) ===
■ 영상 제목: The Serpent's Paradox: Why Snakes Lost Their Legs (뱀의 역설: 뱀은 왜 다리를 잃었는가)

■ 한 줄 요약: 뱀은 좁은 지하 환경에 적응하기 위해 다리와 장기를 극단적으로 재배치하며 진화했고, 이후 도구를 쓰는 직립 인류의 위협에 대응해 안구를 정밀 타격하는 장거리 독 분사 능력을 발달시켰다.

■ 전체 흐름 (대본 순서대로 8~15줄):
- 백악기 화석과 현대 비단뱀류의 골반 박차(pelvic spurs) 흔적은 뱀의 조상에게 다리가 있었음을 증명한다.
- 뱀은 다리 유전자를 완전히 잃은 것이 아니라, 소닉 헤지호그(Sonic hedgehog) 유전자 조절 부위 변이로 사지 성장이 멈추고 혹스(Hox) 유전자에 의해 척추가 증식했다.
- 초기 조상 뱀은 좁은 지하 터널과 설치류 굴을 드나들기 위해 걸림돌이 되던 네 다리를 퇴화시켰다.
- 좁은 원통형 체형에 맞추기 위해 왼쪽 폐가 퇴화하여 단일 폐가 되었고, 신장과 간 등 짝 장기들이 전후 일렬로 재배치되었다.
- 외이와 고막을 없애는 대신 아래턱뼈를 지면에 밀착해 미세 진동을 감지하는 골전도 청각 체계를 구축했다.
- 다리가 없어 지상 포식자에게 취약해지자, 조상 파충류의 소화 효소 침을 신경독과 출혈독 등 치명적인 화학 무기(독액)로 진화시켰다.
- 코브라과(Elapidae)는 50밀리초(millisecond) 만에 먹잇감의 혈관에 독을 주입하는 고정형 독니 주사관을 개발했다.
- 사냥용으로는 쓸모없는 독 분사 코브라는 독니 배출구가 전면 90도 각도로 꺾인 연료 분사 노즐 형태를 갖추고 목표물의 눈을 정밀 조준해 독을 뿜는다.
- 약 700만 년 전 아프리카에서 두 발로 직립하여 돌과 막대를 던지는 초기 인류(오스트랄로피테쿠스, 호모 에렉투스 등)의 출현이 코브라의 원거리 방어 무기 진화를 촉발했다.
- 독 분사 능력은 인류의 확산 경로와 시기에 맞춰 아프리카 코브라, 아시아 코브라, 링할스에서 독립적으로 총 세 번 진화했다.
- 뱀의 다리 소실과 독 분사 메커니즘은 포식자, 먹잇감, 그리고 직립 인류 사이에서 벌어진 수백만 년간의 극단적인 생존 협상의 결과물이다.

■ 핵심 사실·숫자 (5~10개):
- 1억 년 전 백악기 뱀 화석인 나자시 리오네그리나(Najash rionegrina)는 기능하는 골반과 발목 관절, 2개의 뒷다리를 가지고 있었다.
- 현대 비단뱀이나 그린 아나콘다의 배설강 양옆에 있는 골반 박차(pelvic spurs) 밑에는 조상의 소형 대퇴골과 골반 뼈가 남아 있다.
- 다리 형성 조절 유전자인 소닉 헤지호그(Sonic hedgehog)의 스위치 돌연변이로 다리 성장이 멈췄고, 혹스(Hox) 유전자가 척추를 계속 늘렸다.
- 좁은 체형 적응으로 왼쪽 폐는 쓸모없는 주머니로 퇴화하고 확장된 오른쪽 폐 하나만 호흡을 담당한다.
- 코브라의 독니 공격은 50밀리초(millisecond) 만에 이루어져 자기 몸무게의 20배에 달하는 사냥감을 쓰러뜨릴 수 있다.
- 독 분사 코브라의 독니 배출구는 90도 각도로 전방을 향해 꺾여 있으며, 2~3미터 거리까지 나선형 안개 형태로 분사된다.
- 분사된 독액은 단백질 분자가 커서 온전한 피부나 털을 통과하지 못하므로 사냥용으로는 전혀 기능하지 못한다.
- 약 700만 년 전(seven million years ago) 아프리카에서 직립보행을 시작한 초기 인류와의 대치가 독 분사 코브라 진화의 배경으로 꼽힌다.
- 독 분사 능력은 분자 분석 결과 아프리카 코브라, 아시아 코브라, 남아프리카 링할스(ringhals)에서 독립적으로 총 3회 출현했다.

■ 숏츠 5편:
- 숏츠 1: The Secret Claws Hidden Inside Pythons (비단뱀 속에 숨겨진 비밀 발톱) — 현대 비단뱀과 아나콘다의 배설강 옆에는 과거 백악기 조상 화석(Najash rionegrina)처럼 다리가 있었음을 증명하는 골반 박차와 내부 대퇴골 흔적이 남아 있다. 뱀은 다리 유전자를 잃은 것이 아니라 소닉 헤지호그(Sonic hedgehog) 유전자의 조절 스위치가 돌연변이를 일으켜 사지 성장이 멈춘 것이다. 지하 굴에서 방해만 되던 다리를 해체하고 척추를 증식시켜 은밀한 이동에 최적화했다.
- 숏츠 2: Why Snakes Only Have One Lung (뱀의 폐가 하나뿐인 이유) — 좁은 튜브형 몸으로 지하 틈새를 누비기 위해 뱀은 대대적인 내부 장기 구조 조정을 거쳤다. 공간 부족으로 왼쪽 폐가 퇴화하여 하나의 긴 오른쪽 폐만 남았고, 신장과 간 같은 짝 장기들도 몸 안에 일렬로 직렬 배치되었다. 또한 귀와 고막을 없애는 대신 아래턱뼈를 지면에 대고 미세 진동을 읽는 감각 안테나로 탈바꿈시켰다.
- 숏츠 3: The Cobra Fang Is a Fuel Injector (연료 분사 노즐을 닮은 코브라의 독니) — 독 분사 코브라의 독니 배출구는 일반 독사와 달리 전방을 향해 90도로 꺾여 있으며 내부에 난류를 유도하는 미세 홈이 파여 있다. 관자놀이 근육이 독샘을 피스톤처럼 압박하면 2미터 이상 나선형 안개 형태로 고압 분사된다. 코브라는 무작위로 쏘지 않고 침입자의 움직이는 눈을 추적해 정확히 각막을 조준한다.
- 숏츠 4: The Venom That Cannot Kill Prey (사냥감을 죽이지 못하는 독) — 코브라가 분사하는 독액은 분자 크기가 커서 쥐의 털이나 피부를 뚫지 못해 사냥용으로는 전혀 쓸모가 없다. 막대한 대사 에너지가 드는 독액 분사는 포식자로부터 벗어나기 위한 순수한 원거리 방어 무기다. 눈의 점막에 닿는 즉시 각막 세포막을 찢어 극심한 통증과 실명을 일으켜 뱀이 도망칠 시간을 벌어준다.
- 숏츠 5: Why Upright Primates Created Spitting Cobras (직립 영장류가 독 분사 코브라를 만든 이유) — 700만 년 전 아프리카에서 두 발로 걷기 시작한 초기 인류는 도구와 돌을 던져 원거리에서 뱀의 머리를 박살 낼 수 있는 위협적인 존재였다. 짧은 타격 거리로는 돌을 던지는 유인원을 막을 수 없었기에 뱀은 거리를 두고 공격하는 화학 무기를 고안했다. 코브라의 독 분사 능력은 인류의 확산 시기와 일치하게 아프리카, 아시아, 링할스에서 각각 3번 독립적으로 진화했다.

[영상 제목]: The Serpent's Paradox: Why Snakes Lost Their Legs

=== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 ===

You walk down a crowded sidewalk every morning without giving your two legs a second thought. Yet the most successful predators on Earth conquered seven continents by throwing their limbs away entirely. It sounds like an evolutionary dead end, giving up four running legs to drag a belly through dirt and gravel. But losing limbs was not an accident or a genetic mistake. It was one of the most calculated engineering redesigns in the history of vertebrate life.

Over one hundred million years ago, during the warm greenhouse climate of the Cretaceous period, reptiles were experimenting with radical body plans. The world was dominated by lumbering four-legged dinosaurs, yet buried fossil beds in Argentina and the Mediterranean reveal ancient serpents that still possessed two tiny hind legs. Creatures like Najash rionegrina possessed a functioning pelvis and delicate ankle joints resting right above the tail. They were walking and crawling simultaneously, caught midway through an irreversible anatomical revolution.

You do not even need to dig into ancient limestone to witness this ancient history. Take a close look at a modern python or a green anaconda resting in a zoological enclosure. Just beside the cloacal vent beneath their muscular tails, you can see two hardened, claw-like structures known as pelvic spurs. These tiny claws are not ornamental scales. Beneath the skin, they connect to miniature ancestral femurs and pelvic bones that never fully disappeared. 

How does a creature dismantle four limbs without causing its entire body architecture to fall apart? The answer hides inside a fragile switch within vertebrate DNA. Scientists discovered that snakes did not lose the genetic code for building legs. Instead, a tiny regulatory region that controls a gene called Sonic hedgehog suffered subtle mutations over millions of years. This genetic switch simply failed to keep limb buds growing, while another set of developmental switches, the Hox genes, kept stacking vertebrae like beads on a necklace.

Why would any animal abandon the speed and stability of four running feet? For decades, paleontologists debated two competing theories, arguing between an oceanic origin and an underground nursery. Today, scientists suspect early ancestral snakes slipped beneath the earth into damp leaf litter and rodent burrows. When you try to squeeze through tight, subterranean root networks, limbs do not push you forward. They catch on branches, snag against damp soil, and act as anchors that drag you down.

Living life inside a narrow underground tunnel demanded an immediate, ruthless internal renovation. If you compress a wide ribcage into a flexible garden hose, where do your organs go? Evolution answered by tearing out the spare parts. The left lung in most modern snakes shrank until it became a useless vestigial pouch, leaving one elongated right lung to handle all respiration. Paired organs like the kidneys, gonads, and liver could no longer sit side by side, so nature stacked them in a single-file line. 

Losing limbs also meant giving up the luxury of external ears and delicate eardrums. In the dark soil, sound does not travel efficiently through air, but shockwaves ripple effortlessly through solid ground. The ancestral snake fused its lower jaw bones into high-sensitivity acoustic antennas, resting its chin directly on the dirt. Every creeping beetle, burrowing mole, and approaching predator sent microscopic tremors directly into the snake's inner ear bones. The world transformed from an auditory landscape of birdsong into a tactile map of subterranean vibrations.

By shedding their legs and slipping underground, snakes became masters of hidden spaces, but that transformation came with a terrifying price tag. Stripped of claws to slash and limbs to sprint away, a snake stranded on open ground was utterly defenseless against trampling hooves and sharp-eyed predators. To reclaim the surface world and hunt across daylight plains, they needed an equalizer that could stop a target instantly without a wrestling match. They needed chemical warfare.

Venom did not begin as an instant lethal cocktail designed to stop a beating heart. Long before modern vipers and cobras emerged, ancestral squamates produced saliva packed with digestive enzymes to lubricate rough meals. Over tens of millions of years, duplicated genes mutated these gentle digestive fluids into molecular shears. Harmless proteins transformed into neurotoxins that paralyze nerve synapses, and hemotoxins that liquefy blood vessels and turn muscular tissue into soup.

As chemical weaponry grew more lethal, nature engineered the delivery hardware to match. In the family of elapids, which includes modern mambas, kraits, and cobras, front teeth evolved into hollow hypodermic needles. These fixed fangs feature microscopic channels that guide pressurized venom straight into the bloodstream of a struggling prey animal. A lightning strike lasting fifty milliseconds could bring down an animal twenty times the snake's size, allowing the predator to swallow its prize at leisure.

For millions of years across dense forests and open grasslands, venom served one primary goal: hunting food. A quiet ambush, a single injection, and the serpent could wait patiently while its chemical weapon did all the dangerous work. The venom was expensive to brew, requiring vast amounts of metabolic energy and concentrated proteins. Wasting a single drop on anything other than a meal was a costly survival mistake that no wild predator could easily afford.

Yet if you travel to the arid scrublands of sub-Saharan Africa or the sun-baked forests of Southeast Asia, you meet a bizarre exception. Certain cobras will rise up, flare their wide ribs into a terrifying hood, and instead of lunging forward to sink their fangs, they shoot high-pressure venom straight into the empty air. A fine mist erupts across two or three meters of distance, sparkling under the harsh sunlight. Why would a master hunter spray its most precious, expensive weapon into the wind?

Look closely at the fang of a spitting cobra through an electron microscope, and the secret reveals itself. In non-spitting cobras, the venom exit orifice sits right near the tip, facing straight down like the opening of a standard medical syringe. In a spitting cobra, that exit aperture bends sharply forward at a ninety-degree angle, resembling the precision nozzle of a modern fuel injector. The interior duct is rounded and ridged to induce hydraulic turbulence, preventing the venom from dribbling out as clumsy droplets.

When an intruder steps too close, powerful temporal muscles clamp down over the venom glands like biological pistons. The pressurized liquid rushes through the fangs, vaporizing into twin jets of mist that weave an oscillating spiral pattern. Even more astonishingly, the snake does not fire blindly into the surrounding brush. As it lunges, the cobra actively tracks the moving face of its target, subtly tilting its head to blanket both eyes in a geometric spray of burning fluid.

Here is the central mystery that puzzled field biologists for generations: why bother developing a long-range squirt gun if the venom cannot bring down dinner? If a cobra spits venom onto the thick fur of a desert rat or the scales of a lizard, absolutely nothing happens. Venom proteins are far too large to penetrate intact mammalian skin or keratinous armor. A spitting cobra could spray a thousand liters of venom at a rodent, and the prey would simply groom itself and walk away unharmed.

Spitting venom is utterly useless for hunting prey. It serves exactly one purpose in the natural kingdom: long-range, preemptive defense against a dedicated attacker. But that realization creates an even deeper evolutionary riddle. What kind of predator justifies developing ballistic chemical defense? Birds of prey dive out of the sun at blinding speeds, while badgers and mongooses charge in low and fast. Neither threat can be deterred by a spray of venom aimed two meters off the ground.

Scientists suspect the true adversary that forced cobras to reinvent their fangs is the very ancestor looking back at us in the mirror. Around seven million years ago in Africa, early hominins separated from their ape relatives and stood permanently upright on two feet. For the first time in evolutionary history, a predatory mammal walked through tall savanna grass with an elevated line of sight and two completely free hands capable of using tools.

To an upright hominin armed with a heavy wooden branch or a sharp river stone, a crawling serpent was an accessible meal or a deadly hazard to eliminate quickly. Our ancestors did not need to wrestle a venomous snake with bare hands; they could stand three paces back and smash its skull with projectiles. Against a stone-throwing ape, a standard defensive strike that reaches only a few inches is a death sentence for the snake. The serpent needed a way to strike first, from a distance, without ever making physical contact.

Genetic timelines reveal a chilling synchronization that supports this hominin defense hypothesis. Modern molecular analysis shows that spitting venom evolved three separate times across cobra lineages: once in African spitting cobras, once in Asian spitting cobras, and independently in the South African ringhals. Intriguingly, each evolutionary burst matches the timing and geographic arrival of tool-using, bipedal hominins like Australopithecus and Homo erectus radiating across ancient continents.

When a blast of spitting cobra venom strikes the cornea of a large mammal, the effect is instantaneous and catastrophic. Unlike ordinary venom that must enter the bloodstream, spitting cobra venom contains hyper-specialized cytotoxins that rip through sensitive eye membranes within seconds. The result is blinding, searing pain, triggering uncontrollable tears and temporary or permanent blindness. The stone drops from the primate's hand, the hunting club clatters into the dust, and the legless serpent slips quietly away into the tall grass.

When you trace the grand arc of snake evolution, you realize that life is an ongoing negotiation of extreme trade-offs. The snake sacrificed four limbs, an entire lung, external ears, and traditional mobility to conquer the cracks and crevices of the earth. But when the surface world responded with intelligent, upright primates armed with rocks and sticks, the serpent did not evolve its legs back. Instead, it weaponized its saliva, turned its teeth into artillery nozzles, and learned to aim for the eyes.

As you sit in your living room today, looking down at your hands and resting your feet flat against the floor, you are feeling the echoes of that ancient standoff. We developed stereoscopic depth perception, upright posture, and throwing shoulders to survive the dangerous shadows of the ancient savanna. And in the grass below, a legless survivor shaped its body into a living bow, learning to shoot venom into the light to keep our ancestors at bay. 

Nature does not design a living creature toward a single vision of physical perfection. It carves away whatever is unnecessary, refines whatever remains, and answers every new threat with breathtaking, lethal ingenuity. The missing legs of the serpent and the blinding spray of the cobra are not strange anomalies of biology. They are enduring signatures of an ancient conversation between predator, prey, and our own ancestors, written in bone, muscle, and venom across millions of unremembered years.

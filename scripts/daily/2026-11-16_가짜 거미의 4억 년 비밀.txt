=== 한국어 요약 (전체 번역이 아닌 요약본) ===
■ 영상 제목: The 400-Million-Year Secret of the Fake Spider (가짜 거미의 4억 년 비밀)

■ 한 줄 요약: 거미로 흔히 오해받는 통발거미목(Opiliones, daddy longlegs) 생물이 독과 거미줄 없이도 독특한 신체 구조와 방어 기작으로 4억 년 동안 대멸종을 견뎌내며 생존해 온 비밀을 밝힌다.

■ 전체 흐름 (대본 순서대로 8~15줄):
- 가을 저녁 정원 벽에서 흔히 발견되며 거미로 오인받는 통발거미(harvestmen / daddy longlegs)를 소개한다.
- 세상에서 가장 치명적인 독을 지녔지만 이빨이 작아 못 문다는 유명한 도시전설의 실체를 밝힌다.
- 이들은 독이나 독니가 전혀 없으며 거미가 아닌 별개의 거미강 통발거미목(Opiliones)에 속함을 설명한다.
- 좁은 허리가 있는 거미와 달리 머리·가슴·배가 하나로 합쳐진 일체형 타원형 몸통 구조를 짚는다.
- 머리 중앙의 돌기 위에 위치한 2개의 잠망경 같은 눈 구조를 소개한다.
- 거미줄을 만드는 실젖이 없으며 협각(chelicerae)으로 고체 음식을 씹어 먹는 잡식성 청소부 역할을 설명한다.
- 길게 뻗은 두 번째 다리 쌍이 지팡이이자 후각·미각·촉각을 담당하는 감각 기관(일본명 Zatomushi)임을 밝힌다.
- 다리를 스스로 떼어내는 자절(autotomy)과 잘린 다리가 독립적으로 격렬히 움직여 포식자를 교란하는 전략을 설명한다.
- 성체는 다리가 재생되지 않지만 즉각 보행 방식을 재조정하며, 퀴논(quinones) 분비 및 의사행동(thanatosis) 등 다양한 방어책을 쓴다.
- 건조한 환경이나 추위에서 수천 마리가 뭉쳐 흔들리는 군집 형성(aggregation)의 생존 이유를 분석한다.
- 스코틀랜드 라이니 처트(Rhynie chert)에서 발견된 4억 년 전 데본기 초기 화석을 통해 이들의 오랜 기원을 조명한다.
- 페름기 대멸종(Great Dying)과 공룡 멸종 소행성 충돌 속에서도 변함없이 살아남은 조용한 생존력의 가치를 강조하며 마무리한다.

■ 핵심 사실·숫자 (5~10개):
- 통발거미목(Opiliones)은 거미목과 전혀 다른 목이며, 독샘과 독니가 전혀 없다.
- 거미와 달리 허리(pedicel)가 없이 머리, 가슴, 배가 단일 캡슐 형태로 융합되어 있다.
- 보통 6~8개의 눈을 가진 거미와 달리, 머리 중앙 융기된 돌기 위에 2개의 눈만 가지고 있다.
- 실젖(spinnerets)이 없어 거미줄을 만들지 못하며 집게형 협각(chelicerae)으로 고체 음식을 씹어 먹는 잡식성(omnivore)이다.
- 두 번째 다리 쌍은 앞을 감지하고 화학 분자를 맛보고 냄새 맡는 탐지 지팡이 역할을 한다.
- 자절(autotomy)로 떼어낸 다리는 내부 신경세포로 인해 몇 분 동안 뇌의 명령 없이도 스스로 펄쩍펄쩍 뛴다.
- 성체는 잃어버린 다리를 다시 재생할 수 없지만 남은 4~7개의 다리로 보행 알고리즘을 즉시 수정한다.
- 스코틀랜드 라이니 처트(Rhynie chert)에서 4억 년 이상 된(데본기 초기) 화석이 발견되었다.
- 지구 생명체의 90% 이상이 사라진 페름기 말 대멸종과 백악기 말 공룡 대멸종을 모두 겪고 살아남았다.

■ 숏츠 5편:
- 숏츠 1: The Deadliest Spider Myth Debunked (가장 치명적인 거미 신화의 진실) — 세상에서 가장 강한 독을 지녔다는 통발거미의 도시전설은 완전한 허구입니다. 이들은 독과 독니가 전혀 없으며 거미와 다른 통발거미목(Opiliones)에 속해 집게로 부식물을 씹어 먹는 무해한 정원 청소부입니다.
- 숏츠 2: Why Does A Severed Leg Keep Dancing? (잘린 다리가 계속 춤추는 이유는?) — 포식자에게 붙잡힌 통발거미는 자절 작용을 통해 스스로 다리를 떼어내고 즉시 도망칩니다. 잘려 나간 다리는 뇌 없이도 독자적인 신경 신호로 몇 분간 격렬히 뛰어 포식자의 시선을 완벽히 분산시킵니다.
- 숏츠 3: The Giant Pulsating Hairball In The Woods (숲속의 거대한 맥박 치는 털 뭉치) — 창고 처마 밑에서 꿈틀거리는 거대한 털 뭉치는 수천 마리의 통발거미가 엉켜 있는 군집 현상입니다. 이들은 수분 증발을 막고, 악취 물질을 뿜어내며, 거대한 괴물처럼 흔들리는 시각적 위협으로 포식자를 쫓아냅니다.
- 숏츠 4: It Looks Like A Spider, But Look Closer (거미처럼 보이지만 자세히 보면 다르다) — 겉보기엔 거미 같지만 허리가 없는 일체형 몸통과 중앙 돌기 위에 달린 2개의 잠망경 눈을 지녔습니다. 거미줄을 만드는 실젖이 전혀 없으며, 긴 두 번째 다리로 냄새와 맛을 느끼며 고체 음식을 씹어 먹습니다.
- 숏츠 5: The Fragile Creature That Outlived Dinosaurs (공룡보다 오래 살아남은 연약한 생물) — 4억 년 전 스코틀랜드 라이니 처트 화석에서 발견된 통발거미는 거미가 거미줄을 치기 전부터 지구에 살았습니다. 페름기 대멸종과 공룡을 멸종시킨 소행성 충돌을 모두 이겨내고 고대 형태 그대로 오늘날까지 생존해 있습니다.

=== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 ===

That quiet little creature on your garden wall is not a spider at all. Take a close look at the damp garden wall outside your window on an autumn evening. You might notice a tiny round speck suspended beneath eight ridiculously long, thread-like legs. When you step closer, it bobs up and down rhythmically, swaying in mid-air as if trembling with nervousness. Most people instantly step back and whisper a single word: spider. Everything you thought you knew about this familiar shadow is completely wrong.

For generations, countless backyards and campfires have hosted the exact same mistaken identity. People around the world call them harvestmen or daddy longlegs, pointing at them with a mixture of shudder and curiosity. You may have heard children whisper terrifying urban legends about them on school playgrounds. Perhaps someone once told you they possess the deadliest venom on earth, but their fangs are simply too small to pierce human skin. It is worth asking whether that chilling tale holds even a single grain of truth.

The truth is far more astonishing than any playground rumor. These gentle wanderers do not carry a drop of venom, and they possess no fangs whatsoever. In fact, calling them spiders is a biological case of mistaken identity that spans centuries. They belong to an entirely separate order of arachnids called Opiliones, walking their own evolutionary path for unimaginable eons. While true spiders were still learning how to weave silk, the ancestors of harvestmen were already treading silently across primeval continents. They are living time capsules, surviving catastrophes that wiped out legendary giants.

To understand how different they are from spiders, look closely at their anatomy. A true spider has an unmistakable hourglass silhouette, divided clearly into two separate body sections joined by a narrow waist. Behind that slender waist sits an abdomen packed with silk glands and venom sacs. Harvestmen, on the other hand, show no waist at all. Their head, thorax, and abdomen are fused together into a single, compact, pill-shaped capsule. When you look at one, you are looking at an unbroken, armored pebble suspended on silken stilts.

Their faces reveal an even stranger portrait when observed through a magnifying glass. Most true spiders possess six or eight gleaming eyes arranged in watchful rows across their foreheads. Harvestmen usually have just two eyes, perched atop a tiny raised turret in the very center of their head. Imagine walking through the world with a miniature lighthouse tower sitting right between your shoulders. These two little lenses point sideways, offering panoramic views of danger approaching from above and behind. Their world is not viewed through a sprawling battery of eyes, but through a modest dual-periscope.

Have you ever seen a harvestman catch a meal in a delicate orb web? You never will, because harvestmen are completely incapable of producing silk. They have no spinnerets on their bodies, and they never weave sticky snares to trap drifting moths. While spiders build elaborate architectural wonders and wait patiently for vibrations, harvestmen walk actively through the night. They do not inject digestive venom into their prey to drink it like a melted smoothie. Instead, they possess small, mechanical pincer-claws called chelicerae that actually chew solid food.

Because they can chew solid food, harvestmen enjoy an astonishingly varied and opportunistic menu. True spiders are strict predatory carnivores that only drink liquified insect insides. Harvestmen, by contrast, are enthusiastic omnivores that serve as the quiet cleanup crew of the forest floor. They eagerly consume aphids, snails, fallen fruit, rotting leaves, soft fungi, bird droppings, and decaying carrion. They walk through damp undergrowth like tiny robotic vacuum cleaners, tidying up organic debris that other creatures ignore. Without their constant nighttime scavenging, fallen woodland waste would linger far longer than it does.

If they have no venom, no fangs, and no sticky webs to hide behind, how do they navigate the darkness? The secret lies in those mesmerizing, impossibly delicate legs. Those legs are not just levers for walking; they are walking sensory antennae. In particular, the second pair of legs is significantly longer than the others, acting like the white cane of a blind wanderer. As the harvestman steps forward, it gently taps the ground ahead, feeling vibrations and tasting chemical molecules in the air. Their legs smell, taste, touch, and measure the environment all at once.

This remarkable walking equipment explains why the Japanese name for them is Zatomushi, a reference to an ancient, blind cane-tapping traveler. When you see a harvestman hovering across leaf litter, you are watching a creature feeling its way across a sensory map. Those thin legs contain dozens of flexible micro-joints near the tips, allowing them to wrap around pine needles like tiny fingers. Have you ever tried climbing a vertical sheet of smooth glass with your bare hands? A harvestman can grasp almost microscopic imperfections in bark, navigating twisted briars with effortless, weightless grace.

Yet walking on such slender stilts comes with immense vulnerability in a forest teeming with hungry predators. Birds, frogs, toads, and beetles constantly search the moss for an easy snack. How could a creature with no venom and soft bodies survive such relentless hunting pressure for millions of years? The answer is an evolutionary toolbox filled with bizarre and ingenious defensive tricks. When cornered, harvestmen do not fight back with lethal force; instead, they unleash psychological warfare, mechanical illusions, and chemical deterrence that leave predators thoroughly baffled.

The first line of defense is an extraordinary biological trick known as autotomy, or voluntary limb shedding. If an aggressive beetle grabs a harvestman by one of its spindly legs, the harvestman does not struggle desperately. Instead, a specialized muscle snaps a built-in breaking joint near the base of the limb. The harvestman casually walks away on its remaining seven legs, leaving the severed limb behind in the attacker's jaws. For many animals, losing a limb means fatal bleeding, but the harvestman's wound seals instantly with a microscopic internal valve.

The truly eerie part begins after the leg is completely detached from the body. That severed leg does not lie motionless; it begins to rhythmically twitch, kick, and jump violently for several minutes. A dedicated cluster of nerve cells inside the severed limb continues firing autonomous electrical pulses without any brain input. To a confused predator, that violently dancing leg looks like a fighting, nutritious meal demanding immediate attention. While the attacker wrestles with an empty, kicking stick, the harvestman quietly vanishes into the safety of the dark leaf litter.

Can harvestmen grow those lost legs back like a salamander regrowing its tail? Once they reach full adulthood, the answer is unfortunately no; a lost limb is gone forever. Yet these resourceful survivors manage remarkably well on six, five, or even four remaining legs. Researchers often find elderly harvestmen hobbling contentedly through the woods with missing limbs, still successfully finding food and mates. Their nervous system instantly recalibrates its walking algorithm the moment a leg snaps off. They adapt their gait in fractions of a second, proving their resilience under pressure.

If physical sacrifice fails, harvestmen can turn themselves into miniature chemical weapons factories. On the sides of their bodies, right near the base of their front legs, sit specialized scent glands. When threatened, they secrete droplets of foul-smelling, noxious chemicals composed of quinones and volatile organic compounds. To a human standing upright, the odor might smell faintly medicinal, pungent, or like burning plastic. But to a small toad, mouse, or bird with sensitive senses, that chemical spray tastes intensely bitter and burns the eyes.

Some species take physical deception even further by practicing thanatosis, which is the theatrical art of playing dead. When startled by a sudden shadow or footstep, a harvestman pulls all eight legs tightly against its body. It collapses instantly into the leaf litter, resembling nothing more than a dried, brittle twig or a dead flake of tree bark. Predators that rely on detecting movement walk right past it without noticing a single sign of life. Once the danger passes and quiet returns, the creature slowly unfurls its legs and resumes its silent journey.

Have you ever looked under the eaves of an old wooden shed and gasped at a giant, throbbing ball of hair? That shocking sight is one of nature’s most mysterious behaviors: harvestman aggregation. During dry seasons or chilling autumn winds, hundreds, sometimes even thousands, of harvestmen gather into a single, dense cluster. They intertwine their long legs tightly, forming a pulsating, living carpet that covers tree trunks or cave entrances. If you approach them, the entire mass begins bobbing together in unison, resembling a shifting, breathing monster.

Why would solitary creatures gather in such enormous, quivering hordes? Scientists suspect this collective huddle serves multiple life-saving purposes. First, grouping together creates a humid microclimate, trapping precious body moisture and preventing their delicate joints from drying out in harsh winds. Second, the collective strength of thousands of scent glands creates an overwhelming chemical cloud that no predator dares approach. Finally, a sudden, synchronized shaking motion terrifies birds, making the mass appear far too intimidating to attack. There is undeniable power in numbers, even for the gentlest creatures.

Now, consider the sheer depth of time these creatures have witnessed on our planet. True spiders are ancient, but harvestmen walked the earth long before the very first spider spun a strand of silk. In prehistoric rocks found in Scotland, known as the Rhynie chert, paleontologists uncovered fossilized harvestmen preserved with astonishing clarity. Those fossils date back more than four hundred million years, into the early Devonian period. Think about what that number truly means in the grand timeline of life on earth.

Four hundred million years ago, the land was a silent, alien wilderness devoid of familiar life. There were no flowers, no birds singing in canopies, no mammals burrowing in soil, and not a single dinosaur roamed the earth. The continents were dominated by primitive, leafless plants growing alongside primeval swamps, and the oceans were ruled by armored fish. Yet creeping through those damp, ancient mosses were creatures with eight long legs, two periscope eyes, and segmented bodies. If you placed that prehistoric fossil next to a harvestman from your garden today, you would be shocked by how little has changed.

How did such a fragile, stilt-legged wanderer survive extinction events that pulverized mightier beasts? They lived through the Great Dying of the Permian extinction, which wiped out over ninety percent of all marine and terrestrial life. They walked under the heavy footsteps of towering sauropods, and they survived the fiery asteroid impact that ended the age of dinosaurs. When colossal reptiles vanished forever into fossil beds, these unassuming, venomless scavengers simply kept tapping the damp soil. Their quiet flexibility, modest dietary needs, and stealthy lifestyle carried them safely across epochal apocalypses.

In our human world, we often celebrate speed, predatory power, and dazzling weapons of conquest. We admire the venom of the viper, the speed of the cheetah, and the architectural perfection of the spider’s web. We mistakenly fear harvestmen because we project our anxieties onto their strange, elongated silhouettes. We assume anything with eight creeping legs must carry a deadly sting or hide dark intentions in the shadows. But the harvestman reminds us that survival does not always belong to the most ferocious predator in the forest.

Sometimes, survival belongs to the patient scavenger who tidies the earth without making a sound. It belongs to the creature that lets go of an attacked limb without regret, walking forward on whatever strength remains. It belongs to the humble traveler that navigates the darkness by gently touching its surroundings with respect and caution. The next time you find a daddy longlegs resting quietly in your garage or swaying gently upon a fence post, pause for a moment. Do not reach for a broom or recoil in needless fear.

Instead, gaze upon one of the oldest living lineages that walking earth has ever known. You are looking at an unbroken chain of life that endured continental drift, ice ages, and falling stars. That tiny, trembling speck on your wall is a masterclass in quiet persistence, carrying four hundred million years of calm wisdom into our noisy modern world.

=== 한국어 요약 (전체 번역이 아닌 요약본) ===
■ 영상 제목: The Great Dying: How Earth Was Wiped Clean (대멸종: 지구가 완전히 지워진 방법)
■ 한 줄 요약: 약 2억 5,200만 년 전 시베리아 트랩의 대규모 화산 분출로 인해 촉발된 페름기-트라이아스기 대멸종(Great Dying)의 원인, 전개 과정, 그리고 생태계 회복과 진화적 유산을 조명한다.
■ 전체 흐름 (대본 순서대로 8~15줄):
- 6,600만 년 전 공룡 멸종보다 훨씬 파괴적이었던 약 2억 5,200만 년 전 페름기-트라이아스기 대멸종(Great Dying)을 소개함.
- 해양 생물종 96%, 육상 척추동물 70% 이상이 사라졌으며 곤충까지 대규모로 멸종함.
- 대멸종 이전 초대륙 판게아(Pangaea)에는 단궁류(synapsids)와 울창한 숲이 번성하고 있었음.
- 현대 시베리아 지역에서 시베리아 트랩(Siberian Traps)의 마그마가 수십만 년 동안 대륙 크기의 면적으로 쏟아져 나옴.
- 마그마가 지하의 거대한 석탄, 석유, 탄산염 암반을 태우면서 막대한 양의 온실가스와 황을 대기로 분출시킴.
- 폭주 온실효과로 기온이 급상승(열대 지역 8~10°C 상승 추정)하고, 극심한 산성비가 쏟아져 숲과 토양을 파괴함.
- 해수 온도가 급상승하여 해양 순환이 멈추고 심해가 무산소 상태에 빠지면서 해양 생물이 집단 폐사함.
- 무산소 해양에서 번식한 혐기성 박테리아가 치명적인 황화수소를 대량 방출하여 바다가 보라색으로 물들고 유독 가스가 육지까지 확산됨.
- 화산성 할로겐과 메탄으로 오존층이 파괴되어 자외선-B(UV-B)가 쏟아졌고, 식물의 유전자가 손상되어 꽃가루 기형 및 먹이사슬 붕괴로 이어짐.
- 굴을 파고 저산소 환경을 견디는 능력을 지닌 돼지 크기의 단궁류 리스트로사우루스(Lystrosaurus)가 살아남아 초기 트라이아스기 육상을 독점함.
- 숲의 궤멸로 인해 수백만 년간 석탄층이 생기지 않는 '석탄 간극(coal gap)'과 해양 생존종이 소형화되는 '릴리푸트 효과(Lilliput effect)'가 발생함.
- 생물 다양성이 회복되는 데 500만~1,000만 년이 걸렸으며, 생태적 공백은 초기 공룡과 파충류(archosaurs)의 번성 및 포유류 조상 진화의 기틀이 됨.
■ 핵심 사실·숫자 (5~10개):
- 발생 시기: 약 2억 5,200만 년 전(roughly 252 million years ago).
- 해양 생물종 약 96%(around 96%) 멸종.
- 육상 척추동물 종 70% 이상(more than 70%) 멸종 및 유일한 곤충 대량 멸종 기록.
- 온실효과로 열대 지역 기온 8~10°C(8 to 10 degrees Celsius) 상승 추정.
- 시베리아 트랩(Siberian Traps)의 마그마 분출이 지하 석탄 및 탄화수소 층을 가열해 대량의 가스 유발.
- 오존층 파괴로 인한 자외선-B(UV-B) 노출로 화석 식물 꽃가루 기형 발생.
- 생존종 리스트로사우루스(Lystrosaurus) 화석이 초기 트라이아스기 육상 척추동물 화석의 대다수를 차지.
- 산림 부재로 인해 수백만 년 동안 석탄층이 형성되지 않은 석탄 간극(coal gap) 확인.
- 생태계의 다양성이 복원되는 데 약 500만~1,000만 년(roughly 5 to 10 million years) 소요.

- 숏츠 3: Meet the Ugly Creature That Inherited Earth (지구를 물려받은 못생긴 생명체를 만나보라) — 대멸종 당시 강력한 최상위 포식자들이 굶어 죽는 동안 볼품없는 외모의 리스트로사우루스(Lystrosaurus)가 살아남은 이유를 소개합니다. 돼지 크기의 이 생물은 땅속 굴을 파고 생활해 열과 산성비를 피할 수 있었고, 탁하고 산소가 희박한 지하 공기를 마시는 강력한 폐를 지녔습니다. 경쟁자와 포식자가 사라진 초기 트라이아스기 지표면으로 나와 육상 척추동물 화석의 약 90%를 차지하는 지구의 주인이 되었습니다.
- 숏츠 4: The Day the Sky Attacked Earth (하늘이 지구를 공격한 날) — 시베리아 트랩 화산 분출물이 대기로 유입되어 황산비가 내리고 오존층이 파괴되면서 하늘 자체가 무기가 되었던 과정을 설명합니다. 맹렬한 산성비가 숲과 토양을 녹여버렸고, 찢겨진 오존층 틈으로 쏟아진 자외선-B(UV-B) 방사선은 지표면 생물들을 태웠습니다. 당시 지층에서 발견된 식물 꽃가루 화석들이 유전자 손상으로 기형 상태였음이 밝혀져 먹이사슬 붕괴의 원인을 보여줍니다.
- 숏츠 5: Why Earth Stopped Making Coal for Millions of Years (지구가 수백만 년 동안 석탄 형성을 멈춘 이유) — 페름기 말 대멸종 이후 지질학적 지층에서 약 1,000만 년 동안 석탄층이 전혀 발견되지 않는 '석탄 간극(coal gap)' 현상을 다룹니다. 대멸종 이전에는 울창한 늪지 숲이 풍부한 석탄을 형성했으나, 급격한 온난화와 산성비로 전 대륙의 산림이 완전히 궤멸되었습니다. 대기와 토양이 회복되고 새로운 식물군이 거대한 숲을 재건하기까지 수백만 년이 걸렸음을 증명합니다.

=== 1부: TTS 입력용 전문 ===

Take a quiet breath and notice how effortless it feels. The air entering your lungs is cool, invisible, and life-giving. You probably never stop to question whether tomorrow morning will bring that same gentle air. Yet, our planet once held an atmosphere so toxic that drawing a single breath could burn your throat. There was a time when the sky turned a bruised yellow, the oceans ran purple with poison, and nearly every living thing simply stopped. We often think of our world as a permanent home, but Earth has a reset button. Have you ever wondered what happens when that button is pressed?

When people talk about the end of the world, they usually picture the dinosaurs. We envision a fiery rock tearing through the clouds, an impact that ended an iconic reign sixty-six million years ago. But that famous catastrophe was not the worst day in Earth's history. Long before the first Tyrannosaurus walked the land, another disaster struck with such fury that it almost killed the planet itself. Geologists call it the Permian-Triassic extinction event, or simply the Great Dying. It occurred roughly two hundred and fifty-two million years ago, and compared to it, the fall of the dinosaurs was practically gentle. 

The numbers from that ancient boundary are hard to grasp. In the oceans, scientists estimate that around ninety-six percent of all marine species vanished forever. On land, more than seventy percent of all vertebrate species disappeared into oblivion. It remains the only known event where even insects, the undisputed champions of survival, suffered a massive die-off. Imagine walking through a modern forest where nine out of every ten animals you see are marked for death. What could possibly unleash such absolute ruin without a cosmic collision? 

To understand the scale of the collapse, we have to look at what came before. Two hundred and fifty million years ago, the land was gathered into a supercontinent called Pangaea. Massive forests stretched across vast interior plains, while rivers teemed with strange, armored fish. Strange creatures roamed the shade, creatures known as synapsids. They were not dinosaurs, but early relatives of our own lineage, often called stem-mammals. There were saber-toothed predators the size of bears and gentle plant-eaters grazing in herds. It was a thriving, mature biosphere that had taken hundreds of millions of years to build. 

Then, deep beneath the crust in what is now modern Siberia, the earth tore itself open. This was not a single volcano exploding with ash, like Mount St. Helens. It was a volcanic province known today as the Siberian Traps, a tear in the planetary mantle that unleashed a nightmare. Magma did not merely erupt; it flooded outward like a rising black tide. Over hundreds of thousands of years, layer after layer of basalt poured out, eventually covering an area roughly the size of an entire continent. How do you stop a wound that bleeds molten rock for millennia?

The sheer volume of lava was catastrophic, but the real trigger was what lay buried beneath the Siberian surface. The magma welled up through massive underground basins of ancient coal, oil, and carbonate rock. As the white-hot rock intersected these carbon-rich layers, it cooked them like a colossal planetary furnace. Incalculable billions of tons of greenhouse gases and sulfur were vaporized into the upper atmosphere. It was essentially an ancient industrial revolution compressed into geological seconds, burning underground fuels without human hands. 

The consequences struck the sky first. Vast plumes of carbon dioxide trapped solar heat, triggering an extreme runaway greenhouse effect. Global temperatures soared, with tropical regions warming by what many scientists suspect was eight to ten degrees Celsius. If you think a heatwave on a summer afternoon is oppressive, imagine an entire planet heating up year after year without relief. The delicate balance that kept global weather patterns moving suddenly disintegrated. Monsoons stalled, weather systems turned chaotic, and continental interiors transformed into baking, cracked deserts. 

Along with the heat came corrosive moisture. Giant clouds of sulfur dioxide reacted with water vapor in the atmosphere, creating concentrated sulfuric acid. When the clouds finally released their moisture, it did not nourish the soil; it poisoned it. Acid rain washed across Pangaea, stripping the topsoil of its vital minerals and burning the leaves of ancient trees. Entire forests withered where they stood, leaving barren hillsides exposed to torrential flash floods. Can any ecosystem survive when its very foundation dissolves beneath its feet?

With the land burning and drowning in acid, you might wonder if life found sanctuary in the deep ocean. Sadly, the marine realm faced an even darker fate. Water holds less dissolved oxygen as it gets warmer. As ocean surfaces heated to temperatures resembling hot bathwater, the natural circulation between shallow and deep waters ground to a halt. The conveyor belts of the ocean simply froze. Without cold water sinking at the poles to carry oxygen downward, the depths began to suffocate.

Soon, vast oxygen-depleted zones spread across the global seabed like an invisible plague. Creatures that needed oxygen to survive, from delicate corals to ancient trilobites, were trapped between suffocating depths and scalding shallows. The seafloor, once a tapestry of vibrant, diverse life, became an endless graveyard. Shells dissolved in waters turning increasingly acidic from absorbed carbon, leaving marine animals without protection. The oceans were quietly dying, but the true horror was yet to rise from the dark.

In the suffocating, oxygen-free waters, a different kind of life began to flourish. Anaerobic, sulfate-reducing bacteria thrived in the stagnant deep, multiplying without limit. As these microbes fed on decaying organic matter, they released vast quantities of a deadly byproduct: hydrogen sulfide. Today, you might know the faint smell of this gas from a rotten egg or a volcanic vent. But back then, scientists suspect the gas built up in such immense concentrations that entire oceans turned a strange, murky shade of purple. 

Eventually, the ocean could no longer hold the poison. Gigantic bubbles of hydrogen sulfide broke the surface, releasing toxic clouds directly into the coastal air. For any land animals that had somehow survived the blistering heat and acid rain, this invisible vapor was a death sentence. Inhaling it paralyzed the respiratory system within moments. The poison did not stop at the shoreline, either; winds pushed the lethal haze deep inland, extinguishing life in valleys and plains alike. 

As if poison gas and suffocation were not enough, the atmosphere faced one final blow. The volcanic halogens, combined with chemical reactions from the methane and sulfur, began to erode Earth's protective ozone layer. The invisible shield that protects genetic material from cosmic violence simply vanished. Deadly ultraviolet-B radiation poured down through the shredded sky, scorching every exposed leaf and creature. 

Fossil records from this period show something chilling: microscopic pollen grains from fossilized plants are twisted and deformed. The radiation was actively breaking the molecular code of life, preventing plants from reproducing properly. Even if a seed managed to germinate in the toxic soil, its DNA was shredded by the relentless sunlight. The sky, which had sustained life for eons, had become a merciless weapon. 

Consider what this meant for the food web. When plants cannot produce healthy seeds, herbivores starve. When the plant-eaters perish, the great apex predators quickly follow. In the insect world, entire orders disappeared, a loss so severe it has never happened before or since. For millions of square kilometers, the only sound across Pangaea was the howling of dry winds over piles of dead wood. Earth was left with an eerie, sweeping silence. 

Yet, amid the ash and poison, a few extraordinary creatures clung to existence. How does anything survive a broken planet? The answers were humility and resilience. Life did not favor the mighty or the specialized. The champions of the Great Dying were creatures that could hide, animals that lived in deep burrows or could survive on decaying scraps. One famous survivor was a pig-sized synapsid called Lystrosaurus. With its barrel chest, short limbs, and beak-like mouth, it was uniquely built to dig underground and breathe stale, low-oxygen air.

When the worst of the crisis finally eased, Lystrosaurus found itself almost completely alone on land. With its competitors wiped out, this humble burrower multiplied until its fossils made up the vast majority of all land vertebrates in the early Triassic period. It is a stunning reminder of evolutionary fortune. The majestic, specialized beasts that ruled the late Permian were gone, while a stubborn, shovel-faced burrower inherited the entire world.

The recovery, however, was not quick. The biosphere was so shattered that it did not simply bounce back in a few centuries. In the rock layers following the extinction, geologists noticed a striking absence known as the coal gap. For millions of years after the Great Dying, no coal seams formed anywhere on Earth. Why? Because there were simply no dense, continuous forests left to produce the peat required for coal. The land remained a scrubby, fragile wasteland for immense spans of time.

In the oceans, life suffered a phenomenon known as the Lilliput effect. The few marine species that survived were drastically smaller than their ancestors. In an environment scarce in nutrients and choked of oxygen, being tiny was the only way to endure. Corals took nearly ten million years to rebuild complex reef structures. It took roughly five to ten million years for the diversity of life on Earth to resemble anything close to what had existed before the Siberian Traps ignited.

Yet, this brutal reset had an unexpected legacy. By wiping the slate nearly clean, the Great Dying destroyed the old hierarchies of life and opened vast evolutionary vacuums. In the warm, barren landscapes of the Triassic, new forms of life began to experiment with movement, metabolism, and structure. Among them were the archosaurs, nimble reptiles that would eventually split into crocodiles and the earliest dinosaurs. Without the Great Dying, the world of Jurassic giants might never have existed at all.

What about our own ancestors? The synapsids, once the dominant rulers of the Permian continents, were reduced to small, furry creatures living in the shadows of the rising reptiles. To survive in a world dominated by giants, they developed better insulation, sharper senses, and warmer blood. Those nocturnal survivors were the direct ancestors of every mammal walking the Earth today, including you. The fact that your heart beats warm blood right now is a direct consequence of a catastrophe that nearly ended everything.

When you look outside today, it is easy to view the world as a tranquil and permanent stage. We see green trees swaying in the breeze, seabirds dipping into the blue waves, and clouds drifting across a clear blue sky. But our planet is not a gentle museum; it is a dynamic, living machine driven by unimaginable internal forces. The rocks beneath our feet carry the memory of an Earth that turned hostile to its own children. 

The story of the Great Dying is both a warning and a testament. It warns us that the delicate chemical envelope supporting our civilization is surprisingly fragile, bound by atmospheric cycles that can unravel when pushed too far. But it is also the ultimate testament to life's stubborn, unyielding grip on reality. Even when ninety-six percent of the oceans perished, life refused to surrender. 

Tomorrow morning, when you step outside, pause for just a second. Take another deep breath of that cool, clear air. Feel the gentle warmth of the sunlight on your skin. Remember that you are standing on the surface of a survivor, breathing air that was once poison, carrying forward a lineage that walked through fire.

[숏츠 1] 제목: When Earth's Oceans Turned Deadly Purple

Take a close look at the ocean next time you visit the coast. You expect blue waves, white foam, and salty, fresh air. But two hundred and fifty-two million years ago, if you stood on a beach, the water before you would have been a murky, sickening purple. 

During the Great Dying, the greatest extinction event in planetary history, Earth's climate went into a violent greenhouse tailspin. Gigantic volcanic eruptions in Siberia flooded the atmosphere with carbon dioxide, causing global temperatures to spike. As the oceans grew warmer, they lost their ability to hold dissolved oxygen. Soon, the ocean's global conveyor belts halted. The deep sea became completely stagnant, dark, and suffocating. 

With all the oxygen gone, familiar creatures like trilobites, corals, and armored fish died by the billions. But in that suffocating grave, something horrifying thrived: anaerobic bacteria that fed on sulfur. As trillions of these microbes multiplied across the planet's seafloor, they produced staggering amounts of hydrogen sulfide gas. This byproduct gave the dead waters a distinct purple tint, but the color was the least of the danger. 

Eventually, the seas became so saturated with hydrogen sulfide that giant bubbles began rising to the surface, bursting into the atmosphere. The gas smells like rotten eggs, but in high concentrations, it is an instant neurotoxin. Coastal winds caught these toxic vapors and carried them hundreds of miles inland, suffocating any land animal that had managed to survive the extreme heat. The ocean had turned into an active chemical weapon, nearly extinguishing life on our planet forever. Ninety-six percent of all ocean life vanished before the waters could finally breathe again.
